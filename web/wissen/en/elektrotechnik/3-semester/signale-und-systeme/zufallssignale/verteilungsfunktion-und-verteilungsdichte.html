What this stage is about:

Averages do not tell everything. How often does a signal take which values? This is described by the distribution.

Definition 5.1 (Distribution)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{Prob}[s(t)\le x],}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{d} P_s(x)}{\mathrm{d} x},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^xp_s(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.}$$
(5.1)

⁠How to read it: The cumulative distribution function $P_s(x)$ is the fraction of the ensemble (for ergodic processes: the fraction of time) in which the signal lies below the threshold $x$. It rises monotonically from $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_s(-\infty)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_s(\infty)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. The probability density function $p_s(x)$ is never negative, has the area 1, and $p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x$ is the probability that the signal lies between $x$ and $x+\mathrm{d} x$. All first-order averages follow from it:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int x\,p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int x^2p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm E\{F[s]\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int F(x)\,p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x.}$$
(5.2)
Exampleuniform distribution

All values in an interval of width $a$ around $m_s$ are equally likely: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1a\operatorname{rect}\bigl(\frac{x-m_s}{a}\bigr)}$. Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1a\int_{m_s-a/2}^{m_s+a/2}x^2\mathrm{d} x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{a^2}{12}+m_s^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{a^2}{12}.}$$

The random numbers of the reference model ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$) have the variance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac1{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0833}$.

Characteristic curves. If a signal passes through a characteristic curve (transfer characteristic) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k(x)}$ (amplifier with saturation, rectifier, quantizer), its distribution changes. For a monotonic characteristic, the probability of every interval is preserved:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_g(y)\,|\mathrm{d} y|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p_s(x)\,|\mathrm{d} x|}$$
$$\Longrightarrow$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_g(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{p_s(x)}{|k'(x)|},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_g\bigl(k(x)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_s(x)\ \ (k\text{ increasing}).}$$
(5.3)

Where the characteristic is steep, the values are spread over a wide range, and the density decreases. With this rule, any distribution can be transformed into a desired one. Example: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2|x|-x^2)\operatorname{sgn}x}$ turns a triangular distribution on $[-1,1]$ into a uniform distribution (Exercise 4).

Quantization. A quantizer replaces every value by the nearest of $M$ levels. With uniform levels of width $\Delta$ and no overload, the error between $-\frac\Delta2$ and $\frac\Delta2$ is approximately uniformly distributed, hence has the variance $\frac{\Delta^2}{12}$. For a signal uniformly distributed over all $M$ levels (variance $\frac{(M\Delta)^2}{12}$), the signal-to-noise ratio is $M^2$, in decibels $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{20\lg M}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6\,\text{dB per bit}}$: 48 dB at 8 bits, 96 dB at 16 bits.

Exercise 3 ★ Uniform distribution

A signal is uniformly distributed between 1 V and 3 V. Compute $m_s$, $L_s$, $\sigma_s^2$ and the probability that it lies above 2.5 V.

Solution

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ V. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{a^2}{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13}$ V$^2$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13+4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.33}$ V$^2$. A quarter of the interval lies above 2.5 V, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{Prob}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{0.5}{2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25}$.

Exercise 4 ★★ Transforming distributions

A signal has the triangular density $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Lambda(x)}$ on $[-1,1]$.

  1. Find $P_s(x)$ for $0\le x\le1$.

  2. Use (5.3) to show that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2x-x^2)}$ for $x\ge0$ (continued as an odd function) produces a uniform distribution on $[-1,1]$. Hint: for the uniform distribution, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_g(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\frac y2}$.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\int_0^x(1-\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+x-\frac{x^2}{2}}$ for $0\le x\le1$.

(b) According to (5.3), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_g(k(x))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_s(x)}$ must hold, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac12+\frac{k(x)}{2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+x-\frac{x^2}{2}}$. This gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x-x^2}$. The characteristic is steep at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ (slope 2), where the triangular density is largest, and flat at the edge. In this way, the frequent values are pulled apart and the rare ones are pushed together. A simulation gives a flat histogram of height 0.5.

Exercise 4a ★★★ (Going further) The optimal quantizer

A quantizer with the thresholds $u_i$ and the level values $v_i$ (level $i$ applies for $u_i<x\le u_{i+1}$) has the mean squared error $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N_q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_i\int_{u_i}^{u_{i+1}}(v_i-x)^2p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x}$.

  1. Show by differentiating with respect to $v_i$: at the optimum, $v_i$ lies at the centroid of the density on its interval.

  2. Show by differentiating with respect to $u_i$: at the optimum, $u_i$ lies exactly halfway between $v_{i-1}$ and $v_i$.

  3. Why is the uniform quantizer optimal for a uniformly distributed signal?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial N_q}{\partial v_i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{u_i}^{u_{i+1}}2(v_i-x)p_s(x)\mathrm{d} x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{\int_{u_i}^{u_{i+1}}x\,p_s\,\mathrm{d} x}{\int_{u_i}^{u_{i+1}}p_s\,\mathrm{d} x}}$, the centroid.

(b) $u_i$ appears as the upper limit of interval $i-1$ and as the lower limit of interval $i$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial N_q}{\partial u_i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(v_{i-1}-u_i)^2p_s(u_i)-(v_i-u_i)^2p_s(u_i)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12(v_{i-1}+v_i)}$. A value is assigned to the level whose level value is closer.

(c) For a constant density, the centroid of each interval is its midpoint. Intervals of equal width with their midpoints as level values satisfy both conditions, with the step size $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a/M}$.
