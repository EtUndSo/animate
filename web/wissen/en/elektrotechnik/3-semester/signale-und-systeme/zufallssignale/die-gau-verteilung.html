What this stage is about:

Why is noise so often Gaussian, and how do you calculate with the Gaussian distribution?

If you convolve many densities with one another, the result approaches a Gaussian curve (central limit theorem, Chapter 3). Noise is usually the sum of very many small, independent contributions, such as the motions of many electrons. That is why it is Gaussian.

Theorem 7.1 (Gaussian distribution)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_s^2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(x-m_s)^2/(2\sigma_s^2)},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\frac12\operatorname{erf}\Bigl(\frac{x-m_s}{\sqrt{2\sigma_s^2}}\Bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\operatorname{erfc}\Bigl(\frac{m_s-x}{\sqrt{2\sigma_s^2}}\Bigr)}$$
(7.1)

with the error function $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{erf}(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^x\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^2}\mathrm{d}\xi}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{erfc}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\operatorname{erf}}$.

⁠How to read it: The mean value and the variance determine the Gaussian distribution completely. Within $\pm\sigma_s$ around the mean lie 68.3 % of the values, within $\pm2\sigma_s$ 95.4 %, within $\pm3\sigma_s$ 99.73 %. The integral of the Gaussian curve has no closed form, hence the function erf, which is tabulated or computed numerically. For large $x$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{erfc}(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}}{\sqrt\pi\,x}}$.

Calculation rules. The sum of two independent Gaussian variables is Gaussian with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m_1+m_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}$. A Gaussian process remains a Gaussian process after an LTI system, because the convolution integral is an (infinite) weighted sum. And for two jointly Gaussian variables, “uncorrelated” is the same as “independent”: if the normalized cross-covariance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\rho}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mu_{sg}/(\sigma_s\sigma_g)}$ is zero, the joint density factors into a product.

Going deeper, not needed for the rest: The two-dimensional Gaussian density

For zero-mean, jointly Gaussian $s,g$ with the correlation coefficient $\rho$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_{sg}(x,y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_s\sigma_g\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\sigma_g^2x^2+\sigma_s^2y^2-2\rho\sigma_s\sigma_gxy}{2\sigma_s^2\sigma_g^2(1-\rho^2)}\Bigr).}$$

It is obtained via the normalized sum and difference variables $\frac{s}{\sigma_s}\pm\frac{g}{\sigma_g}$: they are uncorrelated, have the variances $2(1\pm\rho)$ and, being Gaussian, are independent. Lines of equal density are ellipses; in the normalized coordinates $x/\sigma_s$, $y/\sigma_g$, their axes lie along these sums and differences. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\rho}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ they become circles (when $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma_g}$), and the density is the product of the individual densities.

Exercise 6 ★ Calculating with the Gaussian distribution

A noise voltage is Gaussian with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ mV.

  1. With what probability is it greater than 2 mV? (Hint: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{erfc}(1.414)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0455}$.)

  2. Two such independent voltages are added. What distribution does the sum have?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{Prob}[x>2\,\mathrm{mV}]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\frac{2}{\sqrt2}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\operatorname{erfc}(1.414)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.023}$, that is, 2.3 %.

(b) Gaussian with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ mV$^2$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.41}$ mV.
