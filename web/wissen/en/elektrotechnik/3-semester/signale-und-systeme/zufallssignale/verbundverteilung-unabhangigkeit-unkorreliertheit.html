Stage 6: Joint distribution, independence, uncorrelatedness

What this stage is about:

Two random variables at once: when are they related, and what follows for their sum?

The joint distribution of two variables is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_{sg}(x,y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{Prob}[s\le x\ \text{and}\ g\le y]}$, its density is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_{sg}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial^2P_{sg}}{\partial x\,\partial y}}$. From it follow the individual distributions ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_{sg}(x,\infty)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_s(x)}$) and the correlation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_{sg}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\iint xy\,p_{sg}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d} x\,\mathrm{d} y}$. For jointly stationary processes, everything depends only on the time shift $\tau$ between $s(t)$ and $g(t+\tau)$.

Definition 6.1 (Independent, uncorrelated)

$s$ and $g$ are called statistically independent if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_{sg}(x,y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p_s(x)\,p_g(y)}$. They are called uncorrelated if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mu_{sg}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_{sg}-m_sm_g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Independence implies uncorrelatedness: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_{sg}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int xp_s\mathrm{d} x\cdot\int yp_g\mathrm{d} y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m_sm_g}$. The converse does not hold: uncorrelated only means that there is no linear relationship (Exercise 5).

The sum of two independent variables. The sum $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f+g}$ takes the value $x$ if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x-u}$, for any $u$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{fg}(u,x-u)\,\mathrm{d} u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p_f(x)*p_g(x)\quad(\text{independent}).}$$
(6.1)

This is the convolution from Chapter 1, where it appeared in the example of the dice. Two uniformly distributed variables give a triangular density, three give an almost bell-shaped one (Fig. 2).

Exercise 5 ★★ Uncorrelated, but dependent

$s$ is uniformly distributed on $[-1,1]$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s^2}$. Show that $s$ and $g$ are uncorrelated, although $g$ is completely determined by $s$.

Solution

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm E\{sg\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm E\{s^3\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\int_{-1}^1x^3\mathrm{d} x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mu_{sg}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0-0\cdot m_g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: uncorrelated. Nevertheless, $s$ determines the value of $g$. The relationship is quadratic, and a correlation captures only linear relationships: for positive $s$, $g$ increases with $s$; for negative $s$, it decreases; on average, the two tendencies cancel.
