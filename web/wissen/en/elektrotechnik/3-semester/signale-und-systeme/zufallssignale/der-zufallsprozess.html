Stage 1: The random process

What this stage is about:

How do you describe a signal whose waveform you do not know? You imagine many signals of the same kind and average over them.

The idea. If you measure the noise of many identically built resistors at the same temperature, you get many different curves ${}^ks(t)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,2,\dots}$ (Fig. 1). Each one is called a sample function or realization. The imagined totality of all of them is called a random process $s(t)$. The values of all sample functions at a fixed instant $t_1$ form a random variable $s(t_1)$.

Definition 1.1 (Ensemble averages)
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm E\{s(t_1)\}&=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M{}^ks(t_1),\qquad \mathrm E\{F[s(t_1)]\}=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^MF\bigl[{}^ks(t_1)\bigr],\\ \varphi_{ss}(t_1,t_2)&=\mathrm E\{s(t_1)\,s(t_2)\}. \end{aligned}$$
(1.1)(1.2)

⁠How to read it: $\mathrm E\{\cdot\}$ is called the expected value or ensemble average: you average over all sample functions at the same instant, that is, vertically in Fig. 1. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F[x]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2}$ you obtain the mean square value. The autocorrelation function averages the product of the values at two instants. It tells you how strongly the value at $t_2$ is related to the value at $t_1$.
