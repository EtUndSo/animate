What this stage is about:

The most important model for disturbances: noise with the same power density at all frequencies.

Definition 9.1 (White noise)

A zero-mean process with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Phi_{ss}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{N_0}$ for all $f$ is called white noise. Its ACF is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{N_0\,\delta(\tau)}$.

⁠How to read it: Values at different times are uncorrelated, no matter how close together they are. The power $\int N_0\mathrm{d} f$ is infinite: white noise is a limiting model, like the Dirac impulse. What is actually measured is always filtered, “colored” noise:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Phi_{gg}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{N_0|H(f)|^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{N_0\,\varphi^E_{hh}(\tau),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{N_0\int|h(t)|^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{N_0\int|H(f)|^2\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(9.1)

Behind an ideal low-pass filter with the cutoff frequency $f_\mathrm g$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2N_0f_\mathrm g}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2N_0f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm g\tau)}$ (Fig. 3). The ACF is zero at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k/(2f_\mathrm g)}$, $k\neq0$: samples taken at the Nyquist rate are uncorrelated.

Thermal noise. The thermal motion of the electrons produces a noise voltage across every resistor $R$. In open circuit, measured in the frequency range $|f|\le f_\mathrm g$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm E\{u^2\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4kT_\mathrm{abs}Rf_\mathrm g,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2kT_\mathrm{abs}R,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_{\max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{kT_\mathrm{abs}f_\mathrm g,}$$
(9.2)

with the Boltzmann constant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.38\cdot10^{-23}}$ W s/K. At room temperature (290 K), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{kT}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\cdot10^{-21}}$ W/Hz, that is, 4 pW per GHz of bandwidth. $L_{\max}$ is the maximum power that can be extracted from the resistor, under power matching. In the reference model ($1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$, 20 kHz), the RMS value is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{\mathrm E\{u^2\}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.57}$ µ V. This order of magnitude limits how sensitive a microphone amplifier can be. The spectrum is flat up to about $10^{12}$ Hz.

LTspice gives every resistor its thermal noise. The .noise analysis computes the noise density V(onoise) at a node in V$/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. The measurement .meas noise urms INTEG V(onoise) integrates it in quadrature over the frequency range of the analysis, as in (8.3), and gives the RMS value. .temp 16.85 sets 290 K.

Exercise 8 ★ Noise of a resistor

Compute the RMS noise voltage of a $1\,\mathrm{M\Omega}$ resistor at 290 K and 20 kHz bandwidth. By what factor is it larger than for the $1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ resistor, and why by exactly this factor?

Solution

The RMS value grows with $\sqrt R$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{1000}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{31.6}$ times larger, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.566\cdot31.6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{17.9}$ µ V.

Exercise 9 ★★ $kT/C$ noise

The thermal noise of the resistor ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2kTR}$) passes through the RC circuit that the resistor forms with a capacitor $C$ ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{RC}\mathrm{e}^{-t/RC}\varepsilon(t)}$).

  1. Use (9.1) to show that the noise power across the capacitor is $kT/C$, independent of $R$.

  2. What is the RMS value for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ pF and 290 K? Why does a smaller resistor not help here?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int h^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{(RC)^2}\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-2t/RC}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2RC}}$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{N_0\cdot\frac{1}{2RC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2kTR}{2RC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{kT}{C}}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{4\cdot10^{-21}/10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{4\cdot10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{63}$ µ V. A smaller resistor does produce less noise per hertz, but the RC circuit becomes more broadband ($1/RC$ larger). The two effects cancel exactly. Only a larger capacitor reduces the noise.
