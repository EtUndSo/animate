What this stage is about:

The most important first-order averages and their intuitive meaning.

The expected value is linear: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm E\{\sum_ia_is_i\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_ia_i\mathrm E\{s_i\}}$, for arbitrary random variables. For stationary processes one defines:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm E\{s(t)\},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm E\{s^2(t)\},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{L_s-m_s^2.}$$
(3.1)

⁠How to read it: For an ergodic process, $m_s$ is the time average, that is, the DC component of the signal, and $L_s$ is the time-averaged square, the power (Chapter 6). The variance $\sigma_s^2$ is the power minus the power of the DC component, that is, the AC power. Its square root, the standard deviation $\sigma_s$, is the RMS value of the AC component. A multimeter set to AC shows $\sigma_s$; set to DC, it shows $m_s$.

Exercise 1 ★ Averages of a signal

An ergodic signal has a DC component of 2 V and an RMS value (everything together, “true RMS”) of 2.5 V. What are the normalized power, the variance and the standard deviation?

Solution

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.5^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.25}$ V$^2$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{L_s-m_s^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.25-4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.25}$ V$^2$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5}$ V. An AC meter shows 1.5 V.
