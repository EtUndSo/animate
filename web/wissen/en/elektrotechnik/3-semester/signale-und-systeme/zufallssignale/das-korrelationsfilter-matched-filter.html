Stage 10: The matched filter (correlation filter)

What this stage is about:

A known pulse $s(t)$ arrives corrupted by noise. With which filter does it become most clearly visible at the sampling instant $T$?

The approach. The receiver input carries $s(t)+n(t)$ with white noise $n$. A filter $h$ delivers $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g+n_e}$ with the signal component $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s*h}$ and the noise component $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n_e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n*h}$. Sampling takes place at $T$. The signal-to-noise ratio there is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{S_a}{N}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{g^2(T)}{\mathrm E\{n_e^2(T)\}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\bigl[\int h(\tau)s(T-\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\bigr]^2}{N_0\int|h(\tau)|^2\mathrm{d}\tau}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{E_s}{N_0}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\bigl[\int h(\tau)s(T-\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\bigr]^2}{\int|h|^2\mathrm{d}\tau\int|s(T-\tau)|^2\mathrm{d}\tau}}_{=|p|^2\le1}.}$$

The fraction on the right is the square of a correlation coefficient (Chapter 6), between $h(\tau)$ and $s(T-\tau)$. It is at most 1, and it equals 1 exactly when both have the same shape.

Theorem 10.1 (Matched filter)

Against white noise, the best achievable signal-to-noise ratio is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Bigl(\frac{S_a}{N}\Bigr)_{\max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{E_s}{N_0},}$$
$$\text{achieved with}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\,s(T-t),\quad H(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\,S^*(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi fT}.}$$
(10.1)

⁠How to read it: The best impulse response is the signal itself, time-reversed and shifted by $T$: the matched filter (correlation filter). Its output without noise is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\,\varphi^E_{ss}(t-T)}$, the shifted ACF of the signal, and it is sampled exactly at its maximum. Surprisingly, the result depends only on the energy of the signal, not on its shape. A short, high pulse is as good as a long, flat one with the same energy. For the filter to be causal, $T$ must be at least the duration of the signal. A correlator is equivalent: it multiplies the received signal by $s(t)$ and integrates over the duration of the signal.

Exercise 10 ★★ A rectangular pulse in noise

A rectangular pulse of height 1 V and duration 1 ms is received together with white noise of power spectral density $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-6}}$ V$^2$/Hz.

  1. What does the matched filter look like, and what $S_a/N$ (in dB) does it achieve?

  2. What does the output look like without noise, and when must it be sampled?

  3. The pulse is shortened to 0.25 ms, with the same height. What changes?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\,s(T-t)}$: again a rectangle of 1 ms, causal for $T\ge1$ ms. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{V^2}\cdot1\,\mathrm{ms}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-3}}$ V$^2$s, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_a/N}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-3}/10^{-6}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1000}$, that is, 30 dB.

(b) The ACF of the rectangle, a triangle of width 2 ms, shifted by $T$: it rises from $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{T-1}$ ms and reaches its maximum at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{T}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ ms, you sample exactly at the end of the pulse; at that moment the filter has “seen” all of it.

(c) $E_s$ drops to a quarter, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_a/N}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{250}$, that is, 24 dB, 6 dB worse. Only the energy counts. At the same height, a shorter pulse means less energy.
