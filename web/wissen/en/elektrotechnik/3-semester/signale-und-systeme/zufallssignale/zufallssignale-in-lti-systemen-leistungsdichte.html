What this stage is about:

What does an LTI system do to a random signal? Each sample function is convolved. Simple rules therefore hold for the averages.

Mean value. For each sample function, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{{}^kg}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{{}^ks*h}$. The expected value is linear and may be moved into the convolution integral:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm E\Bigl\{\int s(\tau)h(t-\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\Bigr\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int\mathrm E\{s(\tau)\}\,h(t-\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m_s\int h\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m_s\,H(0).}$$
(8.1)

The mean value is transmitted like a DC component.

Theorem 8.1 (Wiener–Lee for stationary processes)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)*\varphi^E_{hh}(\tau),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Phi_{gg}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Phi_{ss}(f)\,|H(f)|^2,}$$
(8.2)

with the impulse ACF (deterministic ACF) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi^E_{hh}(\tau)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int h(\vartheta)h(\vartheta+\tau)\mathrm{d}\vartheta}$ of the impulse response and the power spectral density $\Phi_{ss}(f)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;\varphi_{ss}(\tau)$. The power is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi_{ss}(f)\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(8.3)
Derivation: Wiener–Lee

Write out both convolution integrals, with different variables:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)g(t+\tau)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\iint s(t-\alpha)\,s(t+\tau-\beta)\,h(\alpha)h(\beta)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\beta.}$$

Move the expected value into the double integral. For a stationary process, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm E\{s(t-\alpha)s(t+\tau-\beta)\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau-\beta+\alpha)}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vartheta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\beta-\alpha}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int\varphi_{ss}(\tau-\vartheta)\underbrace{\int h(\alpha)h(\alpha+\vartheta)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha}_{\varphi^E_{hh}(\vartheta)}\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_{ss}*\varphi^E_{hh}.}$$

In the frequency domain, $\varphi^E_{hh}$ becomes $|H|^2$ (Chapter 6).

⁠How to read it: $\Phi_{ss}(f)$ indicates how the power is distributed over the frequencies. It is real, never negative and, for real signals, even. An LTI system multiplies the power at each frequency by $|H(f)|^2$; its phase plays no role. This is the same rule as for energy signals in Chapter 6, now for processes. (Note: strictly speaking, $\Phi_{ss}$ is defined as the limit $\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\mathrm E\{|S_T(f)|^2\}$ over short-time spectra of segments of duration $T$. That this is the same as the transform of the ACF is the actual statement of the Wiener–Khinchin theorem.)

LTspiceNoise with a mean value through low-pass and high-pass

⁠Draw: a behavioural source bv at net s with V=1+white(1e3*time). white(x) returns random values between $-0.5$ and $0.5$, a new one at every integer $x$, joined smoothly, so here every millisecond. Connected to it in parallel, an RC low-pass (res with 1Meg to tp, cap with 1u to ground) and a CR high-pass (cap with 1u to hp, res with 1Meg to ground).

⁠Directive: .tran 0 200 0 0.1m.

⁠Reading: Zoom to 20 s to 200 s, i.e. to the time after the transient. A click on a trace name while holding the Ctrl key shows the mean (Average) and the RMS value over the visible range.

⁠What you see: The input has the mean 1 V. Compare the means of V(tp) and V(hp) with your result from Exercise 7. The fluctuations are much smaller at the low-pass: plot V(s)-1 and V(tp)-1, their RMS values are about 0.25 and 0.01. The low-pass passes only roughly the part of the broad power density below $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/(2\pi RC)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.16}$ Hz, and by (8.3) the power is the area under the power density.

Exercise 7 ★ Mean value through a filter

Noise with the mean value 1 V passes through an RC low-pass filter and through a CR high-pass filter. What mean values do the outputs have, and why?

Solution

RC low-pass filter: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, mean value 1 V. CR high-pass filter: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, mean value 0. The capacitor does not pass direct current, so the DC component is blocked.
