What this is about

The noise of a resistor, speech, a data stream: such signals cannot be predicted. Their exact waveform is unknown, but their average properties are known: how large they typically are, how fast they change, which frequencies they contain. This article shows how to describe such random signals, what an LTI system does to them and how to detect a known signal in noise most reliably.

The answer in three sentences (by the end of the article they should feel obvious)

  1. A random signal is described by averages: mean value, power, variance, autocorrelation function and probability density function. For ergodic processes, they may be determined from a single, long signal.

  2. The Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function is the power spectral density. An LTI system multiplies it by $|H(f)|^2$, exactly as for energy signals (Part 6).

  3. Against white noise, the matched filter $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(T-t)}$ is optimal. It achieves the signal-to-noise ratio $E_s/N_0$, regardless of the signal shape.

One principle behind all the results. You replace the unknown signal by its averages, and averages are linear:

Stage 1: The random process

What this stage is about:

How do you describe a signal whose waveform you do not know? You imagine many signals of the same kind and average over them.

The idea. If you measure the noise of many identically built resistors at the same temperature, you get many different curves ${}^ks(t)$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1,2,\dots}$ (Fig. 1). Each one is called a sample function or realization. The imagined totality of all of them is called a random process $s(t)$. The values of all sample functions at a fixed instant $t_1$ form a random variable $s(t_1)$.

Definition 1.1 (Ensemble averages)
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm E\{s(t_1)\}&=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M{}^ks(t_1),\qquad \mathrm E\{F[s(t_1)]\}=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^MF\bigl[{}^ks(t_1)\bigr],\\ \varphi_{ss}(t_1,t_2)&=\mathrm E\{s(t_1)\,s(t_2)\}. \end{aligned}$$
(1.1)(1.2)

⁠How to read it: $\mathrm E\{\cdot\}$ is called the expected value or ensemble average: you average over all sample functions at the same instant, that is, vertically in Fig. 1. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{F[x]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{x^2}$ you obtain the mean square value. The autocorrelation function averages the product of the values at two instants. It tells you how strongly the value at $t_2$ is related to the value at $t_1$.

Stage 2: Stationarity and ergodicity

What this stage is about:

You never have an infinite ensemble, usually only a single signal. When is that enough?

A process is called stationary if none of its averages change when all observation times are shifted by the same time $t_0$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{F[s(t_1)]\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{F[s(t_1+t_0)]\},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{F[s(t_1),s(t_2)]\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{F[s(t_1+t_0),s(t_2+t_0)]\}.}$$
(2.1)

Then the mean value $\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{s(t)\}}$ is a constant, and the ACF depends only on the difference $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{t_2-t_1}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{s(t)s(t+\tau)\}}$. A stationary process is called ergodic if, in addition, every time average of a sample function equals the corresponding ensemble average:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{F[s(t)]\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\overline{F[{}^ks(t)]}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}F\bigl[{}^ks(t)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d} t\quad\text{for every }k.}$$
(2.2)

⁠How to read it: Stationary means that the statistics do not change with time. Ergodic means that a single sample function, observed for a long time, shows in the course of time everything that the whole ensemble shows. The noise of a resistor at constant temperature is ergodic. Only for this reason can noise be characterized with a single measuring instrument.

QuestionIs every stationary process also ergodic?

No. Example: each sample function is a constant whose value is drawn at random once, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{{}^ks(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{c_k}$. The process is stationary, because nothing depends on $t$. But the time average of a sample function is $c_k$, which is different for every sample function and is not the ensemble average. A single sample function never “sees” the other values.

Stage 3: Mean value, power, variance

What this stage is about:

The most important first-order averages and their intuitive meaning.

The expected value is linear: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{\sum_ia_is_i\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ia_i\mathrm E\{s_i\}}$, for arbitrary random variables. For stationary processes one defines:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{s(t)\},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{s^2(t)\},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{L_s-m_s^2.}$$
(3.1)

⁠How to read it: For an ergodic process, $m_s$ is the time average, that is, the DC component of the signal, and $L_s$ is the time-averaged square, the power (Part 6). The variance $\sigma_s^2$ is the power minus the power of the DC component, that is, the AC power. Its square root, the standard deviation $\sigma_s$, is the RMS value of the AC component. A multimeter set to AC shows $\sigma_s$; set to DC, it shows $m_s$.

Exercise 1 ★ Averages of a signal

An ergodic signal has a DC component of 2 V and an RMS value (everything together, “true RMS”) of 2.5 V. What are the normalized power, the variance and the standard deviation?

Solution

$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2.5^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{6.25}$ V$^2$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{L_s-m_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{6.25-4}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2.25}$ V$^2$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1.5}$ V. An AC meter shows 1.5 V.

Stage 4: Autocorrelation, covariance and cross-correlation

What this stage is about:

How fast does a random signal change? The ACF tells you: how similar the signal still is to itself after the time $\tau$.

Theorem 4.1 (Properties of the ACF of stationary processes)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{L_s,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{ss}(-\tau),}$$
$$|\varphi_{ss}(\tau)|\le\varphi_{ss}(0).$$
(4.1)

For ergodic processes, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\overline{s(t)s(t+\tau)}}$, that is, the ACF of a power signal from Part 6.

Derivation: Maximum at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$

$\mathrm E\{[s(t)\pm s(t+\tau)]^2\}\ge0$, because the expected value of a square is never negative. Multiplying out and using linearity: $\mathrm E\{s^2(t)\}+\mathrm E\{s^2(t+\tau)\}\pm2\mathrm E\{s(t)s(t+\tau)\}\ge0$, so $2\varphi_{ss}(0)\pm2\varphi_{ss}(\tau)\ge0$. Evenness: substitute $t\to t-\tau$ in the definition.

Covariance. If you subtract the mean value first, you obtain the autocovariance function

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mu_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{[s(t)-m_s][s(t+\tau)-m_s]\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)-m_s^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mu_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sigma_s^2.}$$
(4.2)

If the process contains no periodic components, values that lie far apart become independent: $\varphi_{ss}(\tau)\to m_s^2$ and $\mu_{ss}(\tau)\to0$ for $\tau\to\infty$.

Cross-correlation and sums. For two processes, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{uv}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{u(t)v(t+\tau)\}}$ with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{vu}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{uv}(-\tau)}$, and the cross-covariance is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mu_{uv}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{uv}(\tau)-m_um_v}$. For the sum $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{u+v}$, multiplying out gives

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{uu}+\varphi_{uv}+\varphi_{vu}+\varphi_{vv},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{L_u+L_v+2\varphi_{uv}(0),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sigma_u^2+\sigma_v^2+2\mu_{uv}(0).}$$
(4.3)

⁠In short: Powers add only if the signals are uncorrelated. Otherwise the cross term is added. Two identical signals give four times the power, two opposite ones give zero power.

Exercise 2 ★★ Two noise sources

Two zero-mean noise voltages with the RMS values 3 mV and 4 mV are added. What is the RMS value of the sum if they are (a) uncorrelated, (b) identical in shape ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{uv}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sigma_u\sigma_v}$), (c) identical in shape but of opposite sign ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{uv}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\sigma_u\sigma_v}$)?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sqrt{9+16}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{5}$ mV. (b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{9+16+2\cdot12}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{49}$, so 7 mV: the amplitudes add. (c) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{9+16-24}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, so 1 mV: the signals almost cancel each other.

Stage 5: Distribution function and probability density

What this stage is about:

Averages do not tell everything. How often does a signal take which values? This is described by the distribution.

Definition 5.1 (Distribution)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{Prob}[s(t)\le x],}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\mathrm{d} P_s(x)}{\mathrm{d} x},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^xp_s(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.}$$
(5.1)

⁠How to read it: The cumulative distribution function $P_s(x)$ is the fraction of the ensemble (for ergodic processes: the fraction of time) in which the signal lies below the threshold $x$. It rises monotonically from $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_s(-\infty)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ to $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_s(\infty)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$. The probability density function $p_s(x)$ is never negative, has the area 1, and $p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x$ is the probability that the signal lies between $x$ and $x+\mathrm{d} x$. All first-order averages follow from it:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int x\,p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int x^2p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{F[s]\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int F(x)\,p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x.}$$
(5.2)

Example: uniform distribution. All values in an interval of width $a$ around $m_s$ are equally likely: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1a\operatorname{rect}\bigl(\frac{x-m_s}{a}\bigr)}$. Then

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1a\int_{m_s-a/2}^{m_s+a/2}x^2\mathrm{d} x}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{a^2}{12}+m_s^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{a^2}{12}.}$$

The random numbers of the reference model ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{a}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$) have the variance $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac1{12}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.0833}$.

Characteristic curves. If a signal passes through a characteristic curve (transfer characteristic) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{y}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{k(x)}$ (amplifier with saturation, rectifier, quantizer), its distribution changes. For a monotonic characteristic, the probability of every interval is preserved:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_g(y)\,|\mathrm{d} y|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{p_s(x)\,|\mathrm{d} x|}$$
$$\Longrightarrow$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_g(y)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{p_s(x)}{|k'(x)|},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_g\bigl(k(x)\bigr)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{P_s(x)\ \ (k\text{ increasing}).}$$
(5.3)

Where the characteristic is steep, the values are spread over a wide range, and the density decreases. With this rule, any distribution can be transformed into a desired one. Example: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(2|x|-x^2)\operatorname{sgn}x}$ turns a triangular distribution on $[-1,1]$ into a uniform distribution (Exercise 4).

Quantization. A quantizer replaces every value by the nearest of $M$ levels. With uniform levels of width $\Delta$ and no overload, the error between $-\frac\Delta2$ and $\frac\Delta2$ is approximately uniformly distributed, hence has the variance $\frac{\Delta^2}{12}$. For a signal uniformly distributed over all $M$ levels (variance $\frac{(M\Delta)^2}{12}$), the signal-to-noise ratio is $M^2$, in decibels $\textcolor{#e5383b}{20\lg M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{6\,\text{dB per bit}}$: 48 dB at 8 bits, 96 dB at 16 bits.

Exercise 3 ★ Uniform distribution

A signal is uniformly distributed between 1 V and 3 V. Compute $m_s$, $L_s$, $\sigma_s^2$ and the probability that it lies above 2.5 V.

Solution

$\textcolor{#e5383b}{a}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$ V, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$ V. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{a^2}{12}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac13}$ V$^2$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac13+4}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4.33}$ V$^2$. A quarter of the interval lies above 2.5 V, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{Prob}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{0.5}{2}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.25}$.

Exercise 4 ★★ Transforming distributions

A signal has the triangular density $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Lambda(x)}$ on $[-1,1]$.

  1. Find $P_s(x)$ for $0\le x\le1$.

  2. Use (5.3) to show that $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(2x-x^2)}$ for $x\ge0$ (continued as an odd function) produces a uniform distribution on $[-1,1]$. Hint: for the uniform distribution, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_g(y)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12+\frac y2}$.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12+\int_0^x(1-\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12+x-\frac{x^2}{2}}$ for $0\le x\le1$.

(b) According to (5.3), $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_g(k(x))}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{P_s(x)}$ must hold, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac12+\frac{k(x)}{2}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12+x-\frac{x^2}{2}}$. This gives $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2x-x^2}$. The characteristic is steep at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{x}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ (slope 2), where the triangular density is largest, and flat at the edge. In this way, the frequent values are pulled apart and the rare ones are pushed together. A simulation gives a flat histogram of height 0.5.

Exercise 4a ★★★ (Going further) The optimal quantizer

A quantizer with the thresholds $u_i$ and the level values $v_i$ (level $i$ applies for $u_i<x\le u_{i+1}$) has the mean squared error $\textcolor{#e5383b}{N_q}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_i\int_{u_i}^{u_{i+1}}(v_i-x)^2p_s(x)\,\mathrm{d} x}$.

  1. Show by differentiating with respect to $v_i$: at the optimum, $v_i$ lies at the centroid of the density on its interval.

  2. Show by differentiating with respect to $u_i$: at the optimum, $u_i$ lies exactly halfway between $v_{i-1}$ and $v_i$.

  3. Why is the uniform quantizer optimal for a uniformly distributed signal?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{\partial N_q}{\partial v_i}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{u_i}^{u_{i+1}}2(v_i-x)p_s(x)\mathrm{d} x}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{v_i}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\dfrac{\int_{u_i}^{u_{i+1}}x\,p_s\,\mathrm{d} x}{\int_{u_i}^{u_{i+1}}p_s\,\mathrm{d} x}}$, the centroid.

(b) $u_i$ appears as the upper limit of interval $i-1$ and as the lower limit of interval $i$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{\partial N_q}{\partial u_i}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(v_{i-1}-u_i)^2p_s(u_i)-(v_i-u_i)^2p_s(u_i)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{u_i}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12(v_{i-1}+v_i)}$. A value is assigned to the level whose level value is closer.

(c) For a constant density, the centroid of each interval is its midpoint. Intervals of equal width with their midpoints as level values satisfy both conditions, with the step size $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Delta}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{a/M}$.

Stage 6: Joint distribution, independence, uncorrelatedness

What this stage is about:

Two random variables at once: when are they related, and what follows for their sum?

The joint distribution of two variables is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_{sg}(x,y)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{Prob}[s\le x\ \text{and}\ g\le y]}$, its density is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\partial^2P_{sg}}{\partial x\,\partial y}}$. From it follow the individual distributions ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_{sg}(x,\infty)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{P_s(x)}$) and the correlation $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\iint xy\,p_{sg}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d} x\,\mathrm{d} y}$. For jointly stationary processes, everything depends only on the time shift $\tau$ between $s(t)$ and $g(t+\tau)$.

Definition 6.1 (Independent, uncorrelated)

$s$ and $g$ are called statistically independent if $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_{sg}(x,y)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{p_s(x)\,p_g(y)}$. They are called uncorrelated if $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mu_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{sg}-m_sm_g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$.

Independence implies uncorrelatedness: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int xp_s\mathrm{d} x\cdot\int yp_g\mathrm{d} y}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{m_sm_g}$. The converse does not hold: uncorrelated only means that there is no linear relationship (Exercise 5).

The sum of two independent variables. The sum $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{f+g}$ takes the value $x$ if $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{u}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{x-u}$, for any $u$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{fg}(u,x-u)\,\mathrm{d} u}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{p_f(x)*p_g(x)\quad(\text{independent}).}$$
(6.1)

This is the convolution from Part 1, where it appeared in the example of the dice. Two uniformly distributed variables give a triangular density, three give an almost bell-shaped one (Fig. 2).

Exercise 5 ★★ Uncorrelated, but dependent

$s$ is uniformly distributed on $[-1,1]$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s^2}$. Show that $s$ and $g$ are uncorrelated, although $g$ is completely determined by $s$.

Solution

$\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{sg\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{s^3\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12\int_{-1}^1x^3\mathrm{d} x}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mu_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0-0\cdot m_g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$: uncorrelated. Nevertheless, $s$ determines the value of $g$. The relationship is quadratic, and a correlation captures only linear relationships: for positive $s$, $g$ increases with $s$; for negative $s$, it decreases; on average, the two tendencies cancel.

Stage 7: The Gaussian distribution

What this stage is about:

Why is noise so often Gaussian, and how do you calculate with the Gaussian distribution?

If you convolve many densities with one another, the result approaches a Gaussian curve (central limit theorem, Part 3). Noise is usually the sum of very many small, independent contributions, such as the motions of many electrons. That is why it is Gaussian.

Theorem 7.1 (Gaussian distribution)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_s^2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(x-m_s)^2/(2\sigma_s^2)},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12+\frac12\operatorname{erf}\Bigl(\frac{x-m_s}{\sqrt{2\sigma_s^2}}\Bigr)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12\operatorname{erfc}\Bigl(\frac{m_s-x}{\sqrt{2\sigma_s^2}}\Bigr)}$$
(7.1)

with the error function $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{erf}(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^x\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^2}\mathrm{d}\xi}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{erfc}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1-\operatorname{erf}}$.

⁠How to read it: The mean value and the variance determine the Gaussian distribution completely. Within $\pm\sigma_s$ around the mean lie 68.3 % of the values, within $\pm2\sigma_s$ 95.4 %, within $\pm3\sigma_s$ 99.73 %. The integral of the Gaussian curve has no closed form, hence the function erf, which is tabulated or computed numerically. For large $x$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{erfc}(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}}{\sqrt\pi\,x}}$.

Calculation rules. The sum of two independent Gaussian variables is Gaussian with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{m_1+m_2}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}$. A Gaussian process remains a Gaussian process after an LTI system, because the convolution integral is an (infinite) weighted sum. And for two jointly Gaussian variables, “uncorrelated” is the same as “independent”: if the normalized cross-covariance $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\rho}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mu_{sg}/(\sigma_s\sigma_g)}$ is zero, the joint density factors into a product.

Going deeper, not needed for the rest: The two-dimensional Gaussian density

For zero-mean, jointly Gaussian $s,g$ with the correlation coefficient $\rho$,

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_{sg}(x,y)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_s\sigma_g\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\sigma_g^2x^2+\sigma_s^2y^2-2\rho\sigma_s\sigma_gxy}{2\sigma_s^2\sigma_g^2(1-\rho^2)}\Bigr).}$$

It is obtained via the normalized sum and difference variables $\frac{s}{\sigma_s}\pm\frac{g}{\sigma_g}$: they are uncorrelated, have the variances $2(1\pm\rho)$ and, being Gaussian, are independent. Lines of equal density are ellipses; in the normalized coordinates $x/\sigma_s$, $y/\sigma_g$, their axes lie along these sums and differences. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\rho}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ they become circles (when $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sigma_g}$), and the density is the product of the individual densities.

Stage 8: Random signals in LTI systems, power spectrum

What this stage is about:

What does an LTI system do to a random signal? Each sample function is convolved. Simple rules therefore hold for the averages.

Mean value. For each sample function, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{{}^kg}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{{}^ks*h}$. The expected value is linear and may be moved into the convolution integral:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\Bigl\{\int s(\tau)h(t-\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\Bigr\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int\mathrm E\{s(\tau)\}\,h(t-\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{m_s\int h\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{m_s\,H(0).}$$
(8.1)

The mean value is transmitted like a DC component.

Theorem 8.1 (Wiener–Lee for stationary processes)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)*\varphi^E_{hh}(\tau),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi_{gg}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Phi_{ss}(f)\,|H(f)|^2,}$$
(8.2)

with the impulse ACF (deterministic ACF) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{hh}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int h(\vartheta)h(\vartheta+\tau)\mathrm{d}\vartheta}$ of the impulse response and the power spectral density $\Phi_{ss}(f)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;\varphi_{ss}(\tau)$. The power is

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi_{ss}(f)\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(8.3)
Derivation: Wiener–Lee

Write out both convolution integrals, with different variables:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(t)g(t+\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\iint s(t-\alpha)\,s(t+\tau-\beta)\,h(\alpha)h(\beta)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\beta.}$$

Move the expected value into the double integral. For a stationary process, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{s(t-\alpha)s(t+\tau-\beta)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau-\beta+\alpha)}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\vartheta}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\beta-\alpha}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int\varphi_{ss}(\tau-\vartheta)\underbrace{\int h(\alpha)h(\alpha+\vartheta)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha}_{\varphi^E_{hh}(\vartheta)}\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{ss}*\varphi^E_{hh}.}$$

In the frequency domain, $\varphi^E_{hh}$ becomes $|H|^2$ (Part 6).

⁠How to read it: $\Phi_{ss}(f)$ indicates how the power is distributed over the frequencies. It is real, never negative and, for real signals, even. An LTI system multiplies the power at each frequency by $|H(f)|^2$; its phase plays no role. This is the same rule as for energy signals in Part 6, now for processes. (Note: strictly speaking, $\Phi_{ss}$ is defined as the limit $\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\mathrm E\{|S_T(f)|^2\}$ over short-time spectra of segments of duration $T$. That this is the same as the transform of the ACF is the actual statement of the Wiener–Khinchin theorem.)

Stage 9: White noise and thermal noise

What this stage is about:

The most important model for disturbances: noise with the same power density at all frequencies.

Definition 9.1 (White noise)

A zero-mean process with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi_{ss}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N_0}$ for all $f$ is called white noise. Its ACF is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N_0\,\delta(\tau)}$.

⁠How to read it: Values at different times are uncorrelated, no matter how close together they are. The power $\int N_0\mathrm{d} f$ is infinite: white noise is a limiting model, like the Dirac impulse. What is actually measured is always filtered, “colored” noise:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi_{gg}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N_0|H(f)|^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N_0\,\varphi^E_{hh}(\tau),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N_0\int|h(t)|^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N_0\int|H(f)|^2\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(9.1)

Behind an ideal low-pass filter with the cutoff frequency $f_\mathrm g$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2N_0f_\mathrm g}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2N_0f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm g\tau)}$ (Fig. 3). The ACF is zero at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{k/(2f_\mathrm g)}$, $k\neq0$: samples taken at the Nyquist rate are uncorrelated.

Thermal noise. The thermal motion of the electrons produces a noise voltage across every resistor $R$. In open circuit, measured in the frequency range $|f|\le f_\mathrm g$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{u^2\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4kT_\mathrm{abs}Rf_\mathrm g,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{N_0}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2kT_\mathrm{abs}R,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_{\max}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{kT_\mathrm{abs}f_\mathrm g,}$$
(9.2)

with the Boltzmann constant $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1.38\cdot10^{-23}}$ W s/K. At room temperature (290 K), $\textcolor{#e5383b}{kT}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4\cdot10^{-21}}$ W/Hz, that is, 4 pW per GHz of bandwidth. $L_{\max}$ is the maximum power that can be extracted from the resistor, under power matching. In the reference model ($1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$, 20 kHz), the RMS value is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sqrt{\mathrm E\{u^2\}}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.57}$ µ V. This order of magnitude limits how sensitive a microphone amplifier can be. The spectrum is flat up to about $10^{12}$ Hz.

Stage 10: The matched filter (correlation filter)

What this stage is about:

A known pulse $s(t)$ arrives corrupted by noise. With which filter does it become most clearly visible at the sampling instant $T$?

The approach. The receiver input carries $s(t)+n(t)$ with white noise $n$. A filter $h$ delivers $\textcolor{#e5383b}{y}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{g+n_e}$ with the signal component $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s*h}$ and the noise component $\textcolor{#e5383b}{n_e}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{n*h}$. Sampling takes place at $T$. The signal-to-noise ratio there is

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{S_a}{N}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{g^2(T)}{\mathrm E\{n_e^2(T)\}}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\bigl[\int h(\tau)s(T-\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\bigr]^2}{N_0\int|h(\tau)|^2\mathrm{d}\tau}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{E_s}{N_0}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\bigl[\int h(\tau)s(T-\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\bigr]^2}{\int|h|^2\mathrm{d}\tau\int|s(T-\tau)|^2\mathrm{d}\tau}}_{=|p|^2\le1}.}$$

The fraction on the right is the square of a correlation coefficient (Part 6), between $h(\tau)$ and $s(T-\tau)$. It is at most 1, and it equals 1 exactly when both have the same shape.

Theorem 10.1 (Matched filter)

Against white noise, the best achievable signal-to-noise ratio is

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Bigl(\frac{S_a}{N}\Bigr)_{\max}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{E_s}{N_0},}$$
$$\text{achieved with}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{a\,s(T-t),\quad H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{a\,S^*(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi fT}.}$$
(10.1)

⁠How to read it: The best impulse response is the signal itself, time-reversed and shifted by $T$: the matched filter (correlation filter). Its output without noise is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{a\,\varphi^E_{ss}(t-T)}$, the shifted ACF of the signal, and it is sampled exactly at its maximum. Surprisingly, the result depends only on the energy of the signal, not on its shape. A short, high pulse is as good as a long, flat one with the same energy. For the filter to be causal, $T$ must be at least the duration of the signal. A correlator is equivalent: it multiplies the received signal by $s(t)$ and integrates over the duration of the signal.

Stage 11: Error probability in binary reception

What this stage is about:

The receiver has to decide: was the pulse sent or not? How often is it wrong?

The idea. The sample at the output of the matched filter is Gaussian with the variance $N$ (noise) and the mean value $\sqrt{S_a}$ if the pulse was sent, or 0 if not. The receiver compares it with a threshold $C$. An error occurs when the noise pushes the value across the threshold. The two error probabilities are the areas of the two Gaussian curves beyond the threshold:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_{e1}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{Prob}[y_1\le C]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{S_a}-C}{\sqrt{2N}}\Bigr),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_{e0}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{Prob}[y_0>C]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\Bigl(\frac{C}{\sqrt{2N}}\Bigr).}$$
(11.1)

If both cases are sent equally often, the average error probability is $\frac12(P_{e1}+P_{e0})$. It is smallest when the threshold lies exactly in the middle, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{C}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sqrt{S_a}/2}$. With the matched filter ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_a/N}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{E_s/N_0}$):

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_{e,\min}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt{\frac{E_s}{8N_0}}.}$$
(11.2)

⁠How to read it: The error probability depends only on $E_s/N_0$ and falls extremely fast: at 10 dB $5.7\cdot10^{-2}$, at 16 dB $8\cdot10^{-4}$, at 20 dB $2.9\cdot10^{-7}$ (Fig. 4). A few decibels more signal energy turn an unusable transmission into an almost error-free one.

Exercise 11 ★★ Error probability

  1. Compute $P_{e,\min}$ for $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s/N_0}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{10}$ dB. Hint: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{erfc}(1.118)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.114}$.

  2. Why does the optimal threshold lie in the middle between 0 and $\sqrt{S_a}$? What happens to $P_{e1}$ and $P_{e0}$ if you shift it upward?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s/N_0}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{10}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sqrt{10/8}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1.118}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_{e,\min}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12\cdot0.114}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.057}$.

(b) The two Gaussian curves have the same width. The sum of the two error areas is smallest where the curves intersect, and by symmetry that is the middle. If you shift the threshold upward, $P_{e0}$ becomes smaller, but $P_{e1}$ grows more, because the threshold moves into the dense part of the upper curve.

Stage 12: Discrete-time random signals

What this stage is about:

Everything also holds for sequences of random numbers, with sums instead of integrals.

If a process band-limited to $f_\mathrm g$ is sampled with $T\le\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}$, the samples form a discrete-time process $s(n)$. Distributions, expected values and correlations are defined as before; the time averages are sums, for example $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{2M+1}\sum_{n=-M}^{M}s(n)s(n+m)}$. Through an LSI system with $h(n)$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{m_s\sum_nh(n),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{ss}(m)*\varphi^E_{hh}(m),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi_{gg,\mathrm a}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Phi_{ss,\mathrm a}(f)\,|H_\mathrm a(f)|^2,}$$
(12.1)

with the periodic power spectral density $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi_{ss,\mathrm a}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_m\varphi_{ss}(m)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi fm}}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\Phi_{ss,\mathrm a}\mathrm{d} f}$. Discrete-time white noise has $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi_{ss,\mathrm a}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N\delta(m)}$: successive numbers are uncorrelated. Unlike in the continuous-time case, its power is finite, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N}$.

Example 1: averaging two values. Uniformly distributed white noise ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1{12}}$) passes through $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12[\delta(n)+\delta(n-1)]}$, that is, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12[s(n)+s(n-1)]}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{hh}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac14\delta(m+1)+\frac12\delta(m)+\frac14\delta(m-1)}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac{\sigma_s^2}{4}\delta(m+1)+\tfrac{\sigma_s^2}{2}\delta(m)+\tfrac{\sigma_s^2}{4}\delta(m-1).}$$

The variance is halved to $\frac{1}{24}$, and neighboring output values are now correlated (they share an input value). The output values have a triangular distribution, because each is half the sum of two independent, uniformly distributed numbers. A simulation with 200,000 numbers gives the variance 0.0415 instead of $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac1{24}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.0417}$.

Example 2: recursive filter (AR(1) process). White noise of variance $\sigma_s^2$ passes through $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{b^n\varepsilon(n)}$ (Part 4). With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{hh}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_kb^kb^{k+|m|}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{b^{|m|}}{1-b^2}}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\sigma_s^2}{1-b^2}\,b^{|m|},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi_{gg,\mathrm a}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\sigma_s^2}{1-2b\cos(2\pi f)+b^2}.}$$
(12.2)

For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{b}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.6}$, the variance is $1.56$ times the input variance, and neighboring values have the correlation coefficient $0.6$. The closer $b$ is to 1, the stronger the low frequencies (Fig. 5).

Exercise 12 ★★ AR(1) process

Uniformly distributed white noise ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1{12}}$) passes through $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n)+0.6\,g(n-1)}$.

  1. Compute the output variance and $\varphi_{gg}(1)/\varphi_{gg}(0)$.

  2. Derive $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{hh}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{b^{|m|}}{1-b^2}}$ using the geometric series.

  3. Is the output still uniformly distributed? What distribution do you roughly expect, and why?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_g^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1/12}{1-0.36}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.130}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}(1)/\varphi_{gg}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{b}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.6}$. The simulation gives 0.130 and 0.600.

(b) For $m\ge0$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sum_{k\ge0}b^kb^{k+m}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{b^m\sum_kb^{2k}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{b^m}{1-b^2}}$. The impulse ACF is even, so $b^{|m|}$ holds.

(c) No longer uniformly distributed: each output value is a weighted sum of many independent input values, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_kb^ks(n-k)}$. By the central limit theorem, this is approximately Gaussian. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{b}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.6}$, only a few terms contribute noticeably, so the approximation is rough, but the distribution is already clearly bell-shaped.

Key points

  • Ensemble average $\mathrm E\{\cdot\}$; stationary: independent of shifts; ergodic: time average $=$ ensemble average.

  • $m_s$ (DC component), $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm E\{s^2\}}$ (power), $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{L_s-m_s^2}$ (AC power).

  • ACF: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{L_s}$, even, maximum at 0; covariance $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mu}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi-m^2}$; sum: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma_s^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sigma_u^2+\sigma_v^2+2\mu_{uv}(0)}$.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_s(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{Prob}[s\le x]}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{P_s'}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int xp_s}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int x^2p_s}$; uniform distribution $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{a^2/12}$.

  • Characteristic curve: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_g(y)|\mathrm{d} y|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{p_s(x)|\mathrm{d} x|}$; quantization error $\Delta^2/12$, 6 dB per bit.

  • Independent $\Rightarrow$ uncorrelated; sum of independent variables: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{p_f*p_g}$; Gaussian: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\mathrm{e}^{-(x-m)^2/2\sigma^2}$.

  • LTI: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{m_g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{m_sH(0)}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi_{gg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi_{ss}*\varphi^E_{hh}}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi_{gg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Phi_{ss}|H|^2}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int\Phi\,\mathrm{d} f}$.

  • White: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Phi}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N_0}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{N_0\delta}$; thermal $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm E\{u^2\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4kTRf_\mathrm g}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{kT}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4}$ pW/GHz.

  • Matched filter: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{a\,s(T-t)}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{(S_a/N)_{\max}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{E_s/N_0}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{P_{e,\min}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt{E_s/(8N_0)}}$.
