Stage 3: Power, convergence and LTI systems

What this stage is about:

How much does each harmonic contribute to the signal, how well does a truncated series approximate the signal, and what does an LTI system do with a periodic input?

Theorem 3.1 (Parseval for Fourier series)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1T\int_0^T|s_p(t)|^2\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}|S_p(k)|^2.}$$
(3.1)

⁠How to read it: The average power of the signal is the sum of the powers of the individual harmonics. Cross terms drop out because different harmonics are orthogonal. For the square wave, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$. The DC component contributes $0.25$, that is, 50 %. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm1}$ it is 90.5 %, with $|k|\le3$ 95.0 %, with $|k|\le9$ 98.0 %.

Derivation: Parseval

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|s_p|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_ps_p^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_k\sum_lS_p(k)S_p^*(l)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi(k-l)Ft}}$. Averaged over one period, by (2.4) only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{l}$ remains: $\sum_k|S_p(k)|^2$.

Truncated series and the Gibbs phenomenon. If you take only the harmonics up to $|k|\le N$, the partial sum is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_{p,N}(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{k=-N}^{N}S_p(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}}$. The power of the error $s_p-s_{p,N}$ is the sum of the omitted $|S_p(k)|^2$ and tends to zero as $N\to\infty$ if the signal has finite power. At a jump, however, an overshoot remains (Fig. 2): it becomes narrower as $N$ grows, but its height tends to about 9 % of the jump height (10.0 % for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, 9.1 % for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$, 9.0 % for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{27}$, limit 8.95 %). This is the Gibbs phenomenon. It does not contradict convergence: the area under the overshoot, and with it the error power, still tends to zero.

Periodic input to an LTI system. Each harmonic is an eigenfunction and is multiplied by $H(kF)$. The output therefore has the same period, and its coefficients are

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_p(k)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S_p(k)\,H(kF).}$$
(3.2)

In the reference model, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(kF)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/(1+\mathrm{j} k)}$. The fundamental is transmitted with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|H|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.707}$ and $-45^\circ$, the third harmonic with $0.316$ and $-71.6^\circ$, the fifth with $0.196$ and $-78.7^\circ$. At the output, the third harmonic is only 15 % as large as the first, instead of 33 % at the input. The RC circuit rounds off the edges (Fig. 3). The output swings between 0.041 and 0.959.

QuestionIsn't a calculation over infinitely many harmonics much more laborious than the differential equation?

For a single square wave through an RC circuit, yes: the charging and discharging curves can be written down directly. In return, the series shows why the output looks the way it does. The edges consist of the high harmonics, and it is exactly these that the RC circuit attenuates. Moreover, (3.2) works for every LTI system, even for those whose differential equation is not known at all, only their measured frequency response.

Exercise 3 ★★ Power and higher harmonics

  1. Use (3.1) to confirm that the harmonics $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,\pm1}$ of the square wave carry 90.5 % of the power.

  2. Compute $|G_p(1)|$, $|G_p(3)|$, $|G_p(5)|$ for the RC output. What fraction of the AC power of the output (without $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$) is in the fundamental if you take only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm1,\pm3,\pm5}$ into account?

  3. Why does the output already look “more sinusoidal” than the input?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|S_p(0)|^2+2|S_p(1)|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25+2/\pi^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25+0.2026}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.4526}$, which is 90.5 % of $0.5$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|G_p(k)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|S_p(k)|/\sqrt{1+k^2}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|G_p(1)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.2251}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|G_p(3)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0336}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|G_p(5)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0125}$. AC power from these harmonics: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2(0.0507+0.0011+0.0002)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.1039}$. The share of the fundamental is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.0507/0.0519}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{97.5\,\%}$. At the input, for the same harmonics, it would be only 86.9 %.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|H(kF)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/\sqrt{1+k^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/k}$: the higher harmonics are attenuated more strongly the higher they are. What remains is mainly the fundamental, and a signal that consists almost entirely of the fundamental is almost a sinusoid.
