What this is about

Part 2 showed that every signal is a superposition of building blocks $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$, and that an LTI system merely multiplies each building block by a number. The Fourier description uses only the building blocks on the imaginary axis, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}$: pure oscillations $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}$ that neither grow nor decay. This has a big advantage. An oscillation has a frequency that you can hear, measure and filter. The spectrum $S(f)$ tells you with which amplitude and phase each frequency is contained in the signal.

The answer in three sentences (by the end of the article they should feel obvious)

  1. A periodic signal is a sum of oscillations at multiples of the fundamental frequency (Fourier series); a non-repeating signal is an integral over all frequencies (Fourier integral).

  2. Every oscillation $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}$ is an eigenfunction: an LTI system multiplies it by $H(f)$, and that is why convolution becomes the product $\textcolor{#e5383b}{G(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S(f)\,H(f)}$.

  3. All the rules of calculation (shift, stretching, modulation, symmetry) follow from what the respective operation does to a single oscillation.

Stage 1: Oscillations as eigenfunctions

What this stage is about:

Of the building blocks $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$, only the pure oscillations are used from now on. This stage shows what an LTI system does to them.

For purely imaginary $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}$, the building block becomes an oscillation with constant amplitude:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\cos(2\pi ft)+\mathrm{j}\sin(2\pi ft).}$$

Like every building block, it is an eigenfunction of every LTI system (delaying is multiplying by $\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f\tau}$):

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}*h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$
(1.1)

$H(f)$ is called the frequency response or transfer function. Its magnitude says how strongly the oscillation of frequency $f$ is amplified or attenuated, and its phase says how far it is shifted. Two systems in cascade multiply one after the other: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H_1(f)H_2(f)}$. Where the Laplace transform $H(p)$ exists on the imaginary axis, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H(p)|_{p=\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}$.

⁠In short: The frequency response is the number by which an LTI system multiplies a pure oscillation.

Stage 2: Periodic signals, the Fourier series

What this stage is about:

A periodic signal repeats itself after $T$. It can be built from oscillations, but only from very specific ones. This stage shows which ones, and how to find their weights.

The idea. An oscillation $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}$ repeats itself after $T$ if and only if $fT$ is an integer. So a signal with the period $T$ contains only the frequencies $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{kF}$ with the fundamental frequency $\textcolor{#e5383b}{F}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/T}$, the harmonics:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_p(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}S_p(k)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}.}$$
(2.1)

This is the Fourier series (synthesis). $S_p(k)$ is the complex weight of the $k$th harmonic.

Real signals. For a real signal, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_p^*}$. Conjugating (2.1) turns $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}$ into the oscillation with $-k$. Comparing the coefficients gives

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(-k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_p^*(k).}$$

Each pair $k,-k$ then gives $2\operatorname{Re}\{S_p(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}\}$, a real cosine oscillation. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{|S_p(k)|\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\varphi_p(k)}}$:

$$\begin{aligned} s_p(t)&=S_p(0)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|S_p(k)|\cos\bigl(2\pi kFt+\varphi_p(k)\bigr)\\ &=S_p(0)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl[\operatorname{Re}\{S_p(k)\}\cos(2\pi kFt)-\operatorname{Im}\{S_p(k)\}\sin(2\pi kFt)\Bigr]. \end{aligned}$$
(2.2)(2.3)

The phase $\varphi_p(k)$ is found as for any complex number, with $\arctan$ and the quadrant correction ($+\pi$ for a negative real part). $S_p(0)$ is the DC component, the mean value of the signal.

Finding the weights: orthogonality. How do you extract a single $S_p(n)$ from the sum? Multiply by $\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi nFt}$ and average over one period. In the process, all other harmonics vanish:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac1T\int_0^T\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi(k-n)Ft}\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\begin{cases}1&k=n\\0&k\neq n\end{cases}.}$$
(2.4)

For $k\neq n$, the phasor $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi(k-n)Ft}$ makes exactly $|k-n|$ full turns during the period, and its mean value is zero. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{n}$ it stands still. Oscillations of different frequencies are therefore called orthogonal. What remains is:

Theorem 2.1 (Fourier series)

A signal with the period $\textcolor{#e5383b}{T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/F}$ and finite power has the representation (2.1) with

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\int_{t_1}^{t_1+T}s_p(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}\,\mathrm{d} t,}$$
(2.5)

where $t_1$ is arbitrary (analysis).

⁠How to read it: $S_p(k)$ is the mean value of the signal after it has been “counter-rotated” with the $k$th harmonic. If the harmonic is contained in the signal, the phasor no longer rotates at this point, and its share remains after averaging. $S_p(0)$ is simply the mean value.

Derivation: Analysis formula

Multiply (2.1) by $\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi nFt}$, integrate over one period, interchange sum and integral:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_0^Ts_p(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi nFt}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_kS_p(k)\int_0^T\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi(k-n)Ft}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_p(n)\,T.}$$

For $k\neq n$ the integral is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi(k-n)FT}-1}{\mathrm{j}2\pi(k-n)F}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi(k-n)}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$. Since the integrand has the period $T$, you may integrate over any interval of length $T$.

Example: a finite series. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_p(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1+2\cos(2\pi Ft)+\sin(4\pi Ft)}$. With Euler's formulas you can read off the coefficients without integrating:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{2\cos(2\pi Ft)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sin(4\pi Ft)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2\mathrm{j}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}4\pi Ft}-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{j}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}4\pi Ft},}$$

so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(\pm1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\tfrac{\mathrm{j}}{2}}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(-2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac{\mathrm{j}}{2}}$, all others zero. Check: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(-2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_p^*(2)}$.

Example: the square wave. For the reference model (1 for $|t|\le T_1/2$ with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{T_1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T/2}$):

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{T_1}{T},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\int_{-T_1/2}^{T_1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\sin(\pi kT_1/T)}{\pi k}\quad(k\neq0).}$$
(2.6)

With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{T_1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T/2}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(\pm1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/\pi}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.318}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(\pm2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(\pm3)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-0.106}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(\pm5)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.064}$. The even harmonics are missing because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sin(\pi k/2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ for even $k$. All coefficients are real because the signal is even (Stage 5). Figure 1 shows them for two duty cycles: the narrower the pulse, the more slowly the coefficients decay.

QuestionWhy are there no frequencies between the harmonics in a periodic signal?

Because every oscillation in the sum must itself have the period $T$; otherwise the sum would not be periodic. An oscillation with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1.5F}$ has made only one and a half turns after $T$ and is in a different position than at the start. No weight, however cleverly chosen, can compensate for this, because different frequencies are orthogonal and cannot cancel each other.

Stage 3: Power, convergence and LTI systems

What this stage is about:

How much does each harmonic contribute to the signal, how well does a truncated series approximate the signal, and what does an LTI system do with a periodic input?

Theorem 3.1 (Parseval for Fourier series)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\int_0^T|s_p(t)|^2\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}|S_p(k)|^2.}$$
(3.1)

⁠How to read it: The average power of the signal is the sum of the powers of the individual harmonics. Cross terms drop out because different harmonics are orthogonal. For the square wave, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$. The DC component contributes $0.25$, that is, 50 %. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\pm1}$ it is 90.5 %, with $|k|\le3$ 95.0 %, with $|k|\le9$ 98.0 %.

Derivation: Parseval

$\textcolor{#e5383b}{|s_p|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_ps_p^*}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_k\sum_lS_p(k)S_p^*(l)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi(k-l)Ft}}$. Averaged over one period, by (2.4) only $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{l}$ remains: $\sum_k|S_p(k)|^2$.

Truncated series and the Gibbs phenomenon. If you take only the harmonics up to $|k|\le N$, the partial sum is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_{p,N}(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{k=-N}^{N}S_p(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}}$. The power of the error $s_p-s_{p,N}$ is the sum of the omitted $|S_p(k)|^2$ and tends to zero as $N\to\infty$ if the signal has finite power. At a jump, however, an overshoot remains (Fig. 2): it becomes narrower as $N$ grows, but its height tends to about 9 % of the jump height (10.0 % for $\textcolor{#e5383b}{N}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$, 9.1 % for $\textcolor{#e5383b}{N}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{9}$, 9.0 % for $\textcolor{#e5383b}{N}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{27}$, limit 8.95 %). This is the Gibbs phenomenon. It does not contradict convergence: the area under the overshoot, and with it the error power, still tends to zero.

Periodic input to an LTI system. Each harmonic is an eigenfunction and is multiplied by $H(kF)$. The output therefore has the same period, and its coefficients are

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{G_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_p(k)\,H(kF).}$$
(3.2)

In the reference model, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(kF)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/(1+\mathrm{j} k)}$. The fundamental is transmitted with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|H|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.707}$ and $-45^\circ$, the third harmonic with $0.316$ and $-71.6^\circ$, the fifth with $0.196$ and $-78.7^\circ$. At the output, the third harmonic is only 15 % as large as the first, instead of 33 % at the input. The RC circuit rounds off the edges (Fig. 3). The output swings between 0.041 and 0.959.

QuestionIsn't a calculation over infinitely many harmonics much more laborious than the differential equation?

For a single square wave through an RC circuit, yes: the charging and discharging curves can be written down directly. In return, the series shows why the output looks the way it does. The edges consist of the high harmonics, and it is exactly these that the RC circuit attenuates. Moreover, (3.2) works for every LTI system, even for those whose differential equation is not known at all, only their measured frequency response.

What fraction of the AC power of the output (without $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$) is in the fundamental if you take only $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\pm1,\pm3,\pm5}$ into account?

  3. Why does the output already look “more sinusoidal” than the input?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|S_p(0)|^2+2|S_p(1)|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.25+2/\pi^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.25+0.2026}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.4526}$, which is 90.5 % of $0.5$.

(b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|G_p(k)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{|S_p(k)|/\sqrt{1+k^2}}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|G_p(1)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.2251}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|G_p(3)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.0336}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|G_p(5)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.0125}$. AC power from these harmonics: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{2(0.0507+0.0011+0.0002)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.1039}$. The share of the fundamental is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{0.0507/0.0519}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{97.5\,\%}$. At the input, for the same harmonics, it would be only 86.9 %.

(c) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|H(kF)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/\sqrt{1+k^2}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/k}$: the higher harmonics are attenuated more strongly the higher they are. What remains is mainly the fundamental, and a signal that consists almost entirely of the fundamental is almost a sinusoid.

Stage 4: From periodic to non-repeating signals

What this stage is about:

A single pulse is not periodic. But it can be regarded as a periodic signal with an infinitely long period. In the process, the sum becomes an integral.

The idea. Continue a pulse $s(t)$ of duration less than $T$ periodically. The coefficients of the series are then values of a fixed function:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{F\cdot S(kF),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t.}$$

If you double the period, the line spacing $F$ is halved, and the lines sample the same envelope $S(f)$ twice as densely (Fig. 4). In the limit $T\to\infty$, the spacing $F$ becomes the differential $\mathrm{d} f$, and the sum $\sum_kS(kF)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}F$ becomes the integral.

Theorem 4.1 (Fourier transform)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} t,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(4.1)

Shorthand: $s(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f)$ or $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathcal{F}\{s(t)\}}$.

⁠How to read it: $S(f)$ is a spectral density: weight per frequency interval. If $s$ is a voltage in V, $S$ has the unit V/Hz. The inverse transform builds the signal from all oscillations, each with the weight $S(f)\,\mathrm{d} f$. A sufficient condition for existence is $\int|s(t)|\mathrm{d} t<\infty$. The Fourier transform is the Laplace transform on the imaginary axis, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathrm{j}2\pi f)}$, provided the axis lies in the region of convergence (Part 2).

⁠Convolution becomes a product. As in Part 2: $s(t)*h(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f)\,H(f)$. From $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s*\delta}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\delta\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S(f)}$, so $\delta(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;1$: the Dirac impulse contains all frequencies with equal weight.

Example: the exponential pulse. The impulse response of the RC circuit, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1{RC}\varepsilon(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t/RC}}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1{RC}\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-(1/RC+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)t}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{j}2\pi f\,RC}.}$$

This is the same frequency response as from AC analysis (Part 1). So the impulse response contains all the information about the frequency behavior.

QuestionWhat is the difference between $S_p(k)$ and $S(f)$, apart from the fact that one is discrete?

The unit. $S_p(k)$ is an amplitude (V): the $k$th harmonic really has this strength. $S(f)$ is a density (V/Hz): in a non-repeating signal no single frequency has a finite amplitude; only a frequency band $\Delta f$ carries approximately $S(f)\,\Delta f$. That is why $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{F\cdot S(kF)}$: each line represents a band of width $F$.

Solution

Stage 5: Symmetries in the signal and in the spectrum

What this stage is about:

Whether a signal is real, even or odd can be seen immediately from its spectrum. This stage derives the rules.

Even and odd parts. A complex function is called even if $\textcolor{#e5383b}{x(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{x^*(-t)}$, and odd if $\textcolor{#e5383b}{x(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-x^*(-t)}$. (For real functions these are the usual mirror symmetry and point symmetry about the origin.) Every signal splits uniquely into the two parts:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_g(t)+s_u(t),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_g(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\bigl[s(t)+s^*(-t)\bigr],}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_u(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\bigl[s(t)-s^*(-t)\bigr].}$$
(5.1)
Theorem 5.1 (Symmetries)
$$s_g(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\operatorname{Re}\{S(f)\},$$
$$s_u(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\mathrm{j}\operatorname{Im}\{S(f)\}.$$
(5.2)

For real signals, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(-f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S^*(f)}$: the real part and the magnitude $|S|$ are even in $f$, the imaginary part and the phase are odd. A real, even signal has a real, even spectrum. A real, odd signal has a purely imaginary, odd spectrum.

Derivation: Symmetries

First: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathcal{F}\{s^*(-t)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int s^*(-t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-t}$ this is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int s^*(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\bigl(\int s(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\bigr)^*}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S^*(f)}$. Hence

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathcal{F}\{s_g\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\bigl[S(f)+S^*(f)\bigr]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\{S(f)\},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathcal{F}\{s_u\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\bigl[S(f)-S^*(f)\bigr]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{j}\operatorname{Im}\{S(f)\}.}$$

For real $s$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathcal{F}\{s^*(t)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathcal{F}\{s(t)\}}$. In general, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathcal{F}\{s^*(t)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int s^*\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\bigl(\int s\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t\bigr)^*}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S^*(-f)}$. So $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S^*(-f)}$.

⁠In short: For a real signal, the negative half of the spectrum is the conjugate mirror image of the positive half. It contains no new information.

Example. The exponential pulse $\varepsilon(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t}$ (real) has the even part $\frac12\mathrm{e}^{-|t|}$ and the odd part $\frac12\operatorname{sgn}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-|t|}$. Its spectrum $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}$ has the even real part $\frac{1}{1+(2\pi f)^2}$, the transform of $\frac12\mathrm{e}^{-|t|}$, and the odd imaginary part $\frac{-2\pi f}{1+(2\pi f)^2}$ (Fig. 5).

Stage 6: The rules of calculation (theorems)

What this stage is about:

Instead of evaluating every integral anew, you use rules: what happens to the spectrum when the signal is shifted, compressed, differentiated or modulated? Each rule follows from what the operation does to a single oscillation.

Theorem 6.1 (Theorems of the Fourier transform)
$$\begin{aligned} &\text{Superposition:} && \textstyle\sum_ia_is_i(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\sum_ia_iS_i(f)\\ &\text{Conjugation:} && s^*(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S^*(-f)\\ &\text{Similarity (scaling):} && s(bt)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{1}{|b|}S\Bigl(\frac fb\Bigr),\quad b\neq0;\qquad s(-t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(-f)\\ &\text{Shift:} && s(t-t_0)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft_0},\qquad\delta(t-t_0)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft_0}\\ &\text{Derivative:} && \frac{\mathrm{d}^n}{\mathrm{d} t^n}s(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;(\mathrm{j}2\pi f)^nS(f)\\ &\text{Symmetry (duality):} && S(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;s(-f)\\ &\text{Modulation:} && \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}s(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f-F)\\ &\text{Frequency-domain derivative:} && -\mathrm{j}2\pi t\,s(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} f}S(f)\\ &\text{Convolution, multiplication:} && s_1*s_2\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_1S_2,\qquad s_1s_2\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_1*S_2\\ &\text{Areas:} && \int s(t)\mathrm{d} t=S(0),\qquad\int S(f)\mathrm{d} f=s(0)\\ &\text{Parseval:} && \int|s(t)|^2\mathrm{d} t=\int|S(f)|^2\mathrm{d} f \end{aligned}$$
(6.1)(6.2)(6.3)(6.4)(6.5)(6.6)(6.7)(6.8)(6.9)(6.10)(6.11)

⁠How to read the most important ones:

  • ⁠Similarity: A signal compressed in time ($|b|>1$) has a wider spectrum. Short signals need many frequencies. Duration and bandwidth are inversely related.

  • ⁠Shift: A delay does not change the magnitude, only the phase, and it does so linearly in $f$: every oscillation is shifted by the same time, which at a higher frequency is a larger angle.

  • ⁠Derivative: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}}$. Differentiation amplifies high frequencies.

  • ⁠Symmetry: Forward and inverse transform differ only in the sign of the exponent. Every transform pair therefore yields a second one with the roles swapped.

  • ⁠Modulation: Multiplication by $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}$ shifts the whole spectrum by $F$. This is how every radio transmitter works.

  • ⁠Multiplication: The spectrum of the product of two signals is the convolution of the spectra, the counterpart of the convolution theorem.

  • ⁠Parseval: The energy is the same in both domains. $|S(f)|^2$ is the energy density over frequency.

Derivation: Similarity, shift, derivative, symmetry, modulation, multiplication, Parseval

⁠Similarity ($b>0$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{bt}$): $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int s(bt)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1b\int s(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi(f/b)\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1bS(f/b)}$. For $b<0$ the limits are reversed, hence $1/|b|$.

⁠Shift ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{t-t_0}$): $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int s(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f(\tau+t_0)}\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft_0}S(f)}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\delta}$, the pair for $\delta(t-t_0)$ follows.

⁠Derivative: Differentiate the inverse transform with respect to $t$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\int S\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int\mathrm{j}2\pi f\,S\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} f}$.

⁠Symmetry: In the inverse transform, replace $t$ by $-t$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(-t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int S(f)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} f}$. Now swap the names of the variables $t$ and $f$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(-f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int S(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}$, which is the forward transform of $S(t)$.

⁠Modulation: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi(f-F)t}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S(f-F)}$. The derivative in the frequency domain follows in the same way by differentiating the forward transform with respect to $f$.

⁠Multiplication: Convolution theorem for the signals $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t)$, that is $s_1*s_2\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_1S_2$, then apply symmetry to this pair: $S_1(t)S_2(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;s_1(-f)*s_2(-f)$. Renaming gives (6.9).

⁠Areas: Set $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ in the forward transform and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ in the inverse transform.

⁠Parseval: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int|s|^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int s\,s^*\mathrm{d} t}$ is the product $s(t)s^*(t)$, integrated, so by multiplication, conjugation and the area theorem it equals $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\bigl[S(f)*S^*(-f)\bigr]_{f=0}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int S(\nu)S^*(\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu}$.

QuestionWhy doesn't a delay change the magnitude of the spectrum?

Because a delay neither attenuates nor amplifies any oscillation; it only shifts each one. Every oscillation is shifted by the same time $t_0$, which at the frequency $f$ is the angle $2\pi ft_0$. That is why the phase is a straight line in $f$ with the slope $-2\pi t_0$. A system with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|H|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ and linear phase delays a signal without changing its shape.

Three transform pairs come up all the time. With the theorems, they follow from a single calculation.

Stage 8: Singular signals and line spectra

What this stage is about:

Constants, oscillations, the step and periodic signals are not absolutely integrable. With Dirac impulses in the spectrum they can still be transformed. In this way the Fourier series and the Fourier integral become one.

Theorem 8.1 (Singular pairs)
$$\begin{aligned} \delta(t)&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;1, & c&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;c\,\delta(f),\\ \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\delta(f-F), & \cos(2\pi Ft)&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\tfrac12\delta(f+F)+\tfrac12\delta(f-F),\\ & & \sin(2\pi Ft)&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\tfrac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\delta(f+F)-\tfrac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\delta(f-F),\\ \textstyle\sum_kS_p(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\textstyle\sum_kS_p(k)\,\delta(f-kF),\\ \textstyle\sum_n\delta(t-nT)&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{1}{|T|}\textstyle\sum_k\delta\Bigl(f-\frac kT\Bigr),\\ \varepsilon(t)&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\tfrac12\delta(f)+\frac{1}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}, & \operatorname{sgn}(t)&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{1}{\mathrm{j}\pi f},\\ \int_{-\infty}^ts(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau&\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{S(f)}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}+\tfrac12S(0)\,\delta(f). \end{aligned}$$
(8.1)(8.2)(8.3)(8.4)(8.5)(8.6)(8.7)

⁠How to read it:

  • A constant is an oscillation of frequency 0; its spectrum is a single line at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$. An oscillation $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}$ is a line at $F$. A real cosine has two lines of half weight at $\pm F$.

  • A periodic signal has a line spectrum: Dirac impulses at the harmonics, with the Fourier coefficients as weights. So the Fourier series is a special case of the Fourier integral.

  • The Dirac comb (impulses spaced $T$ apart) again has a Dirac comb as its spectrum, with the spacing $1/T$ and the weight $1/|T|$ (Fig. 7). This pair becomes crucial for sampling (Part 4).

  • The step has a DC component $\frac12$ (line $\frac12\delta(f)$) and an AC component $\frac12\operatorname{sgn}(t)$, whose spectrum decays like $1/f$.

Derivation: Singular pairs

⁠Constant, oscillation: Apply symmetry to $\delta(t)\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\,1$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{1\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\,\delta(-f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\delta(f)}$. Modulation shifts the line: $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}\cdot1\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\,\delta(f-F)$. Cosine and sine follow with Euler's formulas.

⁠Line spectrum: superposition over all harmonics.

⁠Dirac comb: It is periodic with $T$. Its Fourier coefficients are $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\delta(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T}$ for all $k$. With the line spectrum, (8.5) follows.

⁠Signum: $\operatorname{sgn}(t)$ is the limiting case of $\operatorname{sgn}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-a|t|}$ as $a\to0$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-(a+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)t}\mathrm{d} t-\int_{-\infty}^0\mathrm{e}^{(a-\mathrm{j}2\pi f)t}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{a+\mathrm{j}2\pi f}-\frac{1}{a-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{-\mathrm{j}4\pi f}{a^2+(2\pi f)^2}\;\xrightarrow{a\to0}\;\frac{1}{\mathrm{j}\pi f}.}$$

⁠Step: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varepsilon(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12+\frac12\operatorname{sgn}(t)}$, so $\frac12\delta(f)+\frac{1}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}$.

⁠Integration: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_{-\infty}^ts\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s*\varepsilon}$, convolution theorem and sifting property $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)\delta(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S(0)\delta(f)}$.

QuestionBut $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\varepsilon}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\delta}$ implies $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f\,S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}$. Where does the $\frac12\delta(f)$ come from?

The equation $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f\,S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ determines $S$ only for $f\neq0$. At $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ the left-hand side is $0\cdot S(0)$, and a term $c\,\delta(f)$ drops out because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f\delta(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$. This is the counterpart of the fact that differentiation “forgets” a constant: $\varepsilon(t)$ and $\varepsilon(t)+b$ have the same derivative. Which constant is correct is decided by the even part: $\varepsilon$ has the mean value $\frac12$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{c}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$.

Example: the switched-on cosine. $\varepsilon(t)\cos(2\pi Ft)$. Multiplication becomes convolution, and convolution with a Dirac impulse shifts:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Bigl[\tfrac12\delta(f)+\tfrac{1}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}\Bigr]*\tfrac12\bigl[\delta(f+F)+\delta(f-F)\bigr] }\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac14\bigl[\delta(f+F)+\delta(f-F)\bigr]-\frac{\mathrm{j}}{4\pi}\Bigl[\frac{1}{f+F}+\frac{1}{f-F}\Bigr].}$$

Besides the halved lines at $\pm F$, a broad spectrum arises: the switch-on process itself contains frequencies near $\pm F$.

Stage 9: Hilbert transform and analytic signal

What this stage is about:

For a causal signal, the real and imaginary parts of the spectrum are linked: one determines the other. The same calculation yields a signal that contains only positive frequencies.

Causal signals. If $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ for $t<0$, then the even and odd parts are equal for $t>0$ ($\frac12s$) and opposite for $t<0$. So $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_u(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_g(t)\operatorname{sgn}(t)}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_g(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_u(t)\operatorname{sgn}(t)}$. In the frequency domain, multiplication by $\operatorname{sgn}$ becomes convolution with $\frac{1}{\mathrm{j}\pi f}$:

Theorem 9.1 (Hilbert relation for causal signals)

For a causal signal,

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Re}\{S(f)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Im}\{S(f)\}*\frac{1}{\pi f},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Im}\{S(f)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\operatorname{Re}\{S(f)\}*\frac{1}{\pi f}.}$$
(9.1)

⁠How to read it: Convolution with $\frac{1}{\pi f}$ is called the Hilbert transform. For a causal system it is enough to know the real part of the frequency response; the imaginary part follows from it. Causality is therefore a strong constraint on the spectrum.

Analytic component. With the step in the frequency domain you cut off the negative frequencies: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_+(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S(f)\,\varepsilon(f)}$. Symmetry gives $\varepsilon(f)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;\frac12\delta(t)+\frac{\mathrm{j}}{2\pi t}$, so

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_+(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12s(t)+\frac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\,\hat s(t),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\hat s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(t)*\frac{1}{\pi t}\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}(f)\,S(f).}$$
(9.2)

$s_+$ is called the analytic component, $\hat s$ the Hilbert transform of $s$. For real signals, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_-(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_+^*(-f)}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_-}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_+^*}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_++s_-}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\operatorname{Re}\{s_+(t)\}}$. The real and imaginary parts of $s_+$ are again Hilbert transforms of each other.

The Hilbert transformer. The LTI system with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{\pi t}}$ has $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}(f)}$: magnitude 1 at all frequencies (an all-pass), phase $-90^\circ$ for $f>0$ and $+90^\circ$ for $f<0$. It turns $\cos(2\pi Ft)$ into

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tfrac12\bigl[\delta(f+F)+\delta(f-F)\bigr]\cdot\bigl(-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}(f)\bigr)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\delta(f+F)-\tfrac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\delta(f-F)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;\sin(2\pi Ft).}$$

⁠In short: The Hilbert transformer shifts the phase of every oscillation by $90^\circ$ without changing its amplitude.

Stage 10: Short-time Fourier transform

What this stage is about:

The spectrum $S(f)$ describes a signal over all time. Speech or music, however, change their frequencies continually. The short-time Fourier transform (STFT) asks: which frequencies does the signal contain around the instant $t$?

The idea. Cut out a segment around $t$ and transform only that segment. With a rectangular window of width $T_0$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_T(\tau,t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(\tau)\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{\tau-t}{T_0}\Bigr)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_T(f,t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S(f)*\bigl[T_0\operatorname{si}(\pi T_0f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi tf}\bigr].}$$

The spectrum of the segment is the true spectrum, smeared by the si spectrum of the window. A short window localizes well in time but smears strongly in frequency (similarity theorem). The hard edges of the rectangle also produce slowly decaying side lobes. Smooth windows are therefore better, for example the Hann window

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{w(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\Bigl[1+\cos\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\tau}{T_0}\Bigr)\Bigr]\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{\tau}{T_0}\Bigr)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\cos^2\Bigl(\frac{\pi\tau}{T_0}\Bigr)\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{\tau}{T_0}\Bigr).}$$
(10.1)
Theorem 10.1 (Short-time Fourier transform)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_T(f,t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(\tau)\,w(\tau-t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.}$$
(10.2)

If the windows shifted by $T$ add up to a constant, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sum_nw(\tau-nT)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{c}$, then

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1c\sum_ns(\tau)\,w(\tau-nT),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1c\sum_nS_T(f,nT).}$$
(10.3)

⁠How to read it: $S_T(f,t)$ is a spectrum that depends on time. Plotting $|S_T|$ over $t$ and $f$ gives a spectrogram. For the Hann window with the offset $\textcolor{#e5383b}{T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T_0/2}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{c}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\cos^2x+\sin^2x}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ (Fig. 8): the segments fit together seamlessly into the whole signal, and the sum of their spectra is the whole spectrum.

Stage 11: Fourier and Laplace, limits of existence

What this stage is about:

When does the Fourier integral exist, and how is it related to the Laplace transform?

A sufficient condition for existence is absolute integrability, $\int|s(t)|\mathrm{d} t<\infty$. The Laplace transform extends the class by weighting with $\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}$ first (Part 2):

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_{\mathcal{L}}(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathcal{F}\bigl\{s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}\bigr\}(f).}$$

So it examines a signal for two things: for frequency components, like the Fourier transform, and for exponential growth or decay. If $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sigma}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ lies in the region of convergence, the Fourier transform is the Laplace transform on the imaginary axis. With Dirac impulses (Stage 8), however, the Fourier transform also covers signals that the Laplace transform does not cover: constants and oscillations over all time. In general it is sufficient that $\int|s(t)|\mathrm{e}^{-c|t|}\mathrm{d} t<\infty$ holds for every $c>0$. The signal may then grow like a power $t^n$, but not exponentially.

Going deeper, not needed for the rest: Why repeated convolutions become Gaussian

If you form the $M$-fold convolution of $\operatorname{rect}(t)$ with itself, the spectrum is $\operatorname{si}^M(\pi f)$. For small $f$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{si}(\pi f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1-\frac{(\pi f)^2}{6}}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\ln(1+w)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{ w}$, so

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{si}^M(\pi f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{e}^{M\ln\operatorname{si}(\pi f)}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{e}^{-M(\pi f)^2/6}\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;\sqrt{\frac{6}{\pi M}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-6t^2/M}.}$$

For large $M$ only the region of small $f$ contributes, and the approximation becomes better and better. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4}$ the exact value at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ is $0.667$, and the Gaussian curve gives $0.691$. This is an instance of the central limit theorem (the part on statistics): the convolution of many positive pulses of finite area approaches a Gaussian curve. One example is the impulse response of a cascade of many short integrators.

For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4}$: 0.691 instead of 0.667, that is, 3.6 % too high. So even after only a few convolutions the Gaussian curve is close to the exact result. The approximation becomes arbitrarily good only for large $M$, because only then do just the small $f$ contribute to the spectrum.

(b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int\mathrm{e}^{-6t^2/M}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sqrt{\pi M/6}}$; multiplied by $\sqrt{6/(\pi M)}$, this gives 1. This must be so: the area of a convolution is the product of the areas, here $\textcolor{#e5383b}{1^M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$. In the spectrum this is the value at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{si}^M(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, and the approximation $\mathrm{e}^{-M(\pi f)^2/6}$ also has the value 1 there.

Key points

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}*h}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H(f)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int h\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}$.

  • Series: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum S_p(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\int_Ts_p\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}\mathrm{d} t}$; real: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(-k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_p^*(k)}$.

  • Parseval: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac1T\int_T|s_p|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum|S_p(k)|^2}$; Gibbs: about 9 % overshoot at jumps; LTI: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{G_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_p(k)H(kF)}$.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int s\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int S\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} f}$; $s*h\leftrightarrow SH$, $s_1s_2\leftrightarrow S_1*S_2$.

  • Real: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(-f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S^*(f)}$; even $\leftrightarrow\operatorname{Re} S$, odd $\leftrightarrow\mathrm{j}\operatorname{Im} S$.

  • $s(bt)\leftrightarrow\frac1{|b|}S(f/b)$; $s(t-t_0)\leftrightarrow S\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft_0}$; $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{j}2\pi f$; $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}s\leftrightarrow S(f-F)$; $S(t)\leftrightarrow s(-f)$.

  • $\operatorname{rect}(t/T)\leftrightarrow|T|\operatorname{si}(\pi Tf)$; $\Lambda\leftrightarrow\operatorname{si}^2$; $\delta\leftrightarrow1$; $1\leftrightarrow\delta(f)$; $\cos\leftrightarrow\frac12[\delta(f+F)+\delta(f-F)]$.

  • Comb $\leftrightarrow$ comb: $\sum\delta(t-nT)\leftrightarrow\frac1{|T|}\sum\delta(f-k/T)$; $\varepsilon\leftrightarrow\frac12\delta(f)+\frac{1}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}$.

  • Hilbert: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{\pi t}}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}(f)}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_+}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12(s+\mathrm{j}\hat s)}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\operatorname{Re} s_+}$.
