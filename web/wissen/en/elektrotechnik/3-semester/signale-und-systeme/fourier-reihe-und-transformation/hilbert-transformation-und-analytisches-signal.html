Stage 9: Hilbert transform and analytic signal

What this stage is about:

For a causal signal, the real and imaginary parts of the spectrum are linked: one determines the other. The same calculation yields a signal that contains only positive frequencies.

Causal signals. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ for $t<0$, then the even and odd parts are equal for $t>0$ ($\frac12s$) and opposite for $t<0$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_u(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_g(t)\operatorname{sgn}(t)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_u(t)\operatorname{sgn}(t)}$. In the frequency domain, multiplication by $\operatorname{sgn}$ becomes convolution with $\frac{1}{\mathrm{j}\pi f}$:

Theorem 9.1 (Hilbert relation for causal signals)

For a causal signal,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}\{S(f)\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Im}\{S(f)\}*\frac{1}{\pi f},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Im}\{S(f)\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\operatorname{Re}\{S(f)\}*\frac{1}{\pi f}.}$$
(9.1)

⁠How to read it: Convolution with $\frac{1}{\pi f}$ is called the Hilbert transform. Its integral exists only as a principal value: around the point where the kernel becomes infinite, a symmetric piece is cut out and then shrunk to zero. For a causal system it is enough to know the real part of the frequency response; the imaginary part follows from it. Causality is therefore a strong constraint on the spectrum.

Analytic component. With the step in the frequency domain you cut off the negative frequencies: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_+(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(f)\,\varepsilon(f)}$. Symmetry gives $\varepsilon(f)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;\frac12\delta(t)+\frac{\mathrm{j}}{2\pi t}$, so

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_+(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12s(t)+\frac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\,\hat s(t),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s(t)*\frac{1}{\pi t}\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}(f)\,S(f).}$$
(9.2)

$s_+$ is called the analytic component, $\hat s$ the Hilbert transform of $s$; the convolution with $\frac{1}{\pi t}$ is also meant as a principal value. Likewise, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_-(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(f)\,\varepsilon(-f)}$, with the time function $s_-(t)$, contains only the negative frequencies. For real signals, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_-(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S_+^*(-f)}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_+^*}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_++s_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\operatorname{Re}\{s_+(t)\}}$. The real and imaginary parts of $s_+$ are again Hilbert transforms of each other.

The Hilbert transformer. The LTI system with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{\pi t}}$ has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}(f)}$: magnitude 1 at all frequencies (an all-pass), phase $-90^\circ$ for $f>0$ and $+90^\circ$ for $f<0$. It turns $\cos(2\pi Ft)$ into

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12\bigl[\delta(f+F)+\delta(f-F)\bigr]\cdot\bigl(-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}(f)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\delta(f+F)-\tfrac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\delta(f-F)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;\sin(2\pi Ft).}$$

⁠In short: The Hilbert transformer shifts the phase of every oscillation by $90^\circ$ without changing its amplitude.

Exercise 12 ★★ Hilbert transformer

  1. What does the Hilbert transformer make of $\sin(2\pi Ft)$? What does applying it twice give?

  2. Find the analytic component $s_+(t)$ of $\cos(2\pi Ft)$ and check $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\operatorname{Re}\{s_+\}}$.

  3. Why is the Hilbert transformer not causal? Look at $h(t)$.

Solution

(a) $\sin(2\pi Ft)\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\frac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\delta(f+F)-\frac{\mathrm{j}}{2}\delta(f-F)$. Multiplication by $-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}(f)$: at $-F$ by $+\mathrm{j}$, at $+F$ by $-\mathrm{j}$. Result: $-\frac12\delta(f+F)-\frac12\delta(f-F)$, that is, $-\cos(2\pi Ft)$. Applied twice: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{sgn}f)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, so the signal is inverted.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_+(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(f)\varepsilon(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\delta(f-F)}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_+(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\bigl[\cos(2\pi Ft)+\mathrm{j}\sin(2\pi Ft)\bigr]}$. This agrees with $\frac12(s+\mathrm{j}\hat s)$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sin}$. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\operatorname{Re}\{s_+\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\cos(2\pi Ft)}$.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{\pi t}}$ is nonzero for $t<0$ as well. So the output at time $t$ depends on future input values. In practice, the Hilbert transformer is approximated with a truncated impulse response, at the cost of a delay.
