Stage 11: Fourier and Laplace, limits of existence

What this stage is about:

When does the Fourier integral exist, and how is it related to the Laplace transform?

A sufficient condition for existence is absolute integrability, $\int|s(t)|\mathrm{d} t<\infty$. The Laplace transform extends the class by weighting with $\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}$ first (Chapter 2):

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_{\mathcal{L}}(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathcal{F}\bigl\{s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}\bigr\}(f).}$$

So it examines a signal for two things: for frequency components, like the Fourier transform, and for exponential growth or decay. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ lies in the region of convergence, the Fourier transform is the Laplace transform on the imaginary axis. With Dirac impulses (Stage 8), however, the Fourier transform also covers signals that the Laplace transform does not cover: constants and oscillations over all time. In general it is sufficient that the signal grows at most as fast as a power of $t$, more precisely, that $\int|s(t)|\,(1+t^2)^{-n}\mathrm{d} t<\infty$ holds for some $n\ge0$. Constants, oscillations, the step and $t^n$ satisfy this, but exponentially growing signals such as $\mathrm{e}^{t}\varepsilon(t)$ do not; only the Laplace transform covers them.

Going deeper, not needed for the rest: Why repeated convolutions become Gaussian

If you form the $M$-fold convolution of $\operatorname{rect}(t)$ with itself, the spectrum is $\operatorname{si}^M(\pi f)$. For small $f$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{si}(\pi f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\frac{(\pi f)^2}{6}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ln(1+w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ w}$, so

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{si}^M(\pi f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{M\ln\operatorname{si}(\pi f)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-M(\pi f)^2/6}\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;\sqrt{\frac{6}{\pi M}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-6t^2/M}.}$$

For large $M$ only the region of small $f$ contributes, and the approximation becomes better and better. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ the exact value at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is $0.667$, and the Gaussian curve gives $0.691$. This is an instance of the central limit theorem (the part on statistics): the convolution of many identical positive pulses of finite area approaches a Gaussian curve, provided the pulses decay fast enough, more precisely, provided their second moment $\int t^2s(t)\,\mathrm{d} t$ is finite. One example is the impulse response of a cascade of many short integrators. Without this condition it does not work: $\frac{1}{1+t^2}$ from Exercise 7 has an infinite second moment, and the $M$-fold convolution, with the spectrum $\pi^M\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi M|f|}$, is again a stretched curve of the form $\frac{1}{1+t^2}$.

Exercise 14 ★★★ (Going further) Checking the Gaussian approximation

  1. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, compare the exact value $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Lambda(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ with the Gaussian approximation.

  2. Show that the Gaussian curve $\sqrt{6/(\pi M)}\,\mathrm{e}^{-6t^2/M}$ has the area 1. Why must this be so?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{6/(2\pi)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.977}$ instead of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Lambda(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, that is, 2.3 % too low. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$: 0.691 instead of 0.667, that is, 3.6 % too high. So even after only a few convolutions the Gaussian curve is close to the exact result. The approximation becomes arbitrarily good only for large $M$, because only then do just the small $f$ contribute to the spectrum.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int\mathrm{e}^{-6t^2/M}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{\pi M/6}}$; multiplied by $\sqrt{6/(\pi M)}$, this gives 1. This must be so: the area of a convolution is the product of the areas, here $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1^M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. In the spectrum this is the value at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{si}^M(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, and the approximation $\mathrm{e}^{-M(\pi f)^2/6}$ also has the value 1 there.
