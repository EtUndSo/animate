Stage 4: From periodic to non-repeating signals

What this stage is about:

A single pulse is not periodic. But it can be regarded as a periodic signal with an infinitely long period. In the process, the sum becomes an integral.

The idea. Continue a pulse $s(t)$ of duration less than $T$ periodically. The coefficients of the series are then values of a fixed function:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1T\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{F\cdot S(kF),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t.}$$

If you double the period, the line spacing $F$ is halved, and the lines sample the same envelope $S(f)$ twice as densely (Fig. 4). In the limit $T\to\infty$, the spacing $F$ becomes the differential $\mathrm{d} f$, and the sum $\sum_kS(kF)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kFt}F$ becomes the integral.

Theorem 4.1 (Fourier transform)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} t,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(4.1)

Shorthand: $s(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f)$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathcal{F}\{s(t)\}}$.

⁠How to read it: $S(f)$ is a spectral density: weight per frequency interval. If $s$ is a voltage in V, $S$ has the unit V/Hz. The inverse transform builds the signal from all oscillations, each with the weight $S(f)\,\mathrm{d} f$. A sufficient condition for existence is $\int|s(t)|\mathrm{d} t<\infty$. The Fourier transform is the Laplace transform on the imaginary axis, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathrm{j}2\pi f)}$, provided the axis lies in the region of convergence (Chapter 2).

⁠Convolution becomes a product. As in Chapter 2: $s(t)*h(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f)\,H(f)$. From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s*\delta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(f)\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\delta\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(f)}$, so $\delta(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;1$: the Dirac impulse contains all frequencies with equal weight.

Examplethe exponential pulse

The impulse response of the RC circuit, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{RC}\varepsilon(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t/RC}}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{RC}\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-(1/RC+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)t}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{j}2\pi f\,RC}.}$$

This is the same frequency response as from AC analysis (Chapter 1). So the impulse response contains all the information about the frequency behavior.

QuestionWhat is the difference between $S_p(k)$ and $S(f)$, apart from the fact that one is discrete?

The unit. $S_p(k)$ is an amplitude (V): the $k$th harmonic really has this strength. $S(f)$ is a density (V/Hz): in a non-repeating signal no single frequency has a finite amplitude; only a frequency band $\Delta f$ carries approximately $S(f)\,\Delta f$. That is why $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{F\cdot S(kF)}$: each line represents a band of width $F$.

Exercise 4 ★ The two-sided exponential pulse

Compute $\mathcal{F}\{\mathrm{e}^{-a|t|}\}$ for $a>0$ by splitting the integral into $t<0$ and $t>0$. Show that the result is real and even, and explain this with the symmetry of the signal.

Solution

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_{-\infty}^0\mathrm{e}^{at}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t+\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-at}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{a-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}+\frac{1}{a+\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2a}{a^2+(2\pi f)^2}.}$$

Real and even in $f$. This must be so: the signal is real and even, so its odd part is zero, and hence so is $\operatorname{Im} S$ (Stage 5).
