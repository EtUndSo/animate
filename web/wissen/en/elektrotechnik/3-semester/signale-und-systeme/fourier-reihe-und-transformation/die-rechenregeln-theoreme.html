What this stage is about:

Instead of evaluating every integral anew, you use rules: what happens to the spectrum when the signal is shifted, compressed, differentiated or modulated? Each rule follows from what the operation does to a single oscillation.

Theorem 6.1 (Theorems of the Fourier transform)
$$\begin{aligned} &\text{Superposition:} && \textstyle\sum_ia_is_i(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\sum_ia_iS_i(f)\\ &\text{Conjugation:} && s^*(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S^*(-f)\\ &\text{Similarity (scaling):} && s(bt)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{1}{|b|}S\Bigl(\frac fb\Bigr),\quad b\neq0;\qquad s(-t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(-f)\\ &\text{Shift:} && s(t-t_0)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft_0},\qquad\delta(t-t_0)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft_0}\\ &\text{Derivative:} && \frac{\mathrm{d}^n}{\mathrm{d} t^n}s(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;(\mathrm{j}2\pi f)^nS(f)\\ &\text{Symmetry (duality):} && S(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;s(-f)\\ &\text{Modulation:} && \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}s(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f-F)\\ &\text{Frequency-domain derivative:} && -\mathrm{j}2\pi t\,s(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} f}S(f)\\ &\text{Convolution, multiplication:} && s_1*s_2\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_1S_2,\qquad s_1s_2\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_1*S_2\\ &\text{Areas:} && \int s(t)\mathrm{d} t=S(0),\qquad\int S(f)\mathrm{d} f=s(0)\\ &\text{Parseval:} && \int|s(t)|^2\mathrm{d} t=\int|S(f)|^2\mathrm{d} f \end{aligned}$$
(6.1)(6.2)(6.3)(6.4)(6.5)(6.6)(6.7)(6.8)(6.9)(6.10)(6.11)

⁠How to read the most important ones:

  • ⁠Similarity: A signal compressed in time ($|b|>1$) has a wider spectrum. Short signals need many frequencies. Duration and bandwidth are inversely related.

  • ⁠Shift: A delay does not change the magnitude, only the phase, and it does so linearly in $f$: every oscillation is shifted by the same time, which at a higher frequency is a larger angle.

  • ⁠Derivative: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}}$. Differentiation amplifies high frequencies.

  • ⁠Symmetry: Forward and inverse transform differ only in the sign of the exponent. Every transform pair therefore yields a second one with the roles swapped.

  • ⁠Modulation: Multiplication by $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}$ shifts the whole spectrum by $F$. This is how every radio transmitter works.

  • ⁠Multiplication: The spectrum of the product of two signals is the convolution of the spectra, the counterpart of the convolution theorem.

  • ⁠Parseval: The energy is the same in both domains. $|S(f)|^2$ is the energy density over frequency.

Derivation: Similarity, shift, derivative, symmetry, modulation, multiplication, Parseval

⁠Similarity ($b>0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{bt}$): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int s(bt)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1b\int s(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi(f/b)\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1bS(f/b)}$. For $b<0$ the limits are reversed, hence $1/|b|$.

⁠Shift ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t-t_0}$): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int s(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f(\tau+t_0)}\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft_0}S(f)}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\delta}$, the pair for $\delta(t-t_0)$ follows.

⁠Derivative: Differentiate the inverse transform with respect to $t$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\int S\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int\mathrm{j}2\pi f\,S\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} f}$.

⁠Symmetry: In the inverse transform, replace $t$ by $-t$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(-t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int S(f)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} f}$. Now swap the names of the variables $t$ and $f$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(-f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int S(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}$, which is the forward transform of $S(t)$.

⁠Modulation: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Ft}s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi(f-F)t}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(f-F)}$. The derivative in the frequency domain follows in the same way by differentiating the forward transform with respect to $f$.

⁠Multiplication: Convolution theorem for the signals $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t)$, that is $s_1*s_2\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_1S_2$, then apply symmetry to this pair: $S_1(t)S_2(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;s_1(-f)*s_2(-f)$. Renaming gives (6.9).

⁠Areas: Set $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ in the forward transform and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ in the inverse transform.

⁠Parseval: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int|s|^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int s\,s^*\mathrm{d} t}$ is the product $s(t)s^*(t)$, integrated, so by multiplication, conjugation and the area theorem it equals $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\bigl[S(f)*S^*(-f)\bigr]_{f=0}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int S(\nu)S^*(\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu}$.

QuestionWhy doesn't a delay change the magnitude of the spectrum?

Because a delay neither attenuates nor amplifies any oscillation; it only shifts each one. Every oscillation is shifted by the same time $t_0$, which at the frequency $f$ is the angle $2\pi ft_0$. That is why the phase is a straight line in $f$ with the slope $-2\pi t_0$. A system with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|H|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and linear phase delays a signal without changing its shape.

Exercise 6 ★★ Applying the theorems

Use $\operatorname{rect}(t)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\operatorname{si}(\pi f)$ (directly from the definition: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{si}(\pi f)}$) and the theorems.

  1. Find the spectrum of a rectangular pulse of height 1 from $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ s. What is its magnitude at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and where is the first zero?

  2. Find the spectrum of $\cos(2\pi F_0t)\operatorname{rect}(t)$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ Hz and sketch it.

Solution

(a) The pulse is $\operatorname{rect}\bigl(\frac{t-1}{2}\bigr)$. Similarity ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$) and shift ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ s): $2\operatorname{si}(2\pi f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}$. Magnitude at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: 2 (the area). First zero at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5}$ Hz. The shift changes only the phase.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\cos(2\pi F_0t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi F_0t}+\frac12\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi F_0t}}$, modulation: $\frac12\operatorname{si}(\pi(f-5))+\frac12\operatorname{si}(\pi(f+5))$. Two si main lobes of height $\frac12$ at $\pm5$ Hz, with the first zeros 1 Hz on either side.

Exercise 7 ★★ Symmetry as a tool

From Exercise 4 you know that $\mathrm{e}^{-|t|}\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{2}{1+(2\pi f)^2}$. Use this to find the spectrum of $\frac{1}{1+t^2}$ without an integral.

Solution

Apply symmetry to the pair: $\frac{2}{1+(2\pi t)^2}\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\mathrm{e}^{-|f|}$. Now stretch the time axis. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2}{1+(2\pi t)^2}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x\bigl(\frac{t}{2\pi}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2}{1+t^2}}$, so by the similarity theorem with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{2\pi}}$ it is $\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;2\pi\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi|f|}$. Halving gives

$$\frac{1}{1+t^2}\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\pi\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi|f|}.$$

Check with the area theorem: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int\frac{\mathrm{d} t}{1+t^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pi}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(0)}$.
