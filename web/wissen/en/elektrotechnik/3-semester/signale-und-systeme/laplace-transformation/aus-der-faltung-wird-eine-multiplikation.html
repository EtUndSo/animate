Stage 3: Convolution becomes multiplication

What this stage is about:

Stage 1 says what the system does to one building block. Stage 2 says that every signal consists of building blocks. Together they give the most important theorem of the Laplace transform.

The idea. An LTI system is linear: it processes each building block of the input separately and adds the results. It multiplies each building block $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$ by $H(p)$. So the output consists of the same building blocks as the input, except that each weight is multiplied by $H(p)$. Figure 4 shows the two routes to the output.

Theorem 3.1 (Convolution theorem)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s(t)*h(t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(p)\,H(p),}$$
(3.1)

valid for all $p$ that lie in the regions of convergence of both $S$ and $H$.

⁠How to read it: $S(p)$ is the weight of the building block $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$ in the input, $H(p)$ is the factor by which the system multiplies this building block, and $G(p)$ is the weight in the output. The condition on $p$ arises because the line of the inversion formula must run through both regions of convergence: the input must be decomposable into building blocks, and the system must be able to process each of them.

Derivation: Convolution theorem from superposition and eigenfunction

Insert the input as the superposition (2.1) into the convolution, using a line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re} p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma}$ that lies in both regions of convergence:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s(t)*h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left[\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{j}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{j}\infty}S(p)\,\mathrm{e}^{pt}\,\mathrm{d} p\right]*h(t).}$$

Linearity: the convolution may be moved inside the integral, where it acts on each building block separately:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{j}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{j}\infty}S(p)\,\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{pt}*h(t)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d} p.}$$

Eigenfunction (1.1): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{pt}*h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{H(p)\,\mathrm{e}^{pt}}$.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{j}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{j}\infty}\bigl[S(p)H(p)\bigr]\,\mathrm{e}^{pt}\,\mathrm{d} p.}$$

This is the inversion formula for $g$ with the weight $S(p)H(p)$. Since the weights of a signal are unique, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(p)H(p)}$.

⁠In short: Convolution becomes multiplication because the system only multiplies each building block $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$ by the number $H(p)$.

Analogy (a picture for orientation, not mathematics)

A graphic equalizer splits music into frequency bands, multiplies each band by the setting of its slider and puts the bands back together. An LTI system does the same with the building blocks $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$, and the slider setting is $H(p)$. The analogy breaks down in two places: an equalizer has finitely many bands with real slider settings. Here there is a continuum of building blocks, which can also grow or decay, and each “slider” is a complex number that also shifts the phase.

The same mechanism explains derivatives, integrals and differential equations.

  • ⁠Differentiation. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\mathrm{e}^{pt}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p\,\mathrm{e}^{pt}}$. The differentiator is an LTI system, and each building block is an eigenfunction of it with the eigenvalue $p$. So each weight is multiplied by $p$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal{L}\{\dot s\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p\,S(p)}$.

  • ⁠Integration. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{p\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{pt}/p}$, but only for $\operatorname{Re} p>0$; otherwise the integral diverges at $-\infty$. Hence $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal{L}\{\int_{-\infty}^t s\,\mathrm{d}\tau\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(p)/p}$ with the additional condition $\operatorname{Re} p>0$.

  • ⁠Cascade. Two systems in series multiply first by $H_1(p)$ and then by $H_2(p)$, together by $H_1H_2$. The order does not matter, because numbers commute.

  • ⁠Differential equation. Each derivative becomes a factor $p$. A linear differential equation with constant coefficients

    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{r=0}^{R}\alpha_r\frac{\mathrm{d}^r g}{\mathrm{d} t^r}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\beta_q\frac{\mathrm{d}^q s}{\mathrm{d} t^q} \quad\text{becomes}\quad \Bigl(\sum_{r}\alpha_rp^r\Bigr)G(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\sum_q\beta_qp^q\Bigr)S(p),}$$

    that is, an algebraic equation, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G/S}$ is a ratio of polynomials.

Example: the step response of the RC low-pass filter. Input $\varepsilon(t)$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/p}$ ($\operatorname{Re} p>0$), system $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/(p+1)}$ ($\operatorname{Re} p>-1$). The common region is $\operatorname{Re} p>0$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{p+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1p-\frac{1}{p+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t}\bigr)\varepsilon(t).}$$

The inverse transform of the two terms follows from (2.2) with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. Checked directly: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_0^t\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-t}}$ for $t>0$. The inversion formula (2.1), evaluated numerically on the line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5/\mathrm s}$, also gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(0.5\,\mathrm{s})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.3935}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(1\,\mathrm{s})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6321}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(3\,\mathrm{s})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.9502}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(-1\,\mathrm{s})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, exactly $\bigl(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t}\bigr)\varepsilon(t)$.

Exercise 5 ★★ Cascade and derivative via the building blocks

  1. Use the building-block picture (without the convolution integral) to explain why two LTI systems in cascade have the transfer function $H_1(p)H_2(p)$. Why does it follow that convolution is commutative?

  2. Derive $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal{L}\{\dot s(t)\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p\,S(p)}$ from the inversion formula by differentiating it with respect to $t$.

Solution

(a) The input consists of building blocks $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$ with weights $S(p)$. The first system multiplies each building block by $H_1(p)$, so the weights are then $H_1S$. The second multiplies by $H_2(p)$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{H_2H_1S}$. So the cascade has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{H_1H_2}$. Because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H_1H_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{H_2H_1}$, the result does not depend on the order, and since $h_1*h_2$ is the impulse response of the cascade, it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_1*h_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{h_2*h_1}$.

(b) Differentiating under the integral acts only on $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\dot s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{j}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{j}\infty}S(p)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\mathrm{e}^{pt}\,\mathrm{d} p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{j}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{j}\infty}\bigl[p\,S(p)\bigr]\mathrm{e}^{pt}\,\mathrm{d} p.}$$

This is the inversion formula with the weight $pS(p)$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal{L}\{\dot s\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{pS(p)}$.

Exercise 6 ★★ Ramp response of the RC low-pass filter

Input $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t\,\varepsilon(t)}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/p^2}$ for $\operatorname{Re} p>0$, basic transform pair (2.5).

  1. Find $G(p)$ and its region of convergence.

  2. Check the decomposition $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{p^2}-\frac1p+\frac{1}{p+1}}$ and find $g(t)$.

  3. Check: what is $g(0)$, and why must it be so? What is $\dot g(t)$?

  4. How does $g(t)$ behave for $t\gg1$ s? Compare with Exercise 1c.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{1}{p^2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{p+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{1}{p^2(p+1)}}$ for $\operatorname{Re} p>0$ (intersection of $\operatorname{Re} p>0$ and $\operatorname{Re} p>-1$).

(b) Over the common denominator: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{1}{p^2}-\frac1p+\frac{1}{p+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(p+1)-p(p+1)+p^2}{p^2(p+1)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{p^2(p+1)}}$. Inverse transform with (2.5) and (2.2):

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(t-1+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\bigr)\varepsilon(t).}$$

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0-1+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: the capacitor voltage cannot jump, and the input starts at zero. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\dot g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t})\varepsilon(t)}$, exactly the step response. This fits: the ramp is the integral of the step, so the ramp response is the integral of the step response.

(d) For $t\gg1$ s, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ t-1}$: the output follows the ramp with a delay of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{RC}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ s, just as for the two-sided ramp in Exercise 1c. The difference is the building block $\mathrm{e}^{-t}$ (pole $-1$ of the system): the transient, which arises because here the ramp only starts at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.
