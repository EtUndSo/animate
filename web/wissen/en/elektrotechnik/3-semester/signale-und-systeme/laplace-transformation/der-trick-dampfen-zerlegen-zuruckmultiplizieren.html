The trick: damp, decompose, multiply back

2 min read2 figuresJump to summaryAI-generated

  • Español
  • Français
  • Türkçe
  • 中文

The idea. First make the signal decomposable, then undo the change afterwards:

  1. ⁠Damp. Multiply by $\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}$. If a right-sided signal grows at most like $\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}$, then $s(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}$ decays for every $\sigma>\alpha$ and is absolutely integrable.

  2. ⁠Decompose. The damped signal is a superposition of pure oscillations. Its Fourier spectrum is exactly the Laplace transform $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int s(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-pt}\mathrm{d} t}$ on the line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re} p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma}$ (Exercise 2):

    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal{F}\bigl\{s(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}\bigr\}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f).}$$

  3. ⁠Multiply back. Multiply the decomposition by $\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}$. The factor does not depend on $f$ and may be moved inside the integral. There it turns each oscillation $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}$ into the building block $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{pt}}$.

The result is the conjectured superposition.

Theorem 2.1 (Inversion formula of the Laplace transform)

If $\sigma$ lies in the region of convergence of $S(p)$, then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{j}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{j}\infty}S(p)\,\mathrm{e}^{pt}\,\mathrm{d} p }\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)\,\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)t}\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(2.1)

⁠How to read it:

  • The path of integration is the vertical line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re} p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma}$ in the $p$-plane, traversed from bottom to top ($f$ from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, Fig. 2).

  • $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$ is the building block, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(p)\,\mathrm{d} p/(2\pi\mathrm{j})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)\,\mathrm{d} f}$ is its complex weight. $S(p)$ itself is a density: weight per frequency interval. If $s$ is a voltage in V, $S$ has the unit V s.

  • The integral is meant as the limit $\lim_{F\to\infty}\int_{-F}^{F}\dots\mathrm{d} f$. It returns $s(t)$ at every point where $s$ is continuous, and at a jump it returns the mean of the left-hand and right-hand limits.

⁠Limiting case $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: If the imaginary axis lies in the region of convergence, you may choose $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}$, and (2.1) becomes the Fourier inversion formula. The Laplace transform contains the Fourier transform as a special case.

Derivation: Inversion formula from the Fourier inversion formula

By step 2, $S(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)$ is the Fourier spectrum of $s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}$. The Fourier inversion formula gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$

Multiply by $\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}$, which does not depend on $f$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)\,\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f)t}\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$

Substitute $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma+\mathrm{j}2\pi f}$ with $\sigma$ fixed: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{d} p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{j}2\pi\,\mathrm{d} f}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{d} f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{d} p/(2\pi\mathrm{j})}$. The limits $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mp\infty}$ become $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma\mp\mathrm{j}\infty}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{j}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{j}\infty}S(p)\,\mathrm{e}^{pt}\,\mathrm{d} p.}$$

Example: the step from building blocks. For $\varepsilon(t)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/p}$ with $\operatorname{Re} p>0$. We choose $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5/\mathrm s}$ (Fig. 2). Figure 3 shows the result of superposing only the building blocks with $|f|\le F$. Already at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25}$ Hz the shape of the step is recognizable. As $F$ grows, the edge becomes steeper; at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ the curve tends to the mean value $\tfrac12$ (0.40, 0.47, 0.49), and at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ s it tends to zero (0.053, 0.029, 0.008).

QuestionAll building blocks are complex. Why does a real curve come out?

The building blocks come in pairs: $f$ goes with $-f$, so $p$ goes with the conjugate point $p^*$. For a real signal the weight there is the complex conjugate, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(p^*)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(p)^*}$ (computing $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int s(t)\mathrm{e}^{-pt}\mathrm{d} t}$ with real $s$ shows this). Each pair gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S\mathrm{e}^{pt}+(S\mathrm{e}^{pt})^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\operatorname{Re}\{S(p)\mathrm{e}^{pt}\}}$, a real damped or growing oscillation. The imaginary parts cancel in pairs.

QuestionSo is it a sum or an integral?

In general an integral: the line carries a continuum of building blocks, each with an infinitesimal weight $S\,\mathrm{d} f$.
