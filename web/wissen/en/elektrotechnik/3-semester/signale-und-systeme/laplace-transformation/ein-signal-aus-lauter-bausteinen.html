What this stage is about:

Stage 1 applies to a single building block. It becomes useful only if every signal can be built from building blocks $\mathrm{e}^{pt}$. This stage shows that this works, what the weights are called and where the limits lie.

Starting point $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: pure oscillations#

On the imaginary axis ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$) the building blocks are pure oscillations $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}$. An absolutely integrable signal ($\int|s(t)|\,\mathrm{d} t<\infty$, so it decays fast enough on both sides) can be built from them. This is the Fourier representation. Its formula is all that is needed here. The function $\mathcal{F}\{s\}(f)$ is called the spectrum; it gives the weight of the oscillation with frequency $f$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}\{s\}(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} f,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal{F}\{s\}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft}\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$

For many signals in system theory this is not enough. The step $\varepsilon(t)$ does not decay, and $\mathrm{e}^{2t}\varepsilon(t)$ (the impulse response of an unstable system) even grows. A signal that grows without bound cannot be built from oscillations of constant amplitude.
