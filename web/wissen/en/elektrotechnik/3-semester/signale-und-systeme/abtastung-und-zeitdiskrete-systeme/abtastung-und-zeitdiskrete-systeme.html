What this is about

A computer cannot store a function $s(t)$, only a sequence of numbers. The signal is therefore measured at fixed intervals $T$, and one works with the samples $s(nT)$. Two questions shape this article: Is information lost in the process? And: How do you calculate with sequences of numbers the way Parts 1 to 3 calculated with functions?

The answer in three sentences (by the end of the article they should feel obvious)

  1. Sampling is multiplication by a Dirac comb. In the spectrum this becomes a convolution with a comb: the spectrum repeats periodically with the sampling rate.

  2. If the repetitions do not lie on top of each other (sampling rate above twice the highest frequency), the signal can be recovered exactly with an ideal low-pass filter. Otherwise aliasing occurs.

  3. The same tools apply to sequences of numbers as to functions: convolution as a sum, a Fourier transform with a periodic spectrum, the DFT as its samples, the z-transform as the counterpart of the Laplace transform.

One principle behind all the results: discrete in one domain means periodic in the other.

Stage 1: Signal classes and ideal sampling

What this stage is about:

What kinds of signals are there, and how is sampling described mathematically?

Four classes. Time and amplitude can each be continuous or discrete. An analog signal is continuous in both. A discrete-time signal has values only at the times $nT$, but these with arbitrary precision. A digital signal is in addition stepped in amplitude (quantized), because a computer can represent only finitely many numbers. This article deals with the discretization of time. The quantization of the amplitude is a separate topic.

From the real to the ideal sampler. In every clock cycle, a real sampler closes a switch for the short time $T_0$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_0(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(t)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{t-nT}{T_0}\Bigr)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(t)\Bigl[\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{t}{T_0}\Bigr)*\sum_n\delta(t-nT)\Bigr].}$$
(1.1)

If $T_0$ is made shorter and shorter, only the values $s(nT)$ remain. Mathematically, the rectangle is replaced by a Dirac impulse. This is the ideal sampler:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_\mathrm a(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(t)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}s(nT)\,\delta(t-nT).}$$
(1.2)

The sampled signal is a train of Dirac impulses, each with a sample as its weight. The second step is the sifting property $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)\delta(t-nT)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(nT)\delta(t-nT)}$. In this form, sampled signals can be treated with all the tools for continuous signals.

Stage 2: The spectrum of the sampled signal

What this stage is about:

What does sampling do to the spectrum? This stage shows that it repeats periodically.

The idea. Two oscillations whose frequencies differ by a multiple of the sampling rate $1/T$ have the same samples:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi(f_0+k/T)nT}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0nT}\cdot\underbrace{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kn}}_{=1}.}$$

The samples cannot tell them apart. Therefore the spectrum of the samples must look the same at $f_0$ and at $f_0+k/T$. It is periodic.

Theorem 2.1 (Spectrum of the ideally sampled signal)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_\mathrm a(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}S\Bigl(f-\frac kT\Bigr)}$$
$$(T>0).$$
(2.1)

⁠How to read it: the spectrum of the original appears with its shape unchanged, but with the factor $1/T$ and repeated at every multiple of the sampling rate. The copies are called spectral images.

Derivation: Spectrum of the sampled signal

Multiplication becomes convolution (Part 3), and the spectrum of the Dirac comb is again a comb:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)\cdot\sum_n\delta(t-nT)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S(f)*\frac1T\sum_k\delta\Bigl(f-\frac kT\Bigr)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\sum_kS\Bigl(f-\frac kT\Bigr).}$$

Convolution with a shifted Dirac impulse shifts the spectrum.

QuestionHow does the spectrum “know” that the signal was sampled? The signal between the sampling points is simply gone, after all.

That is exactly why. Between the sampling points the sampled signal is zero, except at the impulses. Every oscillation that has the same values at all points $nT$ describes this signal equally well. The spectrum cannot decide between them and contains all of them: the original and its copies at every multiple of $1/T$.

Stage 3: The sampling theorem and reconstruction

What this stage is about:

When can the original be recovered exactly from the samples, and how?

Theorem 3.1 (Sampling theorem)

A signal whose spectrum vanishes above $f_\mathrm g$ (low-pass (band-limited) signal) is uniquely determined by its samples $s(nT)$ if

$$T\le\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}$$
$$\text{that is}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1T\ge2f_\mathrm g.}$$
(3.1)

With the ideal low-pass filter $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_\mathrm{TP}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\operatorname{rect}\bigl(\frac{f}{2f_\mathrm g}\bigr)}$,

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_\mathrm a(t)*\bigl[2f_\mathrm gT\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)\bigr],}$$
(3.2)

and for $\textcolor{#e5383b}{T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/(2f_\mathrm g)}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}s(nT)\,\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\pi\frac{t-nT}{T}\Bigr).}$$
(3.3)

⁠How to read it: for $f_\mathrm A\ge2f_\mathrm g$ the copies in Fig. 1 lie apart. A low-pass filter cuts out the copy at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, and it has exactly the shape of the original. In the time domain this means: each sample is replaced by an si function that is 1 at its own sampling point and zero at all the others (Fig. 2). Their sum hits all the samples and fills the gaps in such a way that no frequency above $f_\mathrm g$ arises. The minimum rate $2f_\mathrm g$ is called the Nyquist rate.

Derivation: Reconstruction

For $|f|\le f_\mathrm g$ only the copy $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ is present, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_\mathrm a(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1TS(f)}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_\mathrm{TP}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\operatorname{rect}(f/2f_\mathrm g)}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_\mathrm a\cdot H_\mathrm{TP}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S}$. In the time domain: $T\operatorname{rect}(f/2f_\mathrm g)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;2f_\mathrm gT\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)$ (Part 3, rectangle and si). With (1.2) and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{2f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/T}$ this gives $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_\mathrm a*\operatorname{si}(\pi t/T)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ns(nT)\operatorname{si}(\pi(t-nT)/T)}$.

Aliasing. If the sampling is too slow, the copies overlap. A frequency above $f_\mathrm A/2$ appears after reconstruction as a false, lower frequency, its alias. In the reference model, a cosine at 700 Hz and one at 300 Hz have exactly the same samples at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ kHz, because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\cos(2\pi\cdot0.7n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\cos(2\pi n-2\pi\cdot0.3n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\cos(2\pi\cdot0.3n)}$ (Fig. 3). Afterwards the two can no longer be separated. This is why the signal is filtered with a low-pass filter before every sampler (anti-aliasing filter).

QuestionIsn't exactly $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2f_\mathrm g}$ enough? For a sine at exactly $f_\mathrm g$ it goes wrong, though.

Yes, exactly at the limit it goes wrong: with an unfavorable phase, a sine with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{f_\mathrm A/2}$ can be sampled at a zero crossing every time, and all samples are zero. Strictly speaking, the theorem therefore requires that the spectrum has no line at $f_\mathrm g$, or that $f_\mathrm A>2f_\mathrm g$. In practice one samples faster anyway, because real low-pass filters do not drop off vertically and need room for their transition band.

Exercise 1 ★ Alias frequencies

$\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ kHz.

  1. At what frequency does a cosine at 1.2 kHz appear after reconstruction?

  2. Which frequencies give the same samples as a cosine at 50 Hz?

Solution

(a) 1.2 kHz lies above $f_\mathrm A/2$. It has the same samples as $\textcolor{#e5383b}{1.2-1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.2}$ kHz and appears as 200 Hz.

(b) All $\pm50\,\mathrm{Hz}+k\cdot1\,\mathrm{kHz}$: 950 Hz, 1050 Hz, 1950 Hz, 2050 Hz and so on. For the cosine, negative and positive frequency count equally, which is why $1000-50$ belongs to them as well.

Exercise 2 ★★ Sampling rates in practice

  1. Telephone speech is limited to 3.4 kHz and sampled at 8 kHz. What is the Nyquist rate, and what is the margin for?

  2. An audio CD samples at 44.1 kHz. Up to what frequency can it store sounds, and how wide is the transition band for the anti-aliasing filter if the audible band extends to 20 kHz?

Solution

(a) Nyquist rate $\textcolor{#e5383b}{2\cdot3.4}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{6.8}$ kHz. With 8 kHz, $f_\mathrm A/2$ lies at 4 kHz. Between 3.4 and 4 kHz the anti-aliasing filter has room to make the transition from passband to stopband.

(b) Up to $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm A/2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{22.05}$ kHz. The transition band from 20 to 22.05 kHz is only 2.05 kHz wide. This is why steep filters or oversampling are needed.

Stage 4: Sampling in the frequency domain

What this stage is about:

The same principle holds with the roles swapped: if you sample the spectrum, the signal becomes periodic.

A spectrum that is known only at the frequencies $kF$ is a comb of weighted Dirac impulses. With the factor $F$, the weights are exactly the Fourier coefficients from Part 3:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{F\sum_kS(kF)\,\delta(f-kF)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{F\,S(f)\cdot\sum_k\delta(f-kF)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;s_p(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ns\Bigl(t-\frac nF\Bigr).}$$
(4.1)

The corresponding signal is the original, repeated periodically with period $1/F$. If the signal is shorter than $1/F$, the repetitions do not overlap. Then it can be recovered with a time window, and the spectrum with si functions:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_p(t)\cdot\operatorname{rect}(Ft)}$$
$$\Longrightarrow$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_kS(kF)\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\pi\frac{f-kF}{F}\Bigr).}$$
(4.2)

⁠In short: discrete in time means periodic in the spectrum, and discrete in the spectrum means periodic in time. The second is the Fourier series from Part 3: its coefficients are samples of the spectrum of a single pulse, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_p(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{F\cdot S(kF)}$.

Stage 5: Discrete-time signals and discrete convolution

What this stage is about:

From now on, all calculations use only the sequences of numbers $s(n)$. This stage carries convolution, elementary signals and LTI systems over to sequences.

Convolution of the samples. If the input $s$ and the impulse response $h$ of a system are low-pass signals and sampling is at the Nyquist rate, each can be written as an si series (3.3). Because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{si}(\pi t/T)*\operatorname{si}(\pi t/T)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\operatorname{si}(\pi t/T)}$ (Part 3), the samples of the output follow from the samples alone:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(nT)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}s(mT)\,h\bigl((n-m)T\bigr).}$$

Normalized to $\textcolor{#e5383b}{T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, this is the discrete convolution:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}s(m)\,h(n-m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n)*h(n).}$$
(5.1)

Elementary sequences.

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\delta(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\begin{cases}1&n=0\\0&n\neq0\end{cases},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varepsilon(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{m=-\infty}^n\delta(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\begin{cases}0&n<0\\1&n\ge0\end{cases},}$$
$$\varepsilon(n)\,a^n,$$
$$\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi Fn}.$$

The unit impulse $\delta(n)$ is an ordinary sequence, not a distribution: it has the value 1 at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$. It is the identity element of discrete convolution, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(n)*\delta(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n)}$. A rectangle of $2M+1$ ones around $\textcolor{#e5383b}{n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ is $\varepsilon(n+M)-\varepsilon(n-M-1)$.

Linear and shift-invariant. A discrete-time system is called linear if the superposition principle holds, and shift-invariant if $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Tr}\{s(n-m)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{g(n-m)}$ for every integer $m$. A system with both properties is called an LSI system. With the impulse response $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Tr}\{\delta(n)\}}$ it follows, as in Part 1, that

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Tr}\Bigl\{\sum_ms(m)\delta(n-m)\Bigr\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ms(m)\operatorname{Tr}\{\delta(n-m)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ms(m)\,h(n-m).}$$

No limit process is needed here: a sequence already is a sum of weighted, shifted unit impulses.

Difference and sum. The counterpart of the derivative is the difference $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n)-s(n-1)}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\delta(n)-\delta(n-1)}$. The counterpart of the integral is the accumulation $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{m\le n}s(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n)*\varepsilon(n)}$. The two cancel each other: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{[\delta(n)-\delta(n-1)]*\varepsilon(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varepsilon(n)-\varepsilon(n-1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\delta(n)}$.

Difference equations, FIR and IIR. Instead of differential equations, difference equations describe discrete-time systems:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\underbrace{\sum_{q=0}^{Q}a_q\,s(n-q)}_{\text{FIR part}}+\underbrace{\sum_{p=1}^{P}b_p\,g(n-p)}_{\text{IIR part}}.}$$
(5.2)

The output is a weighted sum of past input and output values. If it depends only on input values ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{b_p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$), the impulse response ends after $Q+1$ values: an FIR filter (finite impulse response). With feedback ($b_p\neq0$) the impulse response can be infinitely long: an IIR filter (infinite impulse response). Both are built from delay elements (memory for one clock cycle), multipliers and adders (Fig. 4).

Exercise 4 ★ Discrete convolution by hand

Convolve $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(1,1,1)}$ with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(1,2)}$, both starting at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$. How long is the result, and why?

Solution

$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1\cdot2+1\cdot1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(3)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1\cdot2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$: $(1,3,3,2)$. Length $\textcolor{#e5383b}{3+2-1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4}$: the last nonzero value arises when the last value of $h$ meets the last value of $s$.

Stage 6: A recursive system

What this stage is about:

The simplest IIR system shows how feedback produces an infinitely long impulse response and when the system is stable.

The system $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n)+b\,g(n-1)}$ adds the last output, weighted by $b$, to the input. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\delta}$ and a system at rest before $\textcolor{#e5383b}{n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\delta(n)+b\,h(n-1)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1,\ h(1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{b,\ h(2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{b^2,\dots\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varepsilon(n)\,b^n.}$$
(6.1)

The impulse circulates in the feedback loop again and again and is multiplied by $b$ each time. The system is causal ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ for $n<0$). It is stable if $\sum_n|h(n)|<\infty$ (the discrete counterpart of Part 1), so here exactly for $|b|<1$, with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sum_n|b|^n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1-|b|}}$.

Response to a rectangular sequence. Input $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varepsilon(n)-\varepsilon(n-M)}$ ($M$ ones). With the convolution sum and the formula for the geometric sum $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sum_{k=0}^nq^k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1-q^{n+1}}{1-q}}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\begin{cases}0&n<0,\\[2pt]\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}b^{n-m}=\frac{1-b^{n+1}}{1-b}&0\le n<M,\\[8pt]\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}b^{n-m}=\frac{b^{n-M+1}-b^{n+1}}{1-b}&n\ge M.\end{cases}}$$
(6.2)

In the reference model ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{b}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.6}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4}$): $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1,\ 1.6,\ 1.96,\ 2.176}$, then $1.306,\ 0.783,\ 0.470,\dots$, each value 0.6 times the previous one (Fig. 5). This is the discrete counterpart of the response of the RC circuit to a rectangular pulse in Part 1.

Stage 7: The discrete-time Fourier transform

What this stage is about:

What spectrum does a sequence of numbers have? It is the spectrum of the ideally sampled signal, computed directly from the samples.

If you insert $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_\mathrm a(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ns(nT)\delta(t-nT)}$ into the Fourier integral, the sifting property leaves a sum. Normalized to $\textcolor{#e5383b}{T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$:

Theorem 7.1 (Discrete-time Fourier transform)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_\mathrm a(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}s(n)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi nf},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}S_\mathrm a(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi nf}\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(7.1)

⁠How to read it: $S_\mathrm a(f)$ is periodic with period 1, which is the sampling rate. One period, $|f|\le\frac12$, contains everything. This is why the inverse transform integrates only over an interval of length 1. The sum is a Fourier series in $f$, with the samples as coefficients.

Example. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varepsilon(n)\,b^n}$ with $|b|<1$ (geometric series):

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_\mathrm a(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigl(b\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}\bigr)^n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1-b\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{|S_\mathrm a(f)|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1+b^2-2b\cos(2\pi f)}.}$$
(7.2)

This is also the frequency response $H_\mathrm a(f)$ of the recursive system from Stage 6. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{b}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.6}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|H_\mathrm a|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2.5}$ at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, $1.60$ at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.1}$, $0.86$ at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.25}$, $0.625$ at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5}$ (Fig. 6). The system is a low-pass filter, but with a periodic frequency response: at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ it amplifies as strongly as at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ again.

Convolution and multiplication. As with functions, $s(n)*h(n)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_\mathrm a(f)H_\mathrm a(f)$. The product of two sequences gives the periodic convolution of the spectra over one period:

$$s(n)\cdot h(n)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}S_\mathrm a(\nu)\,H_\mathrm a(f-\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\nu.$$
(7.3)

Stage 8: The discrete Fourier transform (DFT)

What this stage is about:

$S_\mathrm a(f)$ is a continuous function, but a computer needs finitely many numbers. For a sequence of finite length, finitely many samples of the spectrum are enough.

The idea. If $s(n)$ is limited to $M$ values, then by Stage 4 samples of the spectrum spaced $\textcolor{#e5383b}{F}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1/M}$ apart are enough. They correspond to the signal $s_d(n)$, repeated periodically with period $M$. Because the spectrum is also periodic (time discrete), $M$ spectral values suffice. Both sides are now discrete and periodic.

Theorem 8.1 (Discrete Fourier transform)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_d(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}s_d(n)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi kn/M},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_d(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1M\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}S_d(k)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kn/M},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{k,n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0,\dots,M-1.}$$
(8.1)

⁠How to read it: $S_d(k)$ is the value of the spectrum $S_\mathrm a(f)$ at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{k/M}$. The index $k$ counts oscillations that fit exactly $k$ times into the $M$ values. The inverse transform is a Fourier series with $M$ terms. The theorems hold analogously, for example the shift $s_d(n-m)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_d(k)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi mk/M}$ (circular: what is shifted out at the end comes back in at the start) and Parseval $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sum_n|s_d(n)|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1M\sum_k|S_d(k)|^2}$.

Example. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_d}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(1,\,2,\,0,\,-1)}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi/4}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\mathrm{j}}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_d(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=0}^{3}s_d(n)(-\mathrm{j})^{kn}:}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_d(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2,\quad S_d(1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1-3\mathrm{j},\quad S_d(2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0,\quad S_d(3)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1+3\mathrm{j}.}$$

Check: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_d(3)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_d^*(1)}$, because the signal is real ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$ corresponds to $\textcolor{#e5383b}{k}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-1}$). Parseval: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{1+4+0+1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{6}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac14(4+10+0+10)}$.

Stage 9: FFT and fast convolution

What this stage is about:

The DFT needs $M^2$ multiplications. The FFT gets by with roughly $M\,\mathrm{lb}\,M$. This also makes convolution via the frequency domain fast.

The idea of the FFT. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{W_M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi/M}}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_d(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ns_d(n)W_M^{kn}}$. For even $M$, split into values with even and odd index:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_d(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{M/2-1}s_d(2n)\,W_{M/2}^{kn}}_{S_{d,1}(k)}+W_M^k\underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{M/2-1}s_d(2n+1)\,W_{M/2}^{kn}}_{S_{d,2}(k)},}$$
(9.1)

because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{W_M^{2kn}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{W_{M/2}^{kn}}$. Instead of one DFT of length $M$ you need two of length $M/2$ and $M$ multiplications by $W_M^k$, so $2(M/2)^2+M$ instead of $M^2$. Both partial DFTs are periodic with period $M/2$, so they also supply the values for $k\ge M/2$. If $M$ is a power of two, the splitting is repeated $\mathrm{lb}\,M$ times. The effort drops to about $M\,\mathrm{lb}\,M$. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1024}$ that is 10,240 instead of 1,048,576 multiplications, roughly a hundred times fewer. This is the fast Fourier transform (FFT).

Circular convolution. For periodic sequences with period $M$, the circular (periodic) convolution is

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g_d(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}s_d(m)\,h_d\bigl((n-m)\bmod M\bigr)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;G_d(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_d(k)\,H_d(k).}$$
(9.2)

It is the periodically repeated result of ordinary convolution. If $M$ is too small, the repetitions overlap. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(1,1,1)}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(1,2)}$ the ordinary convolution is $(1,3,3,2)$, with four values. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$ the last value falls onto the first: $(3,3,3)$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4}$ the result is correct: $(1,3,3,2)$. In general $M\ge M_1+M_2-1$ is required ($M_1$, $M_2$: lengths of the two sequences); to achieve this, the sequences are padded with zeros.

Fast convolution. Instead of evaluating the convolution sum directly, you transform both sequences with the FFT, multiply and transform back. A signal of arbitrary length is split into blocks; each block is convolved separately, and the results are added. This is allowed because convolution is distributive:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(n)*h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Bigl[\sum_is_i(n)\Bigr]*h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_i\bigl[s_i(n)*h(n)\bigr].}$$

Stage 10: Changing the sampling rate, decimation and interpolation

What this stage is about:

A sequence can afterwards be represented at a lower or higher rate. The same rules apply to the spectrum as for the original sampling.

Downsampling by the factor $c$. Only every $c$th value is kept. Conceptually, first multiply by a comb of unit impulses, then drop the zeros:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_c(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n)\sum_m\delta(n-mc),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_u(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(nc).}$$

Multiplication by the comb produces $c$ copies of the spectrum spaced $1/c$ apart, and dropping the zeros stretches the frequency axis by $c$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_{c,\mathrm a}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1c\sum_{k=0}^{c-1}S_\mathrm a\Bigl(f-\frac kc\Bigr),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_{u,\mathrm a}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_{c,\mathrm a}\Bigl(\frac fc\Bigr).}$$
(10.1)

If the spectrum extends up to $f_\mathrm g$ (normalized to the old rate), after downsampling it extends up to $cf_\mathrm g$. Aliasing is avoided only if $cf_\mathrm g\le\frac12$. Otherwise a digital low-pass filter must limit the band beforehand.

Upsampling by the factor $c$. Insert $c-1$ zeros between every two values: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_\uparrow(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n/c)}$ for $n$ divisible by $c$, otherwise 0. The spectrum is compressed by $c$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_{\uparrow,\mathrm a}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S_\mathrm a(cf)}$, and now contains $c-1$ spectral images within the period. A low-pass filter with the cutoff frequency $\frac{1}{2c}$ and the gain $c$ removes them. In doing so it fills the zeros with interpolated values (the si interpolation from Stage 3 in discrete form), and the original values are preserved. Downsampling and upsampling in succession change the rate by a rational factor $c_1/c_2$.

Exercise 10 ★★ Downsampling

A sequence has a spectrum up to $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.2}$.

  1. May it be downsampled by $\textcolor{#e5383b}{c}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$ without a prefilter? By $\textcolor{#e5383b}{c}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$?

  2. What must happen before downsampling by $\textcolor{#e5383b}{c}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$, and what is lost in the process?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{c}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$: afterwards the band extends up to $0.4\le0.5$, no aliasing. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{c}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$: up to $0.6>0.5$, aliasing.

(b) Limit the band beforehand to $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{1}{2c}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac16}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.167}$ with a digital low-pass filter. The components between 0.167 and 0.2 are lost in the process. That is the price of the lower rate.

Stage 11: The z-transform

What this stage is about:

Just as the Laplace transform extends the Fourier transform (Part 2), the z-transform extends the discrete-time Fourier transform: first weight exponentially, then transform.

The idea. Multiply the sequence by $\rho^{-n}$ ($\rho>0$) and transform: $\sum_ns(n)\rho^{-n}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi fn}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{z}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\rho\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}$ this becomes a power series in $z^{-1}$. Equivalently: $z^n$ is an eigenfunction of every LSI system, because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{z^{n-k}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{z^{-k}z^n}$, and the eigenvalue is $H(z)$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{z^n*h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_kh(k)z^{n-k}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{z^n\sum_kh(k)z^{-k}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H(z)\,z^n.}$$

Theorem 11.1 (z-transform)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}s(n)\,z^{-n},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\oint S(z)\,z^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d} z,}$$
(11.1)

where the contour integral runs counterclockwise around a circle $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|z|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\rho}$ in the region of convergence.

⁠How to read it: on the circle $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|z|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\rho}$ lies one period of the spectrum of the weighted sequence $s(n)\rho^{-n}$. On the unit circle $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|z|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, $S(z)$ is the ordinary spectrum $S_\mathrm a(f)$, provided the unit circle lies in the region of convergence. In this way the plane of the Laplace transform is mapped onto the z-plane: the imaginary axis becomes the unit circle, and the left half-plane becomes its interior.

Basic pairs and region of convergence. With the geometric series:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varepsilon(n)\,b^n\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{1}{1-bz^{-1}}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{z}{z-b},\quad|z|>|b|;}$$
$$-\varepsilon(-n-1)\,b^n\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{z}{z-b},\quad|z|<|b|.$$
(11.2)

As with the Laplace transform, the same expression belongs to two signals; only the region of convergence decides. For right-sided sequences it lies outside the circle through the outermost pole, for left-sided ones inside the circle through the innermost pole, and for two-sided ones it is a ring. For $b^{|n|}$ with $0<b<1$ this is the ring $b<|z|<1/b$.

Rules of calculation. Convolution becomes a product: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s*h\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;G(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S(z)H(z)}$. A delay by $n_0$ clock cycles becomes multiplication by $z^{-n_0}$. This turns the difference equation (5.2) into

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{G(z)}{S(z)}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\sum_{q=0}^{Q}a_q\,z^{-q}}{1-\sum_{p=1}^{P}b_p\,z^{-p}}.}$$
(11.3)

For the system from Exercise 6, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1+0.5z^{-1}}{1-0.6z^{-1}}}$: zero at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{z}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-0.5}$, pole at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{z}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.6}$.

Theorem 11.2 (Stability)

A causal LSI system is stable if and only if all poles of $H(z)$ lie inside the unit circle.

⁠Why: causal means a region of convergence outside the outermost pole. Stable means $\sum|h(n)|<\infty$; then $H(z)$ converges on the unit circle, so the unit circle belongs to the region of convergence. Both together are possible only if the outermost pole lies inside the unit circle. For the recursive system with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1-bz^{-1}}}$ this again means $|b|<1$.

Example. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5^n\cos(\pi n/3)\,\varepsilon(n)}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\cos}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\pi n/3}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\pi n/3})}$ and (11.2) for $\textcolor{#e5383b}{b}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{j}\pi/3}}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12\Bigl[\frac{1}{1-0.5\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\pi/3}z^{-1}}+\frac{1}{1-0.5\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\pi/3}z^{-1}}\Bigr]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1-0.25z^{-1}}{1-0.5z^{-1}+0.25z^{-2}},}$$
$$|z|>0.5.$$

Poles at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{0.5\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{j}\pi/3}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.25\pm0.433\mathrm{j}}$, zeros at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{z}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{z}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.25}$ (Fig. 8). The magnitude of the poles gives the decay per clock cycle (0.5), their angle gives the oscillation ($\pi/3$ per clock cycle, so six clock cycles per period).

Exercise 11 ★★ Applying the z-transform

  1. Confirm the numerator and denominator of $S(z)$ in the example by bringing the two fractions to a common denominator.

  2. Does the spectrum $S_\mathrm a(f)$ of the example signal exist? Justify your answer using the region of convergence.

Solution

(a) Common denominator $\textcolor{#e5383b}{(1-az^{-1})(1-a^*z^{-1})}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1-(a+a^*)z^{-1}+|a|^2z^{-2}}$ with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{a}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\pi/3}}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{a+a^*}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\cdot0.25}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|a|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.25}$. Numerator $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac12[2-(a+a^*)z^{-1}]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1-0.25z^{-1}}$.

(b) Yes. The region of convergence $|z|>0.5$ contains the unit circle.

Exercise 12 ★★ Causal or stable

$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\dfrac{1}{1-2z^{-1}}}$.

  1. Which impulse response belongs to the region of convergence $|z|>2$, and which to $|z|<2$?

  2. Which of the two is stable, and which is causal? Is there one that is both?

Solution

(a) $|z|>2$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2^n\varepsilon(n)}$. $|z|<2$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-2^n\varepsilon(-n-1)}$, so $-\frac12,-\frac14,\dots$ for $\textcolor{#e5383b}{n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-1,-2,\dots}$

(b) The first is causal but unstable (it grows). The second is stable ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{\sum_{n\le-1}2^n}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$) but anticausal. Both at once is impossible: the pole at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{z}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$ lies outside the unit circle, and then the region of convergence cannot both lie outside the pole and contain the unit circle.

Key points

  • Ideal sampling: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum s(nT)\delta(t-nT)}$; spectrum $\frac1T\sum_kS(f-k/T)$, periodic with $1/T$.

  • Sampling theorem: $f_\mathrm A\ge2f_\mathrm g$; reconstruction $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum s(nT)\operatorname{si}(\pi(t-nT)/T)}$; otherwise aliasing.

  • Discrete in one domain means periodic in the other.

  • Discrete convolution $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ms(m)h(n-m)}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Tr}\{\delta(n)\}}$; stable: $\sum|h(n)|<\infty$.

  • Recursive system $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(n)+bg(n-1)}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{b^n\varepsilon(n)}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1-b\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}}$.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_\mathrm a(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum s(n)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi nf}}$, period 1; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}S_\mathrm a\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi nf}\mathrm{d} f}$.

  • DFT: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S_d(k)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}s_d(n)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi kn/M}}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_d}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1M\sum S_d\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi kn/M}}$; FFT $\textcolor{#e5383b}{}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{ M\,\mathrm{lb}\,M}$; circular convolution $\leftrightarrow S_dH_d$.

  • Downsampling stretches the band (aliasing if $cf_\mathrm g>\frac12$), upsampling creates spectral images.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum s(n)z^{-n}}$; $z^{-n_0}$ = delay; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(z)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\sum a_qz^{-q}}{1-\sum b_pz^{-p}}}$; causal and stable: all poles in $|z|<1$.
