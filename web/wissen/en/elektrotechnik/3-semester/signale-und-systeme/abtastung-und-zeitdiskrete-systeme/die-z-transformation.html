What this stage is about:

Just as the Laplace transform extends the Fourier transform (Chapter 2), the z-transform extends the discrete-time Fourier transform: first weight exponentially, then transform.

The idea. Multiply the sequence by $\rho^{-n}$ ($\rho>0$) and transform: $\sum_ns(n)\rho^{-n}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi fn}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\rho\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}$ this becomes a power series in $z^{-1}$. Equivalently: $z^n$ is an eigenfunction of every LSI system, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^{n-k}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z^{-k}z^n}$, and the eigenvalue is $H(z)$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^n*h(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_kh(k)z^{n-k}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z^n\sum_kh(k)z^{-k}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{H(z)\,z^n.}$$

Theorem 11.1 (z-transform)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}s(n)\,z^{-n},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{j}}\oint S(z)\,z^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d} z,}$$
(11.1)

where the contour integral runs counterclockwise around a circle $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\rho}$ in the region of convergence.

⁠How to read it: on the circle $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\rho}$ lies one period of the spectrum of the weighted sequence $s(n)\rho^{-n}$. On the unit circle $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $S(z)$ is the ordinary spectrum $S_\mathrm a(f)$, provided the unit circle lies in the region of convergence. In this way the plane of the Laplace transform is mapped onto the z-plane: the imaginary axis becomes the unit circle, and the left half-plane becomes its interior.

Basic pairs and region of convergence. With the geometric series:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon(n)\,b^n\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{1}{1-bz^{-1}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{z}{z-b},\quad|z|>|b|;}$$
$$-\varepsilon(-n-1)\,b^n\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;\frac{z}{z-b},\quad|z|<|b|.$$
(11.2)

As with the Laplace transform, the same expression belongs to two signals; only the region of convergence decides. For right-sided sequences it lies outside the circle through the outermost pole, for left-sided ones inside the circle through the innermost pole, and for two-sided ones it is a ring. For $b^{|n|}$ with $0<b<1$ this is the ring $b<|z|<1/b$.

Rules of calculation. Convolution becomes a product: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s*h\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;G(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S(z)H(z)}$. A delay by $n_0$ clock cycles becomes multiplication by $z^{-n_0}$. This turns the difference equation (5.2) into

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{G(z)}{S(z)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\sum_{q=0}^{Q}a_q\,z^{-q}}{1-\sum_{p=1}^{P}b_p\,z^{-p}}.}$$
(11.3)

For the system from Exercise 6, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1+0.5z^{-1}}{1-0.6z^{-1}}}$: zero at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.5}$, pole at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6}$.

Theorem 11.2 (Stability)

A causal LSI system is stable if and only if all poles of $H(z)$ lie inside the unit circle.

⁠Why: causal means a region of convergence outside the outermost pole. Stable means $\sum|h(n)|<\infty$; then $H(z)$ converges on the unit circle, so the unit circle belongs to the region of convergence. Both together are possible only if the outermost pole lies inside the unit circle. For the recursive system with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{1-bz^{-1}}}$ this again means $|b|<1$.

Example

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5^n\cos(\pi n/3)\,\varepsilon(n)}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\cos}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\pi n/3}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\pi n/3})}$ and (11.2) for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{j}\pi/3}}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\Bigl[\frac{1}{1-0.5\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\pi/3}z^{-1}}+\frac{1}{1-0.5\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\pi/3}z^{-1}}\Bigr]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1-0.25z^{-1}}{1-0.5z^{-1}+0.25z^{-2}},}$$
$$|z|>0.5.$$

Poles at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.5\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{j}\pi/3}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25\pm0.433\mathrm{j}}$, zeros at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25}$ (Fig. 8). The magnitude of the poles gives the decay per clock cycle (0.5), their angle gives the oscillation ($\pi/3$ per clock cycle, so six clock cycles per period).

Exercise 11 ★★ Applying the z-transform

  1. Confirm the numerator and denominator of $S(z)$ in the example by bringing the two fractions to a common denominator.

  2. Does the spectrum $S_\mathrm a(f)$ of the example signal exist? Justify your answer using the region of convergence.

Solution

(a) Common denominator $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1-az^{-1})(1-a^*z^{-1})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-(a+a^*)z^{-1}+|a|^2z^{-2}}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\pi/3}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a+a^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot0.25}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|a|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25}$. Numerator $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac12[2-(a+a^*)z^{-1}]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-0.25z^{-1}}$.

(b) Yes. The region of convergence $|z|>0.5$ contains the unit circle.

Exercise 12 ★★ Causal or stable

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{1}{1-2z^{-1}}}$.

  1. Which impulse response belongs to the region of convergence $|z|>2$, and which to $|z|<2$?

  2. Which of the two is stable, and which is causal? Is there one that is both?

Solution

(a) $|z|>2$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2^n\varepsilon(n)}$. $|z|<2$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2^n\varepsilon(-n-1)}$, so $-\frac12,-\frac14,\dots$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1,-2,\dots}$

(b) The first is causal but unstable (it grows). The second is stable ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{n\le-1}2^n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$) but anticausal. Both at once is impossible: the pole at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ lies outside the unit circle, and then the region of convergence cannot both lie outside the pole and contain the unit circle.
