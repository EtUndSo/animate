What this stage is about:

When can the original be recovered exactly from the samples, and how?

Theorem 3.1 (Sampling theorem)

A signal whose spectrum vanishes above $f_\mathrm g$ (low-pass (band-limited) signal) is uniquely determined by its samples $s(nT)$ if

$$T\le\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}$$
$$\text{that is}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1T\ge2f_\mathrm g.}$$
(3.1)

With the ideal low-pass filter $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H_\mathrm{TP}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{T\operatorname{rect}\bigl(\frac{f}{2f_\mathrm g}\bigr)}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_\mathrm a(t)*\bigl[2f_\mathrm gT\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)\bigr],}$$
(3.2)

and for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/(2f_\mathrm g)}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}s(nT)\,\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\pi\frac{t-nT}{T}\Bigr).}$$
(3.3)

⁠How to read it: for $f_\mathrm A\ge2f_\mathrm g$ the copies in Fig. 1 lie apart. A low-pass filter cuts out the copy at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and it has exactly the shape of the original. In the time domain this means: each sample is replaced by an si function that is 1 at its own sampling point and zero at all the others (Fig. 2). Their sum hits all the samples and fills the gaps in such a way that no frequency above $f_\mathrm g$ arises. The minimum rate $2f_\mathrm g$ is called the Nyquist rate.

Derivation: Reconstruction

For $|f|\le f_\mathrm g$ only the copy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is present, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_\mathrm a(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1TS(f)}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H_\mathrm{TP}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{T\operatorname{rect}(f/2f_\mathrm g)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_\mathrm a\cdot H_\mathrm{TP}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S}$. In the time domain: $T\operatorname{rect}(f/2f_\mathrm g)\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;2f_\mathrm gT\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)$ (Chapter 3, rectangle and si). With (1.2) and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/T}$ this gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_\mathrm a*\operatorname{si}(\pi t/T)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_ns(nT)\operatorname{si}(\pi(t-nT)/T)}$.

Aliasing. If the sampling is too slow, the copies overlap. A frequency above $f_\mathrm A/2$ appears after reconstruction as a false, lower frequency, its alias. In the reference model, a cosine at 700 Hz and one at 300 Hz have exactly the same samples at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ kHz, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\cos(2\pi\cdot0.7n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\cos(2\pi n-2\pi\cdot0.3n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\cos(2\pi\cdot0.3n)}$ (Fig. 3). Afterwards the two can no longer be separated. This is why the signal is filtered with a low-pass filter before every sampler (anti-aliasing filter).

QuestionIsn't exactly $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2f_\mathrm g}$ enough? For a sine at exactly $f_\mathrm g$ it goes wrong, though.

Yes, exactly at the limit it goes wrong: with an unfavorable phase, a sine with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_\mathrm A/2}$ can be sampled at a zero crossing every time, and all samples are zero. Strictly speaking, the theorem therefore requires that the spectrum has no line at $f_\mathrm g$, or that $f_\mathrm A>2f_\mathrm g$. In practice one samples faster anyway, because real low-pass filters do not drop off vertically and need room for their transition band.

A second source with PULSE(0 1 0 10n 10n 1u 1m) drives the control input of the switch: the switch closes every millisecond for 1 µ s ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ kHz), and in between the capacitor holds the sample. After sh comes an e source with output out as the reconstruction low-pass. It is a 4th-order Butterworth low-pass (passband as flat as possible) with cut-off frequency 400 Hz and has the value

Laplace=1/(((s/wg)**2+0.7654*s/wg+1)*((s/wg)**2+1.8478*s/wg+1))

⁠Directives: .model SW1 SW(Ron=1 Roff=1G Vt=0.5), .param wg=2*pi*400, .tran 0 40m 0 1u, .options plotwinsize=0, .four 100 15 V(sh) V(out).

⁠What you see: The staircase at sh follows a 300-Hz oscillation, and at out there is a cosine at 300 Hz instead of 700 Hz. In .four the line at 300 Hz is the strongest. With SINE(0 1 300 0 0 90) the same staircase appears. Set 1.2 kHz and compare with your result from Exercise 1(a).

⁠Anti-aliasing: Put a second, identical e source in front of the switch. At 700 Hz the 300-Hz line at out drops to about one tenth, while a 300-Hz input passes almost unattenuated. So the alias has to be prevented before sampling: afterwards it can no longer be told apart from a genuine 300-Hz signal.

Exercise 1 ★ Alias frequencies

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ kHz.

  1. At what frequency does a cosine at 1.2 kHz appear after reconstruction?

  2. Which frequencies give the same samples as a cosine at 50 Hz?

Solution

(a) 1.2 kHz lies above $f_\mathrm A/2$. It has the same samples as $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1.2-1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.2}$ kHz and appears as 200 Hz.

(b) All $\pm50\,\mathrm{Hz}+k\cdot1\,\mathrm{kHz}$: 950 Hz, 1050 Hz, 1950 Hz, 2050 Hz and so on. For the cosine, negative and positive frequency count equally, which is why $1000-50$ belongs to them as well.

Exercise 2 ★★ Sampling rates in practice

  1. Telephone speech is limited to 3.4 kHz and sampled at 8 kHz. What is the Nyquist rate, and what is the margin for?

  2. An audio CD samples at 44.1 kHz. Up to what frequency can it store sounds, and how wide is the transition band for the anti-aliasing filter if the audible band extends to 20 kHz?

Solution

(a) Nyquist rate $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot3.4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.8}$ kHz. With 8 kHz, $f_\mathrm A/2$ lies at 4 kHz. Between 3.4 and 4 kHz the anti-aliasing filter has room to make the transition from passband to stopband.

(b) Up to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_\mathrm A/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{22.05}$ kHz. The transition band from 20 to 22.05 kHz is only 2.05 kHz wide. This is why steep filters or oversampling are needed.

Exercise 3 ★★ si interpolation by hand

Use (3.3) to compute the value of the signal from Fig. 2 at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.5T}$. Hint: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{si}(\pi\cdot0.5)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2/\pi}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{si}(\pi\cdot1.5)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2/(3\pi)}$. Which samples contribute nothing?

Solution

At $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.5T}$, $\operatorname{si}(\pi(2.5-k))$ equals $\frac2\pi$ for $k\in\{2,3\}$ and $-\frac{2}{3\pi}$ for $k\in\{1,4\}$. The values at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ are zero and contribute nothing:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(2.5T)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac2\pi\Bigl[-\tfrac13\cdot1+0.6-0.4-\tfrac13\cdot0.8\Bigr]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac2\pi\cdot(-0.4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.255.}$$
