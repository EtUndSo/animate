What this stage is about:

The Fourier series decomposes a signal into oscillations. The same works with any system of orthogonal functions. This stage shows how to find the coefficients and why they give the best approximation.

The idea. A vector in space has the components $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\vec f\cdot\vec e_i}$ with respect to perpendicular unit vectors $\vec e_i$. For signals on an interval $[0,T]$, the integral $\int_0^Tf(t)g(t)\mathrm{d} t$ plays the role of the scalar product. Two signals are called orthogonal if it is zero (Chapter 6). A system of $M$ functions $s_i(t)$ is called orthonormal if

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_0^Ts_i(t)\,s_j(t)\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{cases}1&i=j\\0&i\neq j\end{cases}.}$$
(1.1)

An energy signal $f(t)$ is approximated by $\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}b_is_i(t)$, and a residual $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(t)-\sum b_is_i(t)}$ remains.

Theorem 1.1 (Orthogonal expansion)

The energy of the residual, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_0^Tr^2\mathrm{d} t}$, is smallest for

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_0^Tf(t)\,s_i(t)\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$
(1.2)

Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{E_f-\sum_ib_i^2}$. If $E_r\to0$ as $M$ grows (complete system), the generalized Parseval theorem $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_ib_i^2}$ holds.

Derivation: Coefficients and residual energy

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_0^T\bigl(f-\sum_ib_is_i\bigr)^2\mathrm{d} t}$. Differentiate with respect to $b_j$ and set to zero:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial E_r}{\partial b_j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2\int_0^T\Bigl(f-\sum_ib_is_i\Bigr)s_j\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\int_0^Tfs_j\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_ib_i\int_0^Ts_is_j\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b_j.}$$

Orthonormality leaves only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{j}$ in the sum. If these $b_i$ are substituted and the square is multiplied out, the mixed terms drop out, again because of orthonormality: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{E_f-2\sum b_i^2+\sum b_i^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{E_f-\sum b_i^2}$.

⁠How to read it: Each coefficient is determined on its own, independently of the others. If you add a basis function, the previous coefficients do not change. This is the advantage of orthogonal systems, just as with the Fourier series (Chapter 3). $b_i$ is the cross-correlation $\varphi^E_{fs_i}(0)$. That is why the coefficients can be measured with a bank of correlation filters $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_i(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_i(T-t)}$ whose outputs are sampled at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{T}$ (Chapter 7).

Example

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t^2}$ on $[0,1]$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac15}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt3(2t-1)}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_0^1t^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt3\int_0^1t^2(2t-1)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.289,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum b_i^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac19+\tfrac1{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac{7}{36}.}$$

The approximation is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac13+\frac{\sqrt3}{6}\sqrt3(2t-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t-\frac16}$, the best straight line in the sense of energy. The residual energy is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac15-\frac7{36}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{180}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0056}$, that is, 2.8 % of $E_f$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt5(6t^2-6t+1)}$, the coefficient $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\sqrt5}{30}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.075}$ is added, and the residual becomes zero, because $t^2$ is itself a polynomial of degree two.

Exercise 1 ★ Checking orthonormality

Show that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt3(2t-1)}$ are orthonormal on $[0,1]$. Why does $s_1$ need the factor $\sqrt3$?

Solution

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_0^11\cdot\sqrt3(2t-1)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt3\,[t^2-t]_0^1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: orthogonal. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_0^11^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_0^13(2t-1)^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\cdot\bigl[\frac{(2t-1)^3}{6}\bigr]_0^1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\cdot\frac{1+1}{6}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. Without the factor, the energy of $2t-1$ would be only $\frac13$. The factor $\sqrt3$ normalizes it to 1; otherwise $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int fs_1\mathrm{d} t}$ would not be the correct coefficient.

Exercise 2 ★★ Best approximation

  1. Recompute $b_1$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t^2}$ and confirm the approximation $t-\frac16$.

  2. Check $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{180}}$ by computing $\int_0^1\bigl(t^2-t+\frac16\bigr)^2\mathrm{d} t$ directly.

  3. Find the coefficients of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt2\cos(2\pi t)}$ with respect to $s_0$ and $s_1$. What does the result mean?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_0^1t^2(2t-1)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac24-\frac13}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac16}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\sqrt3}{6}}$. Approximation: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac13+\frac{\sqrt3}{6}\cdot\sqrt3(2t-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13+t-\frac12}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t-\frac16}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_0^1\bigl(t^2-t+\tfrac16\bigr)^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_0^1\bigl(t^4-2t^3+\tfrac43t^2-\tfrac13t+\tfrac1{36}\bigr)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac15-\tfrac12+\tfrac49-\tfrac16+\tfrac1{36}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac{1}{180}}$.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt2\int_0^1\cos(2\pi t)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt6\int_0^1(2t-1)\cos(2\pi t)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ (the cosine is symmetric about $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$, and $2t-1$ is antisymmetric). Both coefficients are zero: the function is orthogonal to both basis functions, the approximation is zero, and the entire signal content remains in the residual. A basis is suitable only for signals that resemble its functions.

Summary

Terms
  • orthogonal – Two signals are called orthogonal if it is zero (Chapter 6).
  • orthonormal – A system of $M$ functions $s_i(t)$ is called orthonormal if …
  • generalized Parseval theorem – If $E_r\to0$ as $M$ grows (complete system), the generalized Parseval theorem $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_ib_i^2}$ holds.
