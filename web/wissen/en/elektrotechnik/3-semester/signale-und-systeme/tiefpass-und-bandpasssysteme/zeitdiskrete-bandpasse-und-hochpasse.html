What this stage is about:

Digital bandpass and highpass filters are obtained from a lowpass by modulation.

If the discrete-time lowpass has a real impulse response, multiplication by $2\cos(2\pi f_0n)$ shifts its passband to $\pm f_0$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H_{\mathrm{BP}}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{H_{\mathrm{TP}}(f)*\bigl[\delta(f+f_0)+\delta(f-f_0)\bigr],}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_{\mathrm{BP}}(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cos(2\pi f_0n)\,h_{\mathrm{TP}}(n),}$$
(12.1)

valid as long as the shifted bands do not overlap: $f_{\mathrm g,\mathrm{TP}}\le f_0\le\frac12-f_{\mathrm g,\mathrm{TP}}$. Because of the periodicity, the band at $f_0$ must touch neither the band at $-f_0$ nor its copy at $1-f_0$. In general, also for complex lowpass impulse responses, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_{\mathrm{BP}}(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\operatorname{Re}\{h_{\mathrm{TP}}(n)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0n}\}}$ holds as in (8.1). The factor 2 corresponds to the doubled equivalent lowpass $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2H_{\mathrm{TP}}}$.

A highpass is a lowpass shifted to half the sampling rate, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$. There the band and its mirror image coincide, so it is shifted only once: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H_{\mathrm{HP}}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{H_{\mathrm{TP}}(f-\frac12)}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\pi n}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-1)^n}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_{\mathrm{HP}}(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-1)^n\,h_{\mathrm{TP}}(n).}$$
(12.2)

⁠In short: a digital highpass is obtained from a lowpass by flipping every other sign.

Exercise 11 ★ Highpass from the moving average

The moving average $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_{\mathrm{TP}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13(1,1,1)}$ (Chapter 4) is a simple lowpass.

  1. Form $h_{\mathrm{HP}}$ according to (12.2).

  2. Compute $H_{\mathrm{TP}}$ and $H_{\mathrm{HP}}$ at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$. Is this consistent with a highpass?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_{\mathrm{HP}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13(1,-1,1)}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{H_{\mathrm{TP}}(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}[1+2\cos(2\pi f)]}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|H_{\mathrm{TP}}(0)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|H_{\mathrm{TP}}(\frac12)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13}$. The highpass is shifted by $\frac12$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|H_{\mathrm{HP}}(0)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|H_{\mathrm{HP}}(\frac12)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. It passes the fastest sequence $(+1,-1,+1,\dots)$ in full and attenuates the DC component to one third. A simple highpass, not a very steep one.
