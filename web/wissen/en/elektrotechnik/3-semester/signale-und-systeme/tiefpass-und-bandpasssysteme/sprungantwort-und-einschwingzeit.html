Stage 3: Step response and rise time

What this stage is about:

How does the ideal lowpass respond to a step? How fast does it rise, and how far does it overshoot?

The step response is the running integral of the impulse response (Chapter 1). With the sine integral

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Si}(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_0^x\operatorname{si}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Si}(-x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\operatorname{Si}(x),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Si}(\infty)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac\pi2}$$
(3.1)

this gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_\varepsilon(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-\infty}^t2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm g\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt).}$$
(3.2)
Derivation: Step response

Split the integral up to $t$ at 0. The part from $-\infty$ to 0 is half the area of $h$, that is, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac12H(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$, because $h$ is even. For the rest, substitute $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\xi}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\pi f_\mathrm g\tau}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{d}\xi/(2\pi f_\mathrm g)}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_0^t2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm g\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)}$. The value $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Si}(\infty)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac\pi2}$ follows from the area theorem: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_{-\infty}^\infty\operatorname{si}(\pi u)\mathrm{d} u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\int_0^\infty\operatorname{si}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac\pi2}$.

⁠How to read it: at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ the response is exactly $\frac12$; it begins even earlier (not causal) and tends to 1. The rise time $t_e$ is defined as the duration of the rise of a ramp with the maximum slope of the step response. The maximum slope is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2f_\mathrm g}$, so

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t_e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{h_\varepsilon(\infty)}{\max\dot h_\varepsilon}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}.}$$
(3.3)

The step response overshoots by $8.95\,\%$ (at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}}$). This is the Gibbs phenomenon from Chapter 3: a larger $f_\mathrm g$ shortens the rise time, but the overshoot stays the same (Fig. 1).

QuestionSurely a lowpass with more bandwidth is “better”. So why doesn't the overshoot shrink?

Because it comes from the shape of the frequency response, not its width. The overshoot arises at the vertical edge at $f_\mathrm g$: all frequencies up to there pass in full, all above not at all. If you move the edge, it stays vertical, and the step response is merely compressed in time. The overshoot only gets smaller once the edge becomes soft (Stage 5).

Exercise 3 ★★ Sine integral

  1. Show $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_\varepsilon(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_\varepsilon(\infty)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ using (3.1).

  2. Where is the maximum of the step response? Hint: the derivative of the step response is the impulse response.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_\varepsilon(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_\varepsilon(\infty)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\frac1\pi\cdot\frac\pi2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$.

(b) The slope of the step response is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)}$. It becomes zero for the first time at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\pi f_\mathrm gt}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pi}$ and changes there from positive to negative. So the maximum lies at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}}$ and equals $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac12+\frac{\operatorname{Si}(\pi)}{\pi}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\frac{1.852}{\pi}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.0895}$.
