What this is about

Almost every transmission is meant to deliver a signal as unchanged as possible, but only within a certain frequency range: a lowpass passes the low frequencies, a bandpass passes a band around a carrier frequency. This article asks: What exactly does “unchanged” mean, what does a sharply limited band cost, and how do you calculate with signals that ride on a high-frequency carrier?

The answer in three sentences (by the end of the article they should feel obvious)

  1. Undistorted means: only amplified and delayed. This requires a constant magnitude and a phase that falls linearly with frequency.

  2. A sharply band-limited (ideal) lowpass is not causal, overshoots by about 9 % at steps and needs a rise time of $1/(2f_\mathrm g)$. Softer edges reduce the overshoot at the expense of sharpness.

  3. A bandpass signal is a lowpass signal (the complex envelope) on a carrier. You calculate with the envelope as with a lowpass signal: it is delayed by the group delay, the carrier by the phase delay.

Stage 1: The distortionless system

What this stage is about:

What exactly should a “good” transmission achieve? And with which characteristic quantities do you measure how far a system deviates from it?

The idea. A signal has arrived undistorted if it is only louder or quieter and later, but its shape is the same:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{h_0\,s(t-t_0)}$$
$$\Longleftrightarrow$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{h_0\,\delta(t-t_0),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{h_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi t_0f}.}$$
(1.1)

In the spectrum this means: every oscillation is multiplied by the same factor $h_0$, and every one is shifted by the same time $t_0$. At the frequency $f$, a time shift $t_0$ is the angle $2\pi ft_0$. That is why the phase $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-2\pi t_0f}$ must be a straight line through the origin.

Definition 1.1 (Characteristic quantities of an LTI system)
$$\begin{aligned} &\text{Attenuation:} && a(f)=-20\lg|H(f)|\ \text{dB}\quad\text{or}\quad a(f)=-\ln|H(f)|\ \text{Np},\\ &\text{Phase attenuation angle:} && b(f)=-\varphi(f),\\ &\text{Phase delay:} && t_\mathrm p(f)=-\frac{\varphi(f)}{2\pi f},\\ &\text{Group delay:} && t_\mathrm g(f)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi(f)}{\mathrm{d} f}. \end{aligned}$$
(1.2)(1.3)(1.4)(1.5)

⁠How to read it: the phase delay is the delay of a single oscillation of frequency $f$. The group delay is the delay of a group of neighboring frequencies, that is, of an envelope (Stage 11). For the distortionless system, $a$, $t_\mathrm p$ and $t_\mathrm g$ are constant and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{t_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{t_0}$.

Example: the RC circuit. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\arctan(2\pi f\,RC)}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{RC}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ s:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\arctan(2\pi f\,RC)}{2\pi f},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{RC}{1+(2\pi f\,RC)^2}.}$$

For $f\to0$ both are $\textcolor{#e5383b}{RC}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ s. At $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.159}$ Hz ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{2\pi fRC}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$), $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.785}$ s, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5}$ s and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{a}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3.0}$ dB. So the RC circuit is not distortionless: its attenuation depends on frequency, and different frequencies arrive at different times.

Stage 2: The ideal lowpass

What this stage is about:

The ideal lowpass passes all frequencies up to $f_\mathrm g$ unchanged and blocks all frequencies above. This stage shows what this sharp cutoff means in the time domain.

Theorem 2.1 (Ideal lowpass)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{f}{2f_\mathrm g}\Bigr)}$$
$$\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt).}$$
(2.1)

⁠How to read it: the impulse response extends to infinity in both directions. So the ideal lowpass is not causal: it would have to respond to an impulse before the impulse arrives. It is a mathematical limiting object that can only be approximated (Stages 4, 6).

Duration and bandwidth. The signal duration $t_m$ of an impulse response is taken to be the width of a rectangle with the same height $h_{\max}$ and the same area:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_m}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{h_{\max}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(t)\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{H(0)}{h_{\max}}.}$$
(2.2)

For the ideal lowpass, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_{\max}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2f_\mathrm g}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_m}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g\cdot t_m}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$. In general, lowpass filters satisfy $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g\cdot t_m}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\text{const.}}$: double the bandwidth means half the response duration. This is the similarity theorem from Part 3.

Overshoot. For the si function, the ratio of the side maximum with the largest magnitude, $a_1$, to the main maximum $a_0$ is

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\ddot u}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Bigl|\frac{a_1}{a_0}\Bigr|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{21.7\,\%,}$$

independent of $f_\mathrm g$ (the first side minimum of $\operatorname{si}(\pi u)$ lies at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{u}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1.43}$ and is $-0.217$). Changing $f_\mathrm g$ only compresses the curve; it does not change its shape.

Stage 3: Step response and rise time

What this stage is about:

How does the ideal lowpass respond to a step? How fast does it rise, and how far does it overshoot?

The step response is the running integral of the impulse response (Part 1). With the sine integral

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Si}(x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_0^x\operatorname{si}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Si}(-x)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\operatorname{Si}(x),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Si}(\infty)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac\pi2}$$
(3.1)

this gives

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_\varepsilon(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^t2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm g\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12+\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt).}$$
(3.2)
Derivation: Step response

Split the integral up to $t$ at 0. The part from $-\infty$ to 0 is half the area of $h$, that is, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac12H(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$, because $h$ is even. For the rest, substitute $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\xi}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\pi f_\mathrm g\tau}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\mathrm{d}\xi/(2\pi f_\mathrm g)}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_0^t2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm g\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)}$. The value $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Si}(\infty)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac\pi2}$ follows from the area theorem: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_{-\infty}^\infty\operatorname{si}(\pi u)\mathrm{d} u}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_0^\infty\operatorname{si}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac\pi2}$.

⁠How to read it: at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ the response is exactly $\frac12$; it begins even earlier (not causal) and tends to 1. The rise time $t_e$ is defined as the duration of the rise of a ramp with the maximum slope of the step response. The maximum slope is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2f_\mathrm g}$, so

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_e}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{h_\varepsilon(\infty)}{\max\dot h_\varepsilon}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}.}$$
(3.3)

The step response overshoots by $8.95\,\%$ (at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}}$). This is the Gibbs phenomenon from Part 3: a larger $f_\mathrm g$ shortens the rise time, but the overshoot stays the same (Fig. 1).

QuestionSurely a lowpass with more bandwidth is “better”. So why doesn't the overshoot shrink?

Because it comes from the shape of the frequency response, not its width. The overshoot arises at the vertical edge at $f_\mathrm g$: all frequencies up to there pass in full, all above not at all. If you move the edge, it stays vertical, and the step response is merely compressed in time. The overshoot only gets smaller once the edge becomes soft (Stage 5).

Stage 4: Causal approximation

What this stage is about:

How do you build something that comes as close as possible to the ideal lowpass? You delay the impulse response and cut off the rest.

The idea. Shift $h(t)$ to the right by $t_0$ until the part at $t<0$ is negligible, and cut that part off. The envelope of the si function falls off like $1/(2\pi f_\mathrm g|t|)$. If it is to stay below 1 % of the maximum before $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, you need $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_0}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{16/f_\mathrm g}$. With a symmetric window of width $2t_0$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_k(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}\bigl(2\pi f_\mathrm g(t-t_0)\bigr)\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{t-t_0}{2t_0}\Bigr)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;H_k(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Bigl[\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{f}{2f_\mathrm g}\Bigr)*2t_0\operatorname{si}(2\pi t_0f)\Bigr]\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi t_0f}.}$$
(4.1)

⁠How to read it: cutting off is a multiplication by a window, and so in the spectrum a convolution with the window's si spectrum. The sharp edge is smeared out and acquires ripples. The factor $\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi t_0f}$ is the delay: the causal filter has a linear phase; it delays all frequencies equally (Fig. 2). The larger $t_0$, the narrower the transition, but the later the response.

Exercise 4 ★★ How much delay does causality cost?

A lowpass with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ kHz is to be approximated causally. The shifted ideal lowpass response is to stay below 1 % of its maximum before $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, that is, in the part that is cut off.

  1. What is the minimum value of $t_0$?

  2. What does this mean for a telephone call through a chain of ten such filters?

Solution

(a) The envelope condition $\frac{1}{2\pi f_\mathrm g|t|}<0.01$ requires $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|t|>\frac{100}{2\pi f_\mathrm g}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{15.9}$ ms. So $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_0}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{16}$ ms.

(b) Ten filters delay by about 0.16 s. In a conversation this is noticeable as a delay. The causality of a steep filter costs time, and the narrower the band, the more time it costs.

Stage 5: Echo method and pulse-shaping filters

What this stage is about:

How do you design a lowpass with less overshoot? You assemble the impulse response from shifted si functions and choose their weights.

Echo method. By the sampling theorem, every impulse response of a lowpass up to $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2T}}$ is an si series:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_nh(nT)\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\pi\frac{t-nT}{T}\Bigr)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\operatorname{rect}(Tf)\sum_nh(nT)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi nTf},}$$
(5.1)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(nT)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-f_\mathrm g}^{f_\mathrm g}H(f)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi nTf}\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(5.2)

Each sample $h(nT)$ is an echo: an si function shifted by $nT$. In the spectrum this becomes a Fourier series in $f$. If $h$ is real and even, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\operatorname{rect}(Tf)\bigl[h(0)+2\sum_{n\ge1}h(nT)\cos(2\pi nTf)\bigr]}$.

Pulse-shaping filter. Two echoes with the amplitude $a$ at a distance of $\pm T$ are added to the main component $\operatorname{si}(\pi t/T)$. For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{a}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$ the side oscillations largely cancel, and the spectrum gets a cosine-shaped, soft edge:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\frac{\pi t}{T}\Bigr)+\tfrac12\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\pi\frac{t+T}{T}\Bigr)+\tfrac12\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\pi\frac{t-T}{T}\Bigr)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\operatorname{rect}(Tf)\bigl[1+\cos(2\pi Tf)\bigr].}$$
(5.3)

This is the raised-cosine filter (cosine roll-off filter). Its overshoot is 2.7 % instead of 21.7 %; in exchange, the main lobe is twice as wide (first zero at $2T$ instead of $T$). Figure 3 shows both impulse responses normalized to the same area.

Transversal filter (tapped delay line). A sum of delayed, weighted copies is built with a chain of delay elements whose taps are weighted and added. So that no element has to reach into the future, everything is delayed together. This is the same structure as an FIR filter (Part 4).

Stage 6: Discrete-time lowpass filters, window design

What this stage is about:

How do you design a digital lowpass? Exactly like the causal analog one: take the ideal lowpass, delay it, truncate it with a window.

The ideal discrete-time lowpass has the periodically repeated rectangular frequency response and the impulse response

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gn),}$$

so in the reference model ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.2}$) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.4}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(\pm1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.303}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(\pm2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.094}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(\pm3)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-0.062}$. It, too, is not causal. You multiply it by a window $w(n)$ of $2N+1$ values, shift it by $N$ and obtain an FIR filter with linear phase. The window determines the result (Fig. 4):

  • ⁠Rectangular window: sharpest transition, but high side lobes. In the reference model ($\textcolor{#e5383b}{N}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{10}$), the stopband from $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.3}$ on still reaches $-26.6$ dB, and the passband fluctuates by about $\pm0.5$ dB.

  • ⁠Hann window: softer transition, but a much deeper stopband: from $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.3}$ on at most $-45.9$ dB, passband flat to within $0.05$ dB.

The reason is the same as for the short-time Fourier transform in Part 3: the spectrum of the window is convolved with the ideal, and the hard edges of the rectangle produce slowly decaying side lobes.

Stage 7: The ideal bandpass and its equivalent lowpass

What this stage is about:

A bandpass passes a band around the center frequency $f_0$. This stage shows that it can be described completely by a lowpass and the frequency $f_0$.

Ideal bandpass. Two rectangles of width $\Delta f$ at $\pm f_0$ ($f_0>\Delta f/2$):

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{rect}\Bigl(\frac{f}{\Delta f}\Bigr)*\bigl[\delta(f+f_0)+\delta(f-f_0)\bigr]\;\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ\;h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Delta f\operatorname{si}(\pi\Delta f\,t)\cdot2\cos(2\pi f_0t).}$$
(7.1)

The impulse response is an oscillation at $f_0$ whose envelope is the impulse response of a lowpass with the bandwidth $\Delta f/2$. The modulation rule from Part 3 explains this: it is a lowpass whose spectrum is shifted to $\pm f_0$.

Equivalent lowpass. For a real impulse response, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(-f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H^*(f)}$; the negative half contains nothing new. Keep only the positive frequencies, double them and shift them to the left by a carrier frequency $f_0$. The result is the equivalent lowpass $H_T(f)$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12H_T(f-f_0)+\tfrac12H_T^*(-f-f_0),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_T(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0\ \text{for}\ f\le-f_0.}$$
(7.2)

$H_T$ need not be symmetric about $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, so its impulse response $h_T(t)$ can be complex. If there is an $f_0$ for which $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_T(-f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H_T^*(f)}$ holds (so that $h_T$ is real), the bandpass is called symmetric. For the ideal bandpass with $f_0$ at the center, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_T(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\operatorname{rect}(f/\Delta f)}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_T(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\Delta f\operatorname{si}(\pi\Delta f\,t)}$, real and even. This is a special case: even symmetric bandpass filters generally have an imaginary part in the spectrum.

Stage 8: Complex envelope and quadrature components

What this stage is about:

What holds for systems also holds for signals: a bandpass signal is a lowpass signal on a carrier. This stage shows the different ways of writing this down.

Transforming (7.2) back with the modulation rule gives $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12h_T(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}+\frac12\bigl[h_T(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}\bigr]^*}$, and so, because $\textcolor{#e5383b}{z+z^*}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\operatorname{Re} z}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{h_T(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}\bigr\},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{s_T(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}\bigr\}.}$$
(8.1)

$s_T(t)$ is called the complex envelope, $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}$ the complex carrier. Half their product is the analytic component from Part 3, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_+(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12s_T(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2s_+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}}$.

Theorem 8.1 (Quadrature representation)

With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_{Tr}+\mathrm{j} s_{Ti}}$ and the Hilbert transform $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\hat s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s*\frac{1}{\pi t}}$:

$$\begin{aligned} s(t)&=s_{Tr}(t)\cos(2\pi f_0t)-s_{Ti}(t)\sin(2\pi f_0t),\\ s_{Tr}(t)&=s(t)\cos(2\pi f_0t)+\hat s(t)\sin(2\pi f_0t),\qquad s_{Ti}(t)=\hat s(t)\cos(2\pi f_0t)-s(t)\sin(2\pi f_0t),\\ s(t)&=|s_T(t)|\cos\bigl(2\pi f_0t+\varphi_T(t)\bigr),\qquad|s_T|=\sqrt{s_{Tr}^2+s_{Ti}^2}. \end{aligned}$$
(8.2)(8.3)(8.4)

⁠How to read it: $s_{Tr}$ and $s_{Ti}$ are called quadrature components (the in-phase and the quadrature component): one modulates the cosine of the carrier, the other its sine. $|s_T|$ is the envelope, which you see on an oscilloscope as the “outline” of the signal, and $\varphi_T$ is the phase deviation of the carrier. If the complex envelope is real (symmetric case), $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_T(t)\cos(2\pi f_0t)}$: an amplitude-modulated cosine.

Derivation: Quadrature components

(8.2): $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Re}\{(s_{Tr}+\mathrm{j} s_{Ti})(\cos+\mathrm{j}\sin)\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_{Tr}\cos-s_{Ti}\sin}$. For (8.3): $\textcolor{#e5383b}{2s_+}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s+\mathrm{j}\hat s}$ (Part 3), so

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(s+\mathrm{j}\hat s)(\cos-\mathrm{j}\sin)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(s\cos+\hat s\sin)+\mathrm{j}(\hat s\cos-s\sin).}$$

Stage 9: Bandpass signal through a bandpass system

What this stage is about:

What the envelopes are good for: how a bandpass acts on a bandpass signal can be calculated entirely in the lowpass domain.

Theorem 9.1 (Transmission in the equivalent lowpass domain)

If $S_T$ and $H_T$ both satisfy (7.2) (nothing below $-f_0$), then the envelope of the output satisfies

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{G_T(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12S_T(f)\,H_T(f)}$$
$$\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g_T(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\bigl[s_T(t)*h_T(t)\bigr],}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{g_T(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}\bigr\}.}$$
(9.1)
Derivation: Transmission

Multiplying out $\textcolor{#e5383b}{G}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{S\cdot H}$ with both representations (7.2) gives four products. The mixed ones, such as $S_T(f-f_0)H_T^*(-f-f_0)$, are zero: the first factor lives at positive frequencies, the second at negative ones. What remains is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{G}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac14S_T(f-f_0)H_T(f-f_0)+\frac14S_T^*(-f-f_0)H_T^*(-f-f_0)}$. Comparing this with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{G}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12G_T(f-f_0)+\frac12G_T^*(-f-f_0)}$ gives $\textcolor{#e5383b}{G_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12S_TH_T}$.

⁠How to read it: the factor $\frac12$ compensates for the fact that $S_T$ and $H_T$ were both doubled. Otherwise it is the familiar convolution theorem, just for the envelopes. The carrier need not be included in the calculation at all.

Example: switched-on cosine through the ideal bandpass. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varepsilon(t)\cos(2\pi f_0t)}$ with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_0}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{10}$ Hz, bandpass with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Delta f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$ Hz around $f_0$. The envelope of the input is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varepsilon(t)}$. (Approximation: the spectrum of the step is not limited to $f>-f_0$, but in the passband $|f|\le\Delta f/2\ll f_0$ it is correct.) With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\Delta f\operatorname{si}(\pi\Delta f\,t)}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g_T(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\bigl[\varepsilon(t)*2\Delta f\operatorname{si}(\pi\Delta f\,t)\bigr]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12+\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(\pi\Delta f\,t),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Bigl[\frac12+\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(\pi\Delta f\,t)\Bigr]\cos(2\pi f_0t).}$$

The envelope is the step response of an ideal lowpass with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Delta f/2}$: rise time $\textcolor{#e5383b}{1/\Delta f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5}$ s, overshoot 8.95 % (Fig. 5). A narrow bandpass takes a long time to “settle”.

Stage 10: Quadrature circuit and bandpass sampling

What this stage is about:

How are the quadrature components obtained in practice? And how many samples does a bandpass signal need?

Quadrature circuit. If you multiply a bandpass signal by $\cos(2\pi f_0t)$ and filter it with a lowpass, $\frac12s_{Tr}$ remains. With $-\sin(2\pi f_0t)$, $\frac12s_{Ti}$ remains. The reason is trigonometric:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s\cos}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_{Tr}\cos^2-s_{Ti}\sin\cos}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12s_{Tr}+\underbrace{\tfrac12s_{Tr}\cos(4\pi f_0t)-\tfrac12s_{Ti}\sin(4\pi f_0t)}_{\text{at }2f_0,\text{ removed by the lowpass}},}$$

and likewise $\textcolor{#e5383b}{-s\sin}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12s_{Ti}+(\text{components at }2f_0)}$. The prerequisite is that the band ends below $2f_0$. In the lowpass domain you then filter with four lowpass filters ($h_{Tr}$, $\pm h_{Ti}$) according to (9.1). At the output you modulate back onto the carrier according to (8.2), or you form the envelope $\sqrt{g_{Tr}^2+g_{Ti}^2}$ (Fig. 6).

Sampling theorem for bandpass signals. With respect to the center frequency, a bandpass signal of bandwidth $\Delta f$ has quadrature components that are lowpass signals up to $\Delta f/2$. Each of them can be sampled at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{1/T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Delta f}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_ns_{Tr}(nT)\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\pi\frac{t-nT}{T}\Bigr)\cos(2\pi f_0t)-\sum_ns_{Ti}(nT)\operatorname{si}\Bigl(\pi\frac{t-nT}{T}\Bigr)\sin(2\pi f_0t),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{\Delta f}.}$$
(10.1)

In total you need $2\Delta f$ values per second, as many as for a lowpass signal with the cutoff frequency $\Delta f$. So what matters is the bandwidth, not the highest frequency.

Stage 11: Phase and group delay in a bandpass

What this stage is about:

Why are there two delays? Because the carrier and the envelope can be delayed differently.

The idea. Let a bandpass have a constant magnitude and a slightly curved phase in the passband. Around $f_0$, approximate the phase by its tangent:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{\approx}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi(f_0)+(f-f_0)\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d} f}\Big|_{f_0}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-2\pi f_0t_\mathrm p-2\pi(f-f_0)t_\mathrm g}$$

with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{t_\mathrm p(f_0)}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{t_\mathrm g(f_0)}$ from (1.4) and (1.5). In the lowpass domain ($f-f_0\to f$) this is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_T(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\operatorname{rect}(f/\Delta f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t_\mathrm p}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi ft_\mathrm g}}$: a constant phase and a delay.

Theorem 11.1 (Delays in a bandpass)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g_T(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_T(t-t_\mathrm g)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t_\mathrm p},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{s_T(t-t_\mathrm g)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0(t-t_\mathrm p)}\bigr\}.}$$
(11.1)

⁠How to read it: the envelope, which carries the information, is delayed by the group delay. The carrier is delayed by the phase delay. The transmission is distortionless in the strict sense only if the two are equal. That is, the phase line passes through the origin (or through a multiple of $2\pi$ at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$). For the information, a constant group delay is usually enough: if $t_\mathrm g$ is not constant, different frequency components of the envelope arrive at different times, and pulses spread out (delay distortion).

In the .ac analysis its phase at 10 Hz is $0^\circ$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm p(f_0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$. Exercise 10 ★★ Reading off delays

At $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_0}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ MHz, a bandpass has the phase $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(f_0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\pi/2}$ and the slope $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d} f}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-2\pi\cdot2\,\mu\text{s}}$.

  1. Compute $t_\mathrm p$ and $t_\mathrm g$.

  2. By how much is the envelope of a pulse delayed at the output? Is the carrier then still in the same position relative to the envelope?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\frac{\varphi(f_0)}{2\pi f_0}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\pi/2}{2\pi\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{Hz}}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.25}$ µ s. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$ µ s.

(b) The envelope arrives 2 µ s later. The carrier is shifted by only 0.25 µ s. Relative to the envelope it is therefore rotated by $\textcolor{#e5383b}{2\pi f_0(t_\mathrm g-t_\mathrm p)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3.5\pi}$, which modulo $2\pi$ equals $1.5\pi$, that is $-\pi/2$: a quarter period. The information in the envelope remains undistorted as long as $t_\mathrm g$ is constant over the band.

Stage 12: Discrete-time bandpass and highpass filters

What this stage is about:

Digital bandpass and highpass filters are obtained from a lowpass by modulation.

If the discrete-time lowpass has a real impulse response, multiplication by $2\cos(2\pi f_0n)$ shifts its passband to $\pm f_0$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_{\mathrm{BP}}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H_{\mathrm{TP}}(f)*\bigl[\delta(f+f_0)+\delta(f-f_0)\bigr],}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_{\mathrm{BP}}(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\cos(2\pi f_0n)\,h_{\mathrm{TP}}(n),}$$
(12.1)

valid as long as the shifted bands do not overlap: $f_{\mathrm g,\mathrm{TP}}\le f_0\le\frac12-f_{\mathrm g,\mathrm{TP}}$. Because of the periodicity, the band at $f_0$ must touch neither the band at $-f_0$ nor its copy at $1-f_0$. In general, also for complex lowpass impulse responses, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_{\mathrm{BP}}(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\operatorname{Re}\{h_{\mathrm{TP}}(n)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0n}\}}$ holds as in (8.1). The factor 2 corresponds to the doubled equivalent lowpass $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2H_{\mathrm{TP}}}$.

A highpass is a lowpass shifted to half the sampling rate, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_0}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$. There the band and its mirror image coincide, so it is shifted only once: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_{\mathrm{HP}}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{H_{\mathrm{TP}}(f-\frac12)}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\pi n}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(-1)^n}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_{\mathrm{HP}}(n)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(-1)^n\,h_{\mathrm{TP}}(n).}$$
(12.2)

⁠In short: a digital highpass is obtained from a lowpass by flipping every other sign.

Exercise 11 ★ Highpass from the moving average

The moving average $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_{\mathrm{TP}}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac13(1,1,1)}$ (Part 4) is a simple lowpass.

  1. Form $h_{\mathrm{HP}}$ according to (12.2).

  2. Compute $H_{\mathrm{TP}}$ and $H_{\mathrm{HP}}$ at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$. Is this consistent with a highpass?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h_{\mathrm{HP}}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac13(1,-1,1)}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H_{\mathrm{TP}}(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac13\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}[1+2\cos(2\pi f)]}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|H_{\mathrm{TP}}(0)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|H_{\mathrm{TP}}(\frac12)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac13}$. The highpass is shifted by $\frac12$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|H_{\mathrm{HP}}(0)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac13}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|H_{\mathrm{HP}}(\frac12)|}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$. It passes the fastest sequence $(+1,-1,+1,\dots)$ in full and attenuates the DC component to one third. A simple highpass, not a very steep one.

Key points

  • Distortionless: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{h}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{h_0\delta(t-t_0)}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{h_0\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi t_0f}}$: constant magnitude, linear phase.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{a}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-20\lg|H|}$ dB; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\frac{\varphi}{2\pi f}}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d} f}}$.

  • Ideal lowpass: $\operatorname{rect}(f/2f_\mathrm g)\leftrightarrow2f_\mathrm g\operatorname{si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)$, not causal; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f_\mathrm g t_m}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$; overshoot 21.7 % (impulse), 8.95 % (step).

  • Step response $\frac12+\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(2\pi f_\mathrm gt)$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{Si}(\infty)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac\pi2}$, rise time $\frac{1}{2f_\mathrm g}$.

  • Truncating the impulse response $=$ convolution in the spectrum; smooth windows: deeper stopband, shallower edge.

  • Pulse-shaping filter: $T\operatorname{rect}(Tf)[1+\cos(2\pi Tf)]$, little overshoot.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{H(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12H_T(f-f_0)+\frac12H_T^*(-f-f_0)}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\{s_T\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s_{Tr}\cos-s_{Ti}\sin}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2s_+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}}$.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12s_T*h_T}$; envelope delayed by $t_\mathrm g$, carrier by $t_\mathrm p$.

  • Bandpass sampling: $2\Delta f$ values per second; digital highpass: $(-1)^nh_{\mathrm{TP}}(n)$.
