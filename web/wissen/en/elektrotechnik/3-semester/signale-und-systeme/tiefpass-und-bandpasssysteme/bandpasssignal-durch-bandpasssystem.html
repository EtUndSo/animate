What this stage is about:

What the envelopes are good for: how a bandpass acts on a bandpass signal can be calculated entirely in the lowpass domain.

Theorem 9.1 (Transmission in the equivalent lowpass domain)

If $S_T$ and $H_T$ both satisfy (7.2) (nothing below $-f_0$), then the envelope of the output satisfies

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_T(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12S_T(f)\,H_T(f)}$$
$$\bullet\!\!-\!\!\circ$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_T(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12\bigl[s_T(t)*h_T(t)\bigr],}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{g_T(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}\bigr\}.}$$
(9.1)
Derivation: Transmission

Multiplying out $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S\cdot H}$ with both representations (7.2) gives four products. The mixed ones, such as $S_T(f-f_0)H_T^*(-f-f_0)$, are zero: the first factor lives at positive frequencies, the second at negative ones. What remains is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac14S_T(f-f_0)H_T(f-f_0)+\frac14S_T^*(-f-f_0)H_T^*(-f-f_0)}$. Comparing this with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12G_T(f-f_0)+\frac12G_T^*(-f-f_0)}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12S_TH_T}$.

⁠How to read it: the factor $\frac12$ compensates for the fact that $S_T$ and $H_T$ were both doubled. Otherwise it is the familiar convolution theorem, just for the envelopes. The carrier need not be included in the calculation at all.

Exampleswitched-on cosine through the ideal bandpass

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varepsilon(t)\cos(2\pi f_0t)}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ Hz, bandpass with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ Hz around $f_0$. The envelope of the input is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varepsilon(t)}$. (Approximation: the spectrum of the step is not limited to $f>-f_0$, but in the passband $|f|\le\Delta f/2\ll f_0$ it is correct.) With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\Delta f\operatorname{si}(\pi\Delta f\,t)}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_T(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12\bigl[\varepsilon(t)*2\Delta f\operatorname{si}(\pi\Delta f\,t)\bigr]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(\pi\Delta f\,t),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl[\frac12+\frac1\pi\operatorname{Si}(\pi\Delta f\,t)\Bigr]\cos(2\pi f_0t).}$$

The envelope is the step response of an ideal lowpass with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Delta f/2}$: rise time $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/\Delta f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5}$ s, overshoot 8.95 % (Fig. 5). A narrow bandpass takes a long time to “settle”.

LTspiceSwitched-on carrier through a resonant circuit

An ideal bandpass cannot be built, but a series resonant circuit can. Draw: a voltage source at net in, then in series ind with {L}, cap with {1/((2*pi*f0)**2*L)} and res with {2*pi*df*L} to ground. The output out is the voltage across the resistor. With .param f0=10 df=2 L=1 the center frequency is 10 Hz and the bandwidth between the $-3$-dB points is 2 Hz.

⁠Check: source value AC 1 and .ac lin 2001 5 15 show the maximum 1 at 10 Hz and the $-3$-dB points at 9.05 Hz and 11.05 Hz.

⁠Experiment: source SINE(0 1 10 0 0 90), which is $\varepsilon(t)\cos(2\pi f_0t)$, and .tran 0 3 0 0.1m. The envelope rises without overshoot: the carrier peaks reach 0.72 at 0.2 s, 0.96 at 0.5 s and practically 1 after 1 s, almost exactly like $1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/0.16\,\mathrm s}$. The ideal bandpass, by contrast, overshoots to 1.09 (Fig. 5). The reason is the same as for the lowpass in Stage 3: the edges of the resonant circuit are soft, those of the ideal bandpass are vertical.
