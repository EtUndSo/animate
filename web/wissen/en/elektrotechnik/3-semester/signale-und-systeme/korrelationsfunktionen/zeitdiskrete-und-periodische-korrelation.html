Stage 7: Discrete-time and periodic correlation

What this stage is about:

For samples, the integral becomes a sum. With the DFT, correlations can be computed quickly.

Theorem 7.1 (Discrete-time correlation)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi^E_{sg}(m)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}s^*(n)\,g(n+m)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s^*(-m)*g(m),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_n|s(n)|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|S_\mathrm a(f)|^2\,\mathrm{d} f,}$$
(7.1)
$$\varphi^E_{ss}(m)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;|S_\mathrm a(f)|^2\ (\text{period }1),$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi^E_{gg}(m)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi^E_{ss}(m)*\varphi^E_{hh}(m).}$$
(7.2)

For sampled low-pass signals, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi^E_{sg}(mT)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{T\sum_ns^*(nT)g((n+m)T)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{T\sum_n|s(nT)|^2}$. A discrete-time energy signal is a square-summable sequence.

Example

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,2,1)}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi^E_{ss}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,4,6,4,1)}$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2,\dots,2}$. Maximum $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{E_s}$ at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, even, five values instead of three. Check with Wiener–Khinchin at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|S_\mathrm a(0)|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1+2+1)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_m\varphi^E_{ss}(m)}$.

Periodic correlation. As with convolution (Chapter 4), the following holds for sequences with the period $M$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi^E_{sgd}(m)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}s_d^*(n)\,g_d(n+m)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_d^*(k)\,G_d(k).}$$
(7.3)

Computed with the FFT, this is called fast correlation. For the periodic correlation to reproduce the ordinary one, $M$ must be large enough. For $(1,2,1)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ gives the overlapped sequence $(6,5,5)$, whereas $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ gives $(6,4,1,1,4)$, the ordinary ACF arranged cyclically.

Power signals. For signals of infinite energy, you average:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi^L_{sg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}s^*(t)\,g(t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$
(7.4)

If both signals are periodic with the same period, one period is sufficient, and the correlation function is periodic as well. Example: $A\cos(2\pi Ft+\varphi_0)$ has the ACF $\frac{A^2}{2}\cos(2\pi F\tau)$. The phase $\varphi_0$ has disappeared, and at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ the ACF equals the power $\frac{A^2}{2}$.

Exercise 6 ★ Discrete ACF

  1. Verify the ACF of $(1,2,1)$ by hand.

  2. Find the ACF of $(1,-1)$. What does $\varphi(\pm1)<0$ tell you about the signal?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+4+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi(\pm1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\cdot2+2\cdot1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi(\pm2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\cdot1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi(\pm1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. A negative value at the shift 1 means that neighboring values tend to have opposite signs: the signal changes quickly. Consistent with this, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|S_\mathrm a(f)|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2-2\cos(2\pi f)}$ is zero at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and maximal at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$: a high-pass signal.

Exercise 7 ★★ ACF of a cosine

Derive the ACF of $A\cos(2\pi Ft+\varphi_0)$ with (7.4) over one period. Why does $\varphi_0$ drop out, and what does this mean for measuring the phase with the ACF?

Solution

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\pi F}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\cos a\cos b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12[\cos(a-b)+\cos(a+b)]}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac1T\int_0^TA^2\cos(\omega t+\varphi_0)\cos(\omega t+\omega\tau+\varphi_0)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{A^2}{2}\cos(\omega\tau)+\frac{A^2}{2T}\int_0^T\cos(2\omega t+\omega\tau+2\varphi_0)\mathrm{d} t.}$$

The second integral runs over two full periods and is zero. The phase $\varphi_0$ only shifts the time origin, and averaging over all times knows no origin. The ACF of a single signal therefore cannot measure a phase. The phase difference of two signals, on the other hand, is revealed by the cross-correlation.
