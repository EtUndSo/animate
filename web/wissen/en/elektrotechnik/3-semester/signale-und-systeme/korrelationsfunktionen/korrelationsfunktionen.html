What this is about

A radar sends out a pulse and searches the received signal for its echo. A mobile phone searches the noise for a known synchronization sequence. Both ask the same question: How similar is one signal to another, and at which shift is it most similar? The answer is given by the correlation function. Its Fourier transform also tells you how the energy of a signal is distributed over the frequencies.

The answer in three sentences (by the end of the article they should feel obvious)

  1. The similarity of two signals is measured by the integral of their product. The correlation function does this for every shift $\tau$; it is a convolution with the flipped signal.

  2. The Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function is the energy spectral density $|S(f)|^2$. At $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ it equals the energy (Parseval).

  3. An LTI system multiplies energy spectral densities by $|H(f)|^2$ and convolves autocorrelation functions with the autocorrelation function of $h$.

One principle behind all the results. Correlation is convolution with a flipped signal. That is why all the tools of convolution apply:

ResultWhere it comes from
Correlation coefficientenergy of the difference of two normalized signals (Stage 2)
Correlation productconvolution with the flipped, conjugated signal (Stage 3)
ACF even, maximum at 0flipping, and a signal is most similar to itself (Stage 3)

Stage 1: Energy and power

What this stage is about:

Before comparing signals, you need a measure of their “size”. In system theory this is the energy, or, for infinitely long signals, the power.

In a resistor $R$, the energy $\frac1R\int u^2\mathrm{d} t$ is converted into heat during the time from $t_1$ to $t_2$. Without the factor $1/R$, the expression is called the signal energy:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|s(t)|^2\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$
(1.1)

If it is finite, $s$ is called an energy signal (for example, any pulse). If a signal lasts infinitely long without decaying (a cosine, a step), its energy is infinite. Then you measure the average power:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|s(t)|^2\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$
(1.2)

If it is finite, $s$ is called a power signal. For periodic signals, averaging over one period is sufficient. For Dirac impulses, energy and power are not defined ($\delta^2$ does not exist).

Exercise 1 ★ Energy or power?

Find the energy or the power of (a) $a\operatorname{rect}(t/T)$, (b) $\varepsilon(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t}$, (c) $A\cos(2\pi Ft)$, (d) $\varepsilon(t)$.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{a^2|T|}$. (b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$. (c) Infinite energy, power $\frac{A^2}{2}$ (the mean value of $\cos^2$ is $\frac12$). (d) Infinite energy, power $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\lim\frac{1}{2T}\int_0^T1\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$: the signal is 1 for half of the time.

Stage 2: Measuring similarity, the correlation coefficient

What this stage is about:

How do you measure with a single number how similar two signals are? You measure how large their difference is.

The idea. The energy of the difference $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\Delta(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s(t)-g(t)}$ is small when the signals are similar. Multiplied out:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_\Delta}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int|s-g|^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{E_s+E_g-\int s^*g\,\mathrm{d} t-\int g^*s\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{E_s+E_g-2\operatorname{Re}\int s^*(t)g(t)\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$

So the difference lies solely in the integral of the product. To keep the size of the signals from counting, you normalize both to the energy 1. Then $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_\Delta}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2-2\operatorname{Re}\,p_{sg}}$ with the correlation coefficient

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s^*(t)\,g(t)\,\mathrm{d} t}{\sqrt{E_sE_g}}.}$$
(2.1)

⁠How to read it: $|p_{sg}|\le1$ (Cauchy–Schwarz inequality). $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ means $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{a\,g}$ with $a>0$: the same shape. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-1}$ means $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-a\,g}$: the same shape, opposite sign. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ means $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int s^*g\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$; the signals are orthogonal, like the harmonics of a Fourier series in Part 3. Example: $\operatorname{rect}(t)$ and $\Lambda(t)$ have $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int\operatorname{rect}\cdot\Lambda\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.75}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_{\operatorname{rect}}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_\Lambda}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac23}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.919}$.

Exercise 2 ★★ Correlation coefficients

Compute $p$ for (a) $\operatorname{rect}(t)$ and $\operatorname{rect}(t-0.5)$, (b) $\operatorname{rect}(t)$ and $\Lambda(t)$ (result above), (c) $\operatorname{rect}(t)$ and $\operatorname{sgn}(t)\operatorname{rect}(t)$.

Solution

(a) The rectangles overlap over a length of 0.5, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5}$, and both energies are 1: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5}$. (b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int\operatorname{rect}\cdot\Lambda\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}(1-|t|)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.75}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_\Lambda}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\int_0^1(1-t)^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac23}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{0.75}{\sqrt{2/3}}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.919}$. Very similar shapes. (c) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int\operatorname{sgn}(t)\operatorname{rect}^2(t)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$; the signals are orthogonal, although they are equal on the right half.

Stage 3: Correlation function and correlation product

What this stage is about:

An echo is similar to the transmitted pulse, but shifted. You therefore compare for every shift $\tau$.

Definition 3.1 (Correlation function for energy signals)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s^*(t)\,g(t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s^*(-\tau)*g(\tau).}$$
(3.1)

For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s}$ it is called the autocorrelation function (ACF) $\varphi^E_{ss}(\tau)$, otherwise the cross-correlation function (CCF). Divided by $\sqrt{E_sE_g}$, it is called normalized.

⁠How to read it: You shift $g$ to the left by $\tau$ and form the integral of the product. The second equals sign is the heart of the matter: correlation is convolution with the flipped, conjugated signal. That is why it is also called the correlation product. Unlike convolution, nothing is flipped here; both signals run in the same direction.

Derivation: Correlation as convolution

Set $\textcolor{#e5383b}{t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-\vartheta}$ in (3.1): $\int s^*(-\vartheta)\,g(\tau-\vartheta)\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta$. This is the convolution integral of $s^*(-\tau)$ with $g(\tau)$.

Theorem 3.2 (Properties of correlation functions)
$$\begin{aligned} &\varphi^E_{gs}(\tau)=\varphi^{E*}_{sg}(-\tau),\qquad\varphi^E_{ss}(\tau)=\varphi^{E*}_{ss}(-\tau)\ \ (\text{even for real }s),\\ &|\varphi^E_{ss}(\tau)|\le\varphi^E_{ss}(0)=E_s. \end{aligned}$$
(3.2)(3.3)

A time-limited signal has an ACF of twice the duration.

⁠How to read it: A signal is most similar to itself without a shift, and there the ACF is its energy. The ACF of a rectangle is the triangle: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{ss}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{rect}(-\tau)*\operatorname{rect}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Lambda(\tau)}$, twice as wide as the rectangle (Fig. 1, left).

Example: finding an echo. The transmitted signal is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{rect}(t)}$; the received signal is the attenuated, delayed echo $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5\operatorname{rect}(t-3)}$. The CCF is $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5\,\Lambda(\tau-3)}$: its maximum lies at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$, the delay, and its height $\textcolor{#e5383b}{0.5}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0.5\cdot E_s}$ indicates the attenuation (Fig. 1, right). This is how a radar measures distance.

QuestionConvolution and correlation look almost the same. What is the difference?

In convolution, $\int s(\tau)h(t-\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau$, the second signal runs backward ($-\tau$): the system “remembers” the past. In correlation, $\int s^*(t)g(t+\tau)\mathrm{d} t$, both run forward: you place one shape exactly over the other and measure how well they match. Mathematically, the difference is precisely the flipping; that is why correlation is convolution with the flipped signal.

Exercise 3 ★★ Echo and repetition

  1. A signal consists of two rectangles: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{rect}(t)+\operatorname{rect}(t-2)}$. Find its ACF. How can you see the repetition in it?

  2. Check (3.3) for this signal.

Solution

(a) With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{rect}(-\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{rect}(\tau)}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\operatorname{rect}*\operatorname{rect}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\Lambda}$, the four partial products give

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\Lambda(\tau)+\Lambda(\tau-2)+\Lambda(\tau+2).}$$

The side peaks at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\pm2}$ show the spacing of the repetition. There, in each case, one of the two rectangles lies over the other. At $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ both lie on top of each other, which gives twice the height.

(b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{E_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1+1}$, and the side peaks have only the value 1.

Stage 4: Energy spectral density

What this stage is about:

What is the Fourier transform of a correlation function? It shows how the energy is distributed over the frequencies.

Theorem 4.1 (Wiener–Khinchin for energy signals)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s^*(-\tau)*s(\tau)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S^*(f)\,S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{|S(f)|^2,}$$
$$\varphi^E_{sg}(\tau)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S^*(f)\,G(f).$$
(4.1)

⁠How to read it: $|S(f)|^2$ is called the energy spectral density. It is real, never negative and, for real signals, even in $f$. The phase of the signal is lost: all signals with the same magnitude spectrum have the same ACF. $S^*G$ is called the cross energy spectral density. The derivation needs only the convolution theorem, time reversal and conjugation: $s^*(-\tau)\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\,S^*(f)$ (Part 3).

Parseval. At $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ the ACF is the energy. With the area theorem:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|s(t)|^2\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|S(f)|^2\,\mathrm{d} f.}$$
(4.2)

Two orthogonal signals also have orthogonal spectra: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int s^*g\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int S^*G\,\mathrm{d} f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$.

Example: the exponential pulse. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varepsilon(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t}}$ has $\textcolor{#e5383b}{S(f)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{j}2\pi f}}$, so $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|S|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{1+(2\pi f)^2}}$. This is also $\operatorname{Re}\{S\}$, and its inverse transform is the even part of the signal (Part 3):

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{ss}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\mathrm{e}^{-|\tau|},}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12.}$$

The same value follows from $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$ in the time domain and from $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int\frac{\mathrm{d} f}{1+(2\pi f)^2}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$ in the frequency domain. Figure 2 shows the signal, its ACF and both spectra.

Stage 5: Correlation and LTI systems

What this stage is about:

What does an LTI system do to the ACF and the energy spectral density? And how do you measure a system with cross-correlation?

Theorem 5.1 (Wiener–Lee)

For $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s*h}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{gg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}(\tau)*\varphi^E_{hh}(\tau)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;|G(f)|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{|S(f)|^2\,|H(f)|^2,}$$
(5.1)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}(\tau)*h(\tau)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;|S(f)|^2\,H(f).}$$
(5.2)
Derivation: Wiener–Lee

$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{gg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{g^*(-\tau)*g(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{[s^*(-\tau)*h^*(-\tau)]*[s(\tau)*h(\tau)]}$. Rearrange using the commutative and associative laws: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{[s^*(-\tau)*s(\tau)]*[h^*(-\tau)*h(\tau)]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}*\varphi^E_{hh}}$. Likewise, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s^*(-\tau)*s(\tau)*h(\tau)}$.

⁠How to read it: The system multiplies the energy at each frequency by $|H|^2$. The phase of the system plays no role for the energy. Equation (5.2) is a measurement method: if the input has an ACF that is almost a weighted Dirac impulse $c\,\delta(\tau)$ (a very short pulse or broadband noise, Part 7), then the cross-correlation between input and output is almost $c\,h(\tau)$, that is, the impulse response up to the factor $c$. Unlike $|H|^2$, it also contains the phase.

Exercise 5 ★★ Energy at the output

The exponential pulse $\varepsilon(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t}$ passes through an RC circuit with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{RC}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$ s.

  1. Find $|G(f)|^2$ and from it the output energy. Hint: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\mathrm{d} x}{(1+x^2)^2}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac\pi2}$.

  2. Check in the time domain with $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g(t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{t\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\varepsilon(t)}$ (Part 1, two RC circuits in cascade).

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|G|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{|S|^2|H|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{(1+(2\pi f)^2)^2}}$. With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{x}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\pi f}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_g}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d} x}{(1+x^2)^2}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac\pi2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac14}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int_0^\infty t^2\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{2}{2^3}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac14}$. Both ways agree. The energy drops from $\frac12$ to $\frac14$ because the second RC circuit attenuates the higher frequencies further.

Stage 6: Correlation of bandpass signals

What this stage is about:

Correlation functions of bandpass signals, too, are computed with the complex envelopes (Part 5).

The CCF of two real bandpass signals is again a bandpass signal:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{\varphi^E_{sgT}(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0\tau}\bigr\}.}$$

The flipped signal has the envelope $s_T^*(-t)$, because

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{s(-t)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{s_T(-t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}\bigr\}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{s_T^*(-t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}2\pi f_0t}\bigr\}.}$$

With the transmission rule $\textcolor{#e5383b}{g_T}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12s_T*h_T}$ from Part 5:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sgT}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\bigl[s_T^*(-\tau)*g_T(\tau)\bigr]}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\tfrac12\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s_T^*(t)\,g_T(t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d} t,}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ssT}(0).}$$
(6.1)

⁠How to read it: The correlation of two bandpass signals is half the correlation of their envelopes, modulated onto the carrier. The energy of a bandpass signal is half the energy of its complex envelope: a cosine of amplitude $|s_T|$ has, on average, only half the power.

Stage 7: Discrete-time and periodic correlation

What this stage is about:

For samples, the integral becomes a sum. With the DFT, correlations can be computed quickly.

Theorem 7.1 (Discrete-time correlation)
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}s^*(n)\,g(n+m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s^*(-m)*g(m),}$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_n|s(n)|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|S_\mathrm a(f)|^2\,\mathrm{d} f,}$$
(7.1)
$$\varphi^E_{ss}(m)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;|S_\mathrm a(f)|^2\ (\text{period }1),$$
$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{gg}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}(m)*\varphi^E_{hh}(m).}$$
(7.2)

For sampled low-pass signals, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}(mT)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\sum_ns^*(nT)g((n+m)T)}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{T\sum_n|s(nT)|^2}$. A discrete-time energy signal is a square-summable sequence.

Example. $\textcolor{#e5383b}{s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(1,2,1)}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{ss}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(1,4,6,4,1)}$ for $\textcolor{#e5383b}{m}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-2,\dots,2}$. Maximum $\textcolor{#e5383b}{6}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{E_s}$ at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{m}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$, even, five values instead of three. Check with Wiener–Khinchin at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|S_\mathrm a(0)|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{(1+2+1)^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{16}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_m\varphi^E_{ss}(m)}$.

Periodic correlation. As with convolution (Part 4), the following holds for sequences with the period $M$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sgd}(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}s_d^*(n)\,g_d(n+m)\;\circ\!\!-\!\!\bullet\;S_d^*(k)\,G_d(k).}$$
(7.3)

Computed with the FFT, this is called fast correlation. For the periodic correlation to reproduce the ordinary one, $M$ must be large enough. For $(1,2,1)$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{3}$ gives the overlapped sequence $(6,5,5)$, whereas $\textcolor{#e5383b}{M}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{5}$ gives $(6,4,1,1,4)$, the ordinary ACF arranged cyclically.

Power signals. For signals of infinite energy, you average:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^L_{sg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}s^*(t)\,g(t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$
(7.4)

If both signals are periodic with the same period, one period is sufficient, and the correlation function is periodic as well. Example: $A\cos(2\pi Ft+\varphi_0)$ has the ACF $\frac{A^2}{2}\cos(2\pi F\tau)$. The phase $\varphi_0$ has disappeared, and at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\tau}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ the ACF equals the power $\frac{A^2}{2}$.

Exercise 6 ★ Discrete ACF

  1. Verify the ACF of $(1,2,1)$ by hand.

  2. Find the ACF of $(1,-1)$. What does $\varphi(\pm1)<0$ tell you about the signal?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1+4+1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{6}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(\pm1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1\cdot2+2\cdot1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{4}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(\pm2)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1\cdot1}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{1}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(\pm1)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{-1}$. A negative value at the shift 1 means that neighboring values tend to have opposite signs: the signal changes quickly. Consistent with this, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|S_\mathrm a(f)|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}2\pi f}|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2-2\cos(2\pi f)}$ is zero at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$ and maximal at $\textcolor{#e5383b}{f}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12}$: a high-pass signal.

Exercise 7 ★★ ACF of a cosine

Derive the ACF of $A\cos(2\pi Ft+\varphi_0)$ with (7.4) over one period. Why does $\varphi_0$ drop out, and what does this mean for measuring the phase with the ACF?

Solution

With $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\omega}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{2\pi F}$ and $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\cos a\cos b}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12[\cos(a-b)+\cos(a+b)]}$:

$$\textcolor{#e5383b}{\frac1T\int_0^TA^2\cos(\omega t+\varphi_0)\cos(\omega t+\omega\tau+\varphi_0)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{A^2}{2}\cos(\omega\tau)+\frac{A^2}{2T}\int_0^T\cos(2\omega t+\omega\tau+2\varphi_0)\mathrm{d} t.}$$

The second integral runs over two full periods and is zero. The phase $\varphi_0$ only shifts the time origin, and averaging over all times knows no origin. The ACF of a single signal therefore cannot measure a phase. The phase difference of two signals, on the other hand, is revealed by the cross-correlation.

Key points

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{E_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int|s|^2\mathrm{d} t}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{L_s}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\lim\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^T|s|^2\mathrm{d} t}$.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac{\int s^*g\,\mathrm{d} t}{\sqrt{E_sE_g}}}$, $|p|\le1$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{p}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{0}$: orthogonal.

  • $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int s^*(t)g(t+\tau)\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{s^*(-\tau)*g(\tau)}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{gs}(\tau)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^{E*}_{sg}(-\tau)}$.

  • ACF: even (for real signals), maximum $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{ss}(0)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{E_s}$, twice the duration.

  • $\varphi^E_{ss}\leftrightarrow|S|^2$, $\varphi^E_{sg}\leftrightarrow S^*G$; Parseval $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\int|s|^2\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\int|S|^2\mathrm{d} f}$.

  • Wiener–Lee: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{gg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}*\varphi^E_{hh}}$, $\textcolor{#e5383b}{|G|^2}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{|S|^2|H|^2}$; $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sg}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\varphi^E_{ss}*h}$.

  • Bandpass: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi^E_{sgT}}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\frac12s_T^*(-\tau)*g_T(\tau)}$.

  • Discrete: $\textcolor{#e5383b}{\varphi(m)}\textcolor{#2b6def}{=}\textcolor{#ff9f1c}{\sum s^*(n)g(n+m)}$, $\leftrightarrow|S_\mathrm a|^2$; periodic: $S_d^*G_d$.
