From the ground up: Number systems and decimal numbers

Definition 0.1 (Real number, provisional)

A real number is a number with a decimal representation, that is, with a sign, an $m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}$ and digits $a_1,a_2,\ldots\in\{0,1,\dots,9\}$, finitely or infinitely many:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm\Bigl(m+\frac{a_1}{10}+\frac{a_2}{100}+\frac{a_3}{1000}+\dots\Bigr).}$$
(0.1)

$\mathbb{R}$ is the set of real numbers. The numbers in $\mathbb{R}$ that are not fractions are called irrational.

Stage 2: Quantifiers and indirect proof

Definition 2.1 (Quantifiers)

$\forall$ means “for all” (universal quantifier), $\exists$ “there is at least one” (existential quantifier), $\exists_1$ “there is exactly one,” $\nexists$ “there is no.”

Theorem 2.2 (Negation rule)
$$\neg\bigl(\forall x\in M:\mathcal A(x)\bigr)\Leftrightarrow\bigl(\exists x\in M:\neg\mathcal A(x)\bigr),\quad \neg\bigl(\exists y\in N:\mathcal B(y)\bigr)\Leftrightarrow\bigl(\forall y\in N:\neg\mathcal B(y)\bigr).$$
(2.1)
Theorem 2.3 (Indirect proof)

If $\neg\mathcal A\Rightarrow\mathcal B$ is true and $\mathcal B$ is false, then $\mathcal A$ is true.

Stage 3: Field axioms

Definition 3.1 (Field)

An operation assigns to any two elements an element of the same set. A set $\mathbb K$ with two operations “$+$” and “$\cdot$” is called a field if for all $x,y,z\in\mathbb K$:

associative(A2) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x+(y+z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x+y)+z}$(M2) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x\cdot(y\cdot z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x\cdot y)\cdot z}$

Theorem 3.2 (Rules of arithmetic in a field)

For all $x,y,z,u,v\in\mathbb K$:

  1. Inverses are unique: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(xy}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\wedge xz}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1)\Rightarrow y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x+y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\wedge x+z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0)\Rightarrow y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z}$.

  2. Equations are uniquely solvable: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x+z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ has exactly the solution $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y-x}$ (difference). $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{xz}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ has, for $x\ne0$, exactly the solution $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y\cdot x^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac yx}$ (quotient).

  3. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x\cdot0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and for $x\ne0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x^{-1})^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$.

  4. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-(-x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(-x)(-y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{xy}$.

  5. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{xy}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\Rightarrow(x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\vee y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0)}$.

  6. For $u,v\ne0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac xu\cdot\frac yv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{xy}{uv}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac xu+\frac yv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{xv+yu}{uv}}$, and for $u,v,y\ne0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac xu:\frac yv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{xv}{uy}}$.

Stage 4: Order axioms

Definition 4.1 (Totally ordered field)

A field $\mathbb K$ with a relation “$>0$” is called totally ordered if:

  • ⁠(T1) Every $x\in\mathbb K$ satisfies exactly one of the relations $x>0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $(-x)>0$.

  • ⁠(T2) $x>0\wedge y>0\Rightarrow x+y>0$.

  • ⁠(T3) $x>0\wedge y>0\Rightarrow x\cdot y>0$.

One sets $x>y:\Leftrightarrow x-y>0$, $x<y:\Leftrightarrow y>x$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x\ge y:\Leftrightarrow(x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y\vee x>y)}$, and likewise $x\le y$.

Theorem 4.2 (Calculating with inequalities)

In a totally ordered field, for all $x,y,z,u,v$:

(O1) Trichotomyexactly one of the relations $x<y$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$, $x>y$ holds
(O2) Transitivity$x<y\wedge y<z\Rightarrow x<z$
(O3) Monotonicity of addition$x<y\Rightarrow x+z<y+z$
(O4) Monotonicity of multiplication$x<y\wedge z>0\Rightarrow xz<yz$
(a) Reflecting$x<y\Leftrightarrow-y<-x$
(b) Adding$x<y\wedge u<v\Rightarrow x+u<y+v$
(c) Signs$xy>0\Leftrightarrow\bigl((x>0\wedge y>0)\vee(x<0\wedge y<0)\bigr)$
(d) Squares$x\ne0\Rightarrow x\cdot x>0$; in particular $1>0$
(e) Squaringfor $x\ge0$, $y\ge0$: $x^2\ge y^2\Leftrightarrow x\ge y$

Stage 5: Mathematical induction, sum and product

Theorem 5.1 (Mathematical induction)

Let $\mathcal A(n)$ be statements with $n\in\mathbb{N}$. If (1) $\mathcal A(1)$ is true (base case) and (2) for arbitrary $n$ the implication $\mathcal A(n)\Rightarrow\mathcal A(n+1)$ is true (induction step, $\mathcal A(n)$ is called the induction hypothesis), then $\mathcal A(n)$ is true for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ (induction conclusion).

⁠Second version: Let $n_0\in\mathbb{N}$ be fixed. If $\mathcal A(n_0)$ is true and for every $n\ge n_0$

$$\bigl(\mathcal A(n_0)\wedge\mathcal A(n_0+1)\wedge\dots\wedge\mathcal A(n)\bigr)\Rightarrow\mathcal A(n+1),$$

then $\mathcal A(n)$ is true for all $n\ge n_0$.

Definition 5.2 (Sum, product, power: recursive)

For $x_1,x_2,\ldots\in\mathbb K$ one sets

$$\sum_{i=1}^{1}x_i:=x_1,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_i:=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\Bigr)+x_{n+1},$$
$$\prod_{i=1}^{1}x_i:=x_1,\quad\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}x_i:=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_i\Bigr)\cdot x_{n+1}.$$
(5.1)

If all $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$, then $x^n:=\prod_{i=1}^nx$. For $x\ne0$ and $n\in\mathbb{N}$ one sets $x^0:=1$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^{-n}:=(x^n)^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\frac1x)^n}$. Also $n!:=\prod_{i=1}^ni$ and $0^0:=1$.

Theorem 5.3 (Rules for sum, product, power)

In every field:

  1. The value of $\sum_{i=1}^nx_i$ and $\prod_{i=1}^nx_i$ does not depend on the order of the $x_i$.

  2. Product of sums: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^nx_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^Ny_j\Bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^Nx_iy_j\Bigr)}$.

  3. Power laws for $x,y\ne0$, $p,q\in\mathbb{Z}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^px^q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^{p+q}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x^p)^q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^{p\cdot q}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(xy)^p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^py^p}$.

Stage 6: The completeness axiom

Theorem 6.1 (The square root of 2 is not a fraction)

There is no $r\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$.

Definition 6.2 (Bound, supremum, infimum)

Let $\mathbb K$ be totally ordered and $M\subset\mathbb K$.

  1. $M$ is called bounded above if there is $S\in\mathbb K$ with $x\le S$ for all $x\in M$. Every such $S$ is called an upper bound. Correspondingly bounded below and lower bound with $R\le x$. Both together are called bounded.

  2. $S$ is called the supremum of $M$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sup M}$, if $S$ is an upper bound and for every upper bound $\tilde S$ we have $S\le\tilde S$ (least upper bound).

  3. $R$ is called the infimum of $M$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\inf M}$, if $R$ is a lower bound and for every lower bound $\tilde R$ we have $\tilde R\le R$ (greatest lower bound).

  4. If $S$ lies in $M$, it is called the maximum ($\max M$); if $R$ lies in $M$, it is called the minimum ($\min M$).

Definition 6.3 (Completeness axiom (V))

A totally ordered field $\mathbb K$ is called complete if every nonempty set $M\subset\mathbb K$ that is bounded below has a greatest lower bound $\inf M\in\mathbb K$.

Theorem 6.4 (Main theorem)

There is essentially exactly one totally ordered, complete field that contains $\mathbb{Q}$. It is the field of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$; its order agrees on $\mathbb{Q}$ with the usual one.

Theorem 6.5 (Consequences of completeness)

In $\mathbb{R}$:

  1. Every nonempty set $M$ that is bounded above has a supremum, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sup M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\inf(-M)}$ with $-M:=\{-x:x\in M\}$.

  2. Supremum and infimum of a set are unique.

  3. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sup M}$, then for every $y<S$ there is an $x\in M$ with $y<x\le S$. The corresponding statement holds for the infimum.

  4. $\mathbb{N}$ is not bounded above.

  5. Theorem of Archimedes: For all $x,y>0$ there is an $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $nx>y$.

  6. For every $x>0$ there is an $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $0<\frac1n<x$ (Eudoxus).

Stage 7: Nested intervals and roots

Theorem 7.1 (Nested intervals)

For numbers $a_n,b_n\in\mathbb{R}$, $n\in\mathbb{N}$, suppose that $a_1\le a_2\le a_3\le\dots\le b_3\le b_2\le b_1$ and

$$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists n\in\mathbb{N}:\ 0\le b_n-a_n<\varepsilon.$$
(7.1)

Then there is exactly one $x\in\mathbb{R}$ with $a_n\le x\le b_n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

Theorem 7.2 (Square root)

For every $y\in\mathbb{R}_0^+$ there is exactly one $x\in\mathbb{R}_0^+$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$. One writes $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt y}$ (“square root of $y$”). For $x,y\ge0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{xy}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt x\sqrt y}$.

Stage 8: Distance and absolute value

Definition 8.1 (Distance and absolute value)

For $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$,

$$d(x,y):=\begin{cases}x-y,&x\ge y\\y-x,&x<y\end{cases}$$
$$\text{and}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|x|:=d(x,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{cases}x,&x\ge0\\-x,&x<0\end{cases}}$$
(8.1)

are called the distance of $x$ and $y$ and the absolute value of $x$.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(x,y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d(x-y,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|x-y|,\ \ -|x|\le x\le|x|,\ \ |x|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\max\{x,-x\},\ \ -|x|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\min\{x,-x\}.}$$
(8.2)

For all $x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}$:

Stage 9: Elementary inequalities

Theorem 9.1 (Five inequalities)

In $\mathbb{R}$:

  1. Bernoulli: For $x>-1$, $x\ne0$ and $n\ge2$, $(1+x)^n>1+nx$.

  2. Young: For $\varepsilon>0$ and $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, $|xy|\le\frac\varepsilon2x^2+\frac1{2\varepsilon}y^2$.

  3. Means: For $x,y\ge0$, $\sqrt{xy}\le\frac{x+y}2$. On the left is the geometric mean, on the right the arithmetic mean.

  4. Cauchy-Schwarz: For $x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_n\in\mathbb{R}$, with $\|\vec x\|:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}\ge0$,

    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^n|x_iy_i|\le\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ny_i^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\|\vec x\|\cdot\|\vec y\|.}$$
    (9.1)

  5. Minkowski: With $\vec x+\vec y:=(x_1+y_1,\dots,x_n+y_n)$,

    $$\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i+y_i)^2}\le\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}+\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ny_i^2},\quad\text{that is,}\quad\|\vec x+\vec y\|\le\|\vec x\|+\|\vec y\|.$$
    (9.2)

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. All rules of arithmetic follow by logical reasoning from a few axioms: the field axioms for plus and times and the order axioms for “positive.”

  2. The fractions satisfy these axioms too, but they have gaps: No fraction $r$ solves $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$.

  3. The completeness axiom closes the gaps: Every nonempty set that has a lower bound (a number less than or equal to all elements) also has a greatest one. With it the real numbers are uniquely determined, and $\sqrt2$ exists.

Chapter 2: Complex Numbers

Overview: Complex Numbers

What this is about
The reference model for this chapter

  • ⁠Two fixed numbers: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3+\mathrm{i}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+2\mathrm{i}}$. In $z_1$, 3 is the real part (the portion without $\mathrm{i}$) and 1 is the imaginary part (the real number in front of $\mathrm{i}$). General statements in this chapter use $z$ and $w$.

  • ⁠What becomes of them: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1+z_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4+3\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1z_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+7\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1/z_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z_1+z_2|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$.

  • ⁠Two equations: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2-6z+10}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ with real and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2-(4+3\mathrm{i})z+1+7\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ with complex coefficients. The second has exactly $z_1$ and $z_2$ as solutions.

Stage 1: Pairs instead of single numbers

Definition 1.1 (Complex numbers)

The set of complex numbers is $\mathbb{C}:=\{(x,y):x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}$ with the addition $\oplus$ and the multiplication $\odot$:

$$\begin{aligned} (x_1,y_1)\oplus(x_2,y_2)&:=(x_1+x_2,\;y_1+y_2),\\ (x_1,y_1)\odot(x_2,y_2)&:=(x_1x_2-y_1y_2,\;x_1y_2+x_2y_1). \end{aligned}$$
(1.1)(1.2)

The zero element is $(0,0)$, the unit element is $(1,0)$, the imaginary unit is $\mathrm{i}:=(0,1)$.

Theorem 1.2 (Imaginary unit and standard form)

  1. We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$.

  2. Every complex number has exactly one standard form $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ with $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$.

  3. In standard form, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x_1+\mathrm{i}y_1)+(x_2+\mathrm{i}y_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1+x_2)+\mathrm{i}(y_1+y_2)}$ and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x_1+\mathrm{i}y_1)(x_2+\mathrm{i}y_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1x_2-y_1y_2)+\mathrm{i}(x_1y_2+x_2y_1).}$$
(1.3)

Stage 2: The complex plane

Definition 2.1 (Real part, imaginary part, Gaussian plane)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ with $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{:\operatorname{Re}(z)}$ is called the real part and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{:\operatorname{Im}(z)}$ the imaginary part of $z$. The complex plane with the coordinates $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ is also called the Gaussian plane. We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{w}$ if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Re}(w)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Im}(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Im}(w)}$.

Theorem 2.2 (Multiplication by $\mathrm{i}$)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-y+\mathrm{i}x}$. The arrow of $z$ is thereby rotated by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about the origin.

Stage 3: Reflecting across the real axis

Definition 3.1 (Complex conjugate)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$, the number $\overline{z}:=x-\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{C}$ is called the conjugate of $z$.

Theorem 3.2 (Rules of arithmetic in $\mathbb{C}$)

For all $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u+\mathrm{i}v}$ with real $x,y,u,v$:

  1. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z+w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Re}(z)+\operatorname{Re}(w)}$,

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Im}(z+w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Im}(z)+\operatorname{Im}(w)}$,

  3. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z+w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}+\overline{w}}$,

  4. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z\cdot w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}\cdot\overline{w}}$,

  5. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{\overline{z}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z}$,

  6. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+y^2}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Im}(z\overline{z})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z\overline{z})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+y^2}$,

  7. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z+\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\operatorname{Re}(z)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z-\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\mathrm{i}\operatorname{Im}(z)}$.

Stage 4: Division, and what is lost

Theorem 4.1 (Reciprocal and division)

For every $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y\ne0}$ there is the number

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\overline{z}}{z\overline{z}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}-\mathrm{i}\,\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}}$$
$$\text{with}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\cdot z^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.}$$
(4.1)

For arbitrary $w$, this gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\dfrac wz}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{w\cdot z^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{w\,\overline{z}}{x^2+y^2}}$.

Theorem 4.2 ($\mathbb{C}$ is a field)

$\mathbb{C}$ with $+$ and $\cdot$ satisfies all field axioms from Chapter 1: commutative, associative and distributive laws, neutral elements $0\ne1$, the negative $-z$ for every $z$ and the reciprocal $1/z$ for every $z\ne0$.

Theorem 4.3 ($\mathbb{C}$ is not ordered)

There is no relation “$>0$” on $\mathbb{C}$ with the order axioms from Chapter 1: (T1) For every $x$, exactly one of $x>0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $-x>0$ holds. (T2) From $x>0$ and $y>0$ follows $x+y>0$. (T3) From $x>0$ and $y>0$ follows $x\cdot y>0$.

Stage 5: Absolute value and distance

Definition 5.1 (Distance and absolute value)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u+\mathrm{i}v}$ with real $x,y,u,v$, let

$$d(z,w):=\sqrt{(x-u)^2+(y-v)^2},$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|:=d(z,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.}$$
(5.1)
Theorem 5.2 (Rules for absolute value and distance)

For all $z,w,\tilde z\in\mathbb{C}$:

  1. $|z|\ge0$ and ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\Leftrightarrow z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$), likewise $d(z,w)\ge0$ and ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(z,w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\Leftrightarrow z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{w}$),

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(z,w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d(w,z)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\overline{z}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|{-z}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|}$,

  3. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2}$, so for $z\ne0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}\cdot1/|z|^2}$,

  4. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z\cdot w|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|\cdot|w|}$,

  5. $\max\{|\operatorname{Re}(z)|,|\operatorname{Im}(z)|\}\le|z|\le|\operatorname{Re}(z)|+|\operatorname{Im}(z)|$,

  6. $|z+w|\le|z|+|w|$ and $d(z,w)\le d(z,\tilde z)+d(\tilde z,w)$ (triangle inequality),

  7. $|z-w|\le|z|+|w|$, $\bigl||z|-|w|\bigr|\le|z-w|$ and $\bigl||z|-|w|\bigr|\le|z+w|$ (reverse triangle inequality).

Stage 6: Quadratic equations

Theorem 6.1 (Square roots in $\mathbb{C}$)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s+\mathrm{i}t\ne0}$ with $s,t\in\mathbb{R}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u+\mathrm{i}v}$ with real $u,v$, the equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D}$ is equivalent to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2-v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2uv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t}$. There are exactly two solutions $\pm w$, namely with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{|D|+s}2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{|D|-s}2.}$$
(6.1)

The signs of $u$ and $v$ must be chosen so that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2uv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t}$ holds. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is the only solution.

Theorem 6.2 (Quadratic equation in $\mathbb{C}$)

Let $p,q\in\mathbb{C}$ and $D:=p^2/4-q$. The equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2+pz+q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ has the solutions

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac p2\pm w}$$
$$\text{with}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D.}$$
(6.2)

For $D\ne0$ these are two solutions $z_+\ne z_-$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_++z_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_+z_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ there is only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p/2}$, a double solution. For real $p,q$: $D>0$ gives two real solutions, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ one real solution, $D<0$ the mutually conjugate solutions $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac p2\pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{-D}}$.

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. A complex number is a pair of real numbers, written $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$. The imaginary unit $\mathrm{i}$ is the pair $(0,1)$ and satisfies $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. All pairs together form the complex plane, in which $\mathbb{R}$ is the horizontal axis.

  2. You calculate as in $\mathbb{R}$ and replace $\mathrm{i}^2$ by $-1$. That is why $\mathbb{C}$ is a field (a set in which the four basic operations follow the familiar laws). What is lost is the order: There is no “greater than” between complex numbers.

  3. The complex conjugate $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x-\mathrm{i}y}$ is the mirror image of $z$ across the real axis, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+y^2}$ is real. From this follow division, the absolute value $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}$ (distance from the origin) and the triangle inequality $|z+w|\le|z|+|w|$ (a detour is never shorter).

What this is about
The reference model for this chapter

  • ⁠Parabola $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-4x+3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-2)^2-1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-1)(x-3)}$, lowest point $(2\,|\,{-1})$.

  • ⁠Polynomial $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x^3-3x^2-11x+6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-3)(2x-1)(x+2)}$ with the zeros $3$, $\tfrac12$ and $-2$.

  • ⁠Denominator $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-x-2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-2)(x+1)}$, together the rational function $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P(x)/Q(x)}$.

  • ⁠Polynomial of degree four $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^4-3x^3+x^2+4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-2)^2(x^2+x+1)}$ with a double zero.

Stage 1: What a function is

Definition 1.1 (Function)

Let $A$, $B$ be sets ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{B}$ is possible). A function or map $f:A\to B$ is a rule that assigns to every $a\in A$ a unique $b\in B$:

$$f:\begin{cases}A\longrightarrow B\\ a\longmapsto b=:f(a).\end{cases}$$
(1.1)

$b$ is called the image of $a$, $a$ is called a preimage of $b$. Further,

$$\mathcal D(f):=A,\quad\mathcal W(f):=\{b\in B:\text{there is }a\in A\text{ with }f(a)=b\},\quad\mathcal G(f):=\{(a,f(a)):a\in A\}$$
(1.2)

are called the domain, the range and the graph of $f$; here $\mathcal G(f)\subset A\times B$. For $A,B\subset\mathbb{R}$, $f$ is called a real function. Two functions are equal if their graphs are equal: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g:\Leftrightarrow\mathcal G(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathcal G(g)}$.

Stage 2: Even, monotonic, bounded

Definition 2.1 (Symmetry, monotonicity, boundedness)

Let $f$ be a real function. If $\mathcal D(f)$ contains $-x$ along with $x$, then $f$ is called even if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(-x)}$ for all $x\in\mathcal D(f)$, and odd if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-f(-x)}$ for all $x\in\mathcal D(f)$. Further, $f$ is called, in each case for all $x,y\in\mathcal D(f)$,

  1. monotonically increasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)\le f(y)$;

  2. strictly monotonically increasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)<f(y)$;

  3. monotonically decreasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)\ge f(y)$;

  4. strictly monotonically decreasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)>f(y)$.

$f$ is called bounded above (below) if there is a number $K$ with $f(x)\le K$ ($f(x)\ge K$) for all $x\in\mathcal D(f)$, that is, if the set $\mathcal W(f)$ is bounded above (below). If both hold, $f$ is called bounded.

Theorem 2.2 (Powers are monotonic)

Let $n\in\mathbb{N}$. For odd $n$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^n}$ is strictly monotonically increasing on $\mathbb{R}$; for even $n$, on $\mathbb{R}_0^+$.

Stage 3: Backward: injective, surjective, bijective

Definition 3.1 (Injective, surjective, bijective)

A function $f:A\to B$ is called

  1. injective if for all $x,y\in A$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(y)\Rightarrow x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$;

  2. surjective if for every $y\in B$ there is an $x\in A$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$;

  3. bijective if $f$ is injective and surjective.

Theorem 3.2 (Strictly monotonic implies bijective)

A strictly monotonic real function $f:\mathcal D(f)\to\mathcal W(f)$ is bijective.

Stage 4: Combining and inverting

Definition 4.1 (Combinations)

For real functions $f,g$ let $M:=\mathcal D(f)\cap\mathcal D(g)$ and $M':=\{x\in M:g(x)\neq0\}$. Then the sum, difference and product on $M$ and the quotient on $M'$ are defined by

$$(f\pm g)(x):=f(x)\pm g(x),$$
$$(f\cdot g)(x):=f(x)\cdot g(x),$$
$$\Bigl(\frac fg\Bigr)(x):=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}.$$
(4.1)

For $f:A\to B$ and $g:B\to C$, the composition or successive application $g\circ f:A\to C$ is defined by

$$(g\circ f)(x):=g\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\quad\text{for all }x\in A.$$
(4.2)
Theorem 4.2 (Characterization of injective, surjective, bijective)

Let $f:A\to B$ be a function, $A$ nonempty. Then:

  1. $f$ injective $\Leftrightarrow$ there is $g:B\to A$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(g\circ f)(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ for all $x\in A$ (I).

  2. $f$ surjective $\Leftrightarrow$ there is $g:B\to A$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ for all $y\in B$ (S).

  3. $f$ bijective $\Leftrightarrow$ there is exactly one function $g:B\to A$ with (I) and (S).

Definition 4.3 (Inverse function and root)

The function $g$ from Theorem 4.2(iii) is called the inverse function of $f$, written $f^{-1}:=g$. For all $x\in A$, $y\in B$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y\ \Leftrightarrow\ x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f^{-1}(y),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f\bigl(f^{-1}(y)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y.}$$
(4.3)

The inverse function of $x\mapsto x^n$ (on $\mathbb{R}_0^+$ for even $n$, on $\mathbb{R}$ for odd $n$) is called the $n$th root, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt[n]{y}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y^{1/n}}$.

Theorem 4.4 (Monotonicity is inherited)

Let $f:A\to B$ be real, surjective and strictly monotonically increasing (decreasing). Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f^{-1}:B\to A}$ exists and is likewise strictly monotonically increasing (decreasing).

Stage 5: Polynomials and coefficients

Definition 5.1 (Polynomial)

A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is called a polynomial of degree $n\in\mathbb{N}_0$ if there are real numbers $a_0,\dots,a_n$ with $a_n\neq0$ such that

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\dots+a_1x+a_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=0}^na_ix^i.}$$
(5.1)

One writes $P_n:=f$ and $\deg P_n:=n$. The numbers $a_0,\dots,a_n$ are called the coefficients of $P_n$.

Theorem 5.2 (Identity theorem for polynomials)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{k=0}^na_kx^k}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{k=0}^nb_kx^k}$ be polynomials of degree $\le n$ (so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is allowed). Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q_n(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b_k\text{ for }k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,\dots,n.}$$
(5.2)

Stage 6: Division with remainder

Definition 6.1 (Rational function)

For polynomials $P_n$ and $Q_m$ of degree $n$ and $m$,

$$R(x):=\frac{P_n(x)}{Q_m(x)},$$
$$\mathcal D(R):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:Q_m(x)\neq0\}$$
(6.1)

is called a rational function.

Theorem 6.2 (Division with remainder)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_n(x)/Q_m(x)}$ with $n\ge m\ge1$. Then there are polynomials $S_{n-m}$ and $T_k$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\deg S_{n-m}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n-m}$ such that for all $x\in\mathcal D(R)$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S_{n-m}(x)+\frac{T_k(x)}{Q_m(x)},}$$
(6.2)

where $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\deg T_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k<m}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. After multiplying by $Q_m$ this means for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S_{n-m}(x)\,Q_m(x)+T_k(x).}$$
(6.3)

Stage 7: The Horner scheme

Definition 7.1 (Horner form)

Repeatedly factoring out $x$ turns (5.1) into the Horner form

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl\{\Bigl[\cdots\bigl(\underbrace{\{a_nx+a_{n-1}\}}_{=\,c_{n-1}(x)}\cdot x+a_{n-2}\bigr)\cdot x+\dots+a_2\Bigr]\cdot x+a_1\Bigr\}\cdot x+a_0.}$$
(7.1)

Read from the inside out, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_n}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_i(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c_{i+1}(x)\,x+a_i}$ and at the end $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_0(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_n(x)}$.

Theorem 7.2 (Horner at $x_0$)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{k=0}^na_kx^k}$ be a polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ and $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ fixed. Set

$$c_n:=a_n,$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_i:=c_{i+1}\,x_0+a_i\quad\text{for }i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n-1,n-2,\dots,0.}$$
(7.2)

Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_n(x_0),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-x_0)\,Q_{n-1}(x)+c_0}$$
(7.3)

with $Q_{n-1}(x):=c_nx^{n-1}+c_{n-1}x^{n-2}+\dots+c_2x+c_1$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\deg Q_{n-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n-1}$.

Stage 8: Every zero splits off a linear factor

Definition 8.1 (Zero and multiplicity)

$x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ is called a zero of the real function $f$ if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. $x_0$ is called an $m$-fold zero of the polynomial $P_n$ if there is a polynomial $Q_{n-m}$ with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-x_0)^m\,Q_{n-m}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{n-m}(x_0)\neq0.}$$
(8.1)

$m$ is called the multiplicity of the zero $x_0$.

Theorem 8.2 (Linear factor)

Let $P_n$ be a polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ and $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$. Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-x_0)\,Q_{n-1}(x)\text{ with a polynomial }Q_{n-1}.}$$
(8.2)

Therefore $P_n$ has at most $n$ zeros, even if each is counted with its multiplicity.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_n(x-x_1)(x-x_2)\cdots(x-x_n),}$$
(8.3)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_n(x^2+\beta_1x+\gamma_1)\cdots(x^2+\beta_kx+\gamma_k)\,(x-x_{2k+1})\cdots(x-x_n),}$$
(8.4)
$$\begin{aligned} a_3&=-(x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4), & a_2&=x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_1x_4+x_2x_3+x_2x_4+x_3x_4,\\ a_1&=-(x_1x_2x_3+x_1x_2x_4+x_1x_3x_4+x_2x_3x_4), & a_0&=x_1x_2x_3x_4. \end{aligned}$$
(8.5)

Stage 9: Rational functions and poles

Definition 9.1 (Pole and gap)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_n/Q_m}$. Cancel all common linear factors of numerator and denominator. A point $x_0$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_m(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is called

  • a pole of order $m_0$ if after canceling it is an $m_0$-fold zero of the denominator and not a zero of the numerator;

  • a gap if after canceling it is no longer a zero of the denominator.

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. A function is an assignment that gives each element of its domain (the set of admissible inputs) exactly one image. A formula is only one way of specifying it.

  2. The equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ has exactly one solution for every $y$ if $f$ is bijective, that is, if no $y$ is hit twice and none is missed. Then the inverse function $f^{-1}$ exists, and its graph (the picture of the assignment in the plane) is the graph of $f$ reflected in $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$.

  3. A polynomial is determined by its coefficients (the fixed numbers in front of the powers). Dividing it by $x-x_0$ leaves the remainder $P(x_0)$. Therefore every zero splits off a linear factor, and a polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ zeros.
