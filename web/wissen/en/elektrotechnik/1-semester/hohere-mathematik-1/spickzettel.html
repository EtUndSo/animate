Chapter 1: Sets, Logic and Real Numbers

Key points

Overview: Sets, Logic and Real Numbers

Terms
  • set, statement – A set collects objects; a statement is a sentence that is true or false.
  • axiom – An axiom is a basic assumption that is laid down and not proved.
  • field – Three groups of axioms suffice: A field is a set in which plus and times work according to the familiar rules.
  • order – The order fixes which numbers are positive.
  • Completeness – Completeness says that the number line has no gaps.
  • mathematical induction, absolute value – In addition there are mathematical induction, the proof method for statements about all natural numbers, and the absolute value, the distance of a number from zero.

From the ground up: Number systems and decimal numbers

Terms
  • natural numbers, integers, rational numbers – The symbol $:=$ means “is defined as.” The natural numbers are $\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$, the integers $\mathbb{Z}:=\{0,1,-1,2,-2,\dots\}$, the rational numbers (fractions) $\mathbb{Q}:=\{p/q:p,q\in\mathbb{Z},\ q\ne0\}$.
  • real number – A real number is a number with a decimal representation, that is, with a sign, an $m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}$ and digits $a_1,a_2,\ldots\in\{0,1,\dots,9\}$, finitely or infinitely many.
  • irrational – The numbers in $\mathbb{R}$ that are not fractions are called irrational.
Formulas
  • Definition 0.1 (Real number, provisional) (0.1)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm\Bigl(m+\frac{a_1}{10}+\frac{a_2}{100}+\frac{a_3}{1000}+\dots\Bigr).}$$

Stage 1: Sets and statements

Terms
  • Venn diagram – A Venn diagram (sets drawn as regions) only illustrates; it shows one possible arrangement of the sets (Fig. 1).
  • elements – A set is an unordered collection of definite, distinct objects, its elements.
  • subset
  • superset
  • union
  • intersection
  • difference
  • complement
  • empty set
  • power set
  • disjoint – $A$ and $B$ are called disjoint if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A\cap B}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\emptyset}$.
  • truth table – From statements $\mathcal A$, $\mathcal B$ new ones arise, defined by this truth table.
  • negation, disjunction, conjunction, implication, equivalence – The names are negation, disjunction, conjunction, implication and equivalence.
  • parameter – Statements can depend on a parameter: $\mathcal T(n):=(n\text{ divides }12)$ is true for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ and false for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$.
  • tautology – A tautology is a statement that is true for all truth values of its building blocks.

Stage 2: Quantifiers and indirect proof

Terms
  • universal quantifier, existential quantifier – $\forall$ means “for all” (universal quantifier), $\exists$ “there is at least one” (existential quantifier), $\exists_1$ “there is exactly one,” $\nexists$ “there is no.”
  • contradiction hypothesis – ⁠How to read it: You assume the opposite of the claim (contradiction hypothesis) and reason until something false comes out.
  • Russell's paradox – Why axioms are needed. Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{N:N\notin N\}}$ be the set of all sets that do not contain themselves (Russell's paradox, 1903).
Formulas
  • Theorem 2.2 (Negation rule) (2.1)
    $$\neg\bigl(\forall x\in M:\mathcal A(x)\bigr)\Leftrightarrow\bigl(\exists x\in M:\neg\mathcal A(x)\bigr),\quad \neg\bigl(\exists y\in N:\mathcal B(y)\bigr)\Leftrightarrow\bigl(\forall y\in N:\neg\mathcal B(y)\bigr).$$

Stage 3: Field axioms

Terms
  • operation – An operation assigns to any two elements an element of the same set.
  • distributive law – In addition there are (AM) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x\cdot(y+z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x\cdot y)+(x\cdot z)}$ (distributive law) and (NNE) $0\ne1$.
  • difference – Equations are uniquely solvable: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x+z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ has exactly the solution $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y-x}$ (difference).
  • quotient – $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{xz}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ has, for $x\ne0$, exactly the solution $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y\cdot x^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac yx}$ (quotient).

Stage 6: The completeness axiom

Terms
  • bounded above – $M$ is called bounded above if there is $S\in\mathbb K$ with $x\le S$ for all $x\in M$.
  • upper bound – Every such $S$ is called an upper bound.
  • bounded below, lower bound – Correspondingly bounded below and lower bound with $R\le x$.
  • bounded – Both together are called bounded.
  • supremum – $S$ is called the supremum of $M$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sup M}$, if $S$ is an upper bound and for every upper bound $\tilde S$ we have $S\le\tilde S$ (least upper bound).
  • infimum – $R$ is called the infimum of $M$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\inf M}$, if $R$ is a lower bound and for every lower bound $\tilde R$ we have $\tilde R\le R$ (greatest lower bound).
  • maximum, minimum – If $S$ lies in $M$, it is called the maximum ($\max M$); if $R$ lies in $M$, it is called the minimum ($\min M$).
  • complete – A totally ordered field $\mathbb K$ is called complete if every nonempty set $M\subset\mathbb K$ that is bounded below has a greatest lower bound $\inf M\in\mathbb K$.
  • field of real numbers – There is essentially exactly one totally ordered, complete field that contains $\mathbb{Q}$. It is the field of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$; its order agrees on $\mathbb{Q}$ with the usual one.
  • Theorem of Archimedes: – Theorem of Archimedes: For all $x,y>0$ there is an $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $nx>y$.

Stage 7: Nested intervals and roots

Terms
  • interval, nonnegative numbers – An interval is $[a,b]:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\le x\le b\}$; the nonnegative numbers are $\mathbb{R}_0^+:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\ge0\}$.
  • square root – One writes $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt y}$ (“square root of $y$”).
Formulas

Stage 8: Distance and absolute value

Terms

  • triangle inequality
  • reverse triangle inequality – In addition, $|x-y|\le|x|+|y|$, the reverse triangle inequality $\bigl||x|-|y|\bigr|\le|x\pm y|$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|x|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{x^2}}$ hold.

  • (8.2)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(x,y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d(x-y,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|x-y|,\ \ -|x|\le x\le|x|,\ \ |x|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\max\{x,-x\},\ \ -|x|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\min\{x,-x\}.}$$

Stage 9: Elementary inequalities

Terms
  • Bernoulli – Bernoulli: For $x>-1$, $x\ne0$ and $n\ge2$, $(1+x)^n>1+nx$.
  • Young – Young: For $\varepsilon>0$ and $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, $|xy|\le\frac\varepsilon2x^2+\frac1{2\varepsilon}y^2$.
  • Means – Means: For $x,y\ge0$, $\sqrt{xy}\le\frac{x+y}2$.
  • geometric mean, arithmetic mean – On the left is the geometric mean, on the right the arithmetic mean.
  • Cauchy-Schwarz – Cauchy-Schwarz: For $x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_n\in\mathbb{R}$, with $\|\vec x\|:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}\ge0$, …
  • Minkowski – Minkowski: With $\vec x+\vec y:=(x_1+y_1,\dots,x_n+y_n)$, …
  • vector, norm – ⁠How to read it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1,\dots,x_n)}$ is a vector, a list of $n$ numbers, and $\|\vec x\|$ its norm.
Formulas
  • Theorem 9.1 (Five inequalities) (9.1)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^n|x_iy_i|\le\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ny_i^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\|\vec x\|\cdot\|\vec y\|.}$$
  • Theorem 9.1 (Five inequalities) (9.2)
    $$\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i+y_i)^2}\le\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}+\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ny_i^2},\quad\text{that is,}\quad\|\vec x+\vec y\|\le\|\vec x\|+\|\vec y\|.$$

Key points

Overview: Complex Numbers

Terms
  • real part, imaginary part – In $z_1$, 3 is the real part (the portion without $\mathrm{i}$) and 1 is the imaginary part (the real number in front of $\mathrm{i}$).

Stage 1: Pairs instead of single numbers

Terms
  • standard form – Every complex number has exactly one standard form $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ with $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$.
Formulas

    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x_1+\mathrm{i}y_1)(x_2+\mathrm{i}y_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1x_2-y_1y_2)+\mathrm{i}(x_1y_2+x_2y_1).}$$

Stage 2: The complex plane

Terms
  • real axis, imaginary axis, purely imaginary – The horizontal real axis is $\mathbb{R}$; on the vertical imaginary axis lie the purely imaginary numbers $\mathrm{i}y$.
  • Gaussian plane – The complex plane with the coordinates $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ is also called the Gaussian plane.

Stage 5: Absolute value and distance

Terms
  • distance – The distance $d(z,w)$ depends only on the difference: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(z,w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z-w|}$.

Chapter 3: Functions and Polynomials

Key points

Overview: Functions and Polynomials

Terms
  • function – A temperature sensor delivers exactly one voltage for each temperature. Such an assignment is called a function: Every input $x$ gets exactly one output $f(x)$.
  • inverse function – The inverse function provides the way back as a function in its own right.
  • polynomial – A polynomial is a sum of powers of $x$ with fixed numbers in front.
  • zero, linear factor – A zero is an $x_0$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$; it comes with a linear factor $x-x_0$, a factor of degree one.
  • Horner scheme – The Horner scheme is the computational procedure that splits it off.
  • rational function, pole – Then there are the rational function, a quotient of two polynomials, and the pole, where its denominator becomes zero and its absolute value grows beyond every bound.

From the ground up: Pairs and intervals

Terms
  • Cartesian product – The Cartesian product (also cross product) of two sets is the set of all ordered pairs.
  • Intervals – Intervals are the connected pieces of the number line.
  • open, closed, half-open – For $a\le b$ the bounded sets $(a,b):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x<b\}$ are called open, $[a,b]:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\le x\le b\}$ closed, and $(a,b]$ and $[a,b)$ half-open.

Stage 1: What a function is

Terms
  • map – A function or map $f:A\to B$ is a rule that assigns to every $a\in A$ a unique $b\in B$.
  • image, preimage – $b$ is called the image of $a$, $a$ is called a preimage of $b$.
  • domain, range, graph – … are called the domain, the range and the graph of $f$; here $\mathcal G(f)\subset A\times B$.
  • real function – For $A,B\subset\mathbb{R}$, $f$ is called a real function.
Formulas
  • Definition 1.1 (Function) (1.1)
    $$f:\begin{cases}A\longrightarrow B\\ a\longmapsto b=:f(a).\end{cases}$$
  • Definition 1.1 (Function) (1.2)
    $$\mathcal D(f):=A,\quad\mathcal W(f):=\{b\in B:\text{there is }a\in A\text{ with }f(a)=b\},\quad\mathcal G(f):=\{(a,f(a)):a\in A\}$$

Stage 2: Even, monotonic, bounded

Terms
  • even, odd – If $\mathcal D(f)$ contains $-x$ along with $x$, then $f$ is called even if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(-x)}$ for all $x\in\mathcal D(f)$, and odd if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-f(-x)}$ for all $x\in\mathcal D(f)$.
  • monotonically increasing – … monotonically increasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)\le f(y)$; …
  • strictly monotonically increasing – … strictly monotonically increasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)<f(y)$; …
  • monotonically decreasing – … monotonically decreasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)\ge f(y)$; …
  • strictly monotonically decreasing – … strictly monotonically decreasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)>f(y)$.
  • bounded – $f$ is called bounded above (below) if there is a number $K$ with $f(x)\le K$ ($f(x)\ge K$) for all $x\in\mathcal D(f)$, that is, if the set $\mathcal W(f)$ is bounded above (below).

  • bijective – … bijective if $f$ is injective and surjective.

    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-x_0)\,Q_{n-1}(x)\text{ with a polynomial }Q_{n-1}.}$$
  • (8.3)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_n(x-x_1)(x-x_2)\cdots(x-x_n),}$$
  • (8.4)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_n(x^2+\beta_1x+\gamma_1)\cdots(x^2+\beta_kx+\gamma_k)\,(x-x_{2k+1})\cdots(x-x_n),}$$
  • (8.5)
    $$\begin{aligned} a_3&=-(x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4), & a_2&=x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_1x_4+x_2x_3+x_2x_4+x_3x_4,\\ a_1&=-(x_1x_2x_3+x_1x_2x_4+x_1x_3x_4+x_2x_3x_4), & a_0&=x_1x_2x_3x_4. \end{aligned}$$

Stage 9: Rational functions and poles

Terms
  • pole, order – … a pole of order $m_0$ if after canceling it is an $m_0$-fold zero of the denominator and not a zero of the numerator; …
  • gap – … a gap if after canceling it is no longer a zero of the denominator.
  • asymptote – Because $\deg T<\deg Q$, $T/Q$ becomes arbitrarily small for large $|x|$, since the highest power of the denominator grows faster. So $R$ approaches the polynomial $S$, the asymptote (for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n-m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ a straight line).
