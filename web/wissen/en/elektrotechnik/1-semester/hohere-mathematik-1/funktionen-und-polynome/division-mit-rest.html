Stage 6: Division with remainder

What this stage is about:

Sum, difference and product of polynomials are again polynomials, but the quotient usually is not. Division with remainder decomposes it into a polynomial and a remainder that is as small as possible, as in $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{17:5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ remainder $2$.

The idea. With numbers you subtract multiples of the divisor until the remainder is smaller than the divisor. With polynomials the degree measures the size. You subtract a multiple of the denominator that exactly cancels the highest power of the numerator, and repeat this until the degree of the remainder is smaller than that of the denominator (Fig. 7).

Definition 6.1 (Rational function)

For polynomials $P_n$ and $Q_m$ of degree $n$ and $m$,

$$R(x):=\frac{P_n(x)}{Q_m(x)},$$
$$\mathcal D(R):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:Q_m(x)\neq0\}$$
(6.1)

is called a rational function.

⁠How to read it: The zeros of the denominator do not belong to $\mathcal D(R)$. In the reference model $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ are missing.

Theorem 6.2 (Division with remainder)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_n(x)/Q_m(x)}$ with $n\ge m\ge1$. Then there are polynomials $S_{n-m}$ and $T_k$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\deg S_{n-m}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n-m}$ such that for all $x\in\mathcal D(R)$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S_{n-m}(x)+\frac{T_k(x)}{Q_m(x)},}$$
(6.2)

where $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\deg T_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k<m}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. After multiplying by $Q_m$ this means for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S_{n-m}(x)\,Q_m(x)+T_k(x).}$$
(6.3)
Proof: Division with remainder

Let $a_n$, $b_m\neq0$ be the leading coefficients of $P_n$, $Q_m$. Set

$$\widetilde P(x):=P_n(x)-\frac{a_n}{b_m}x^{n-m}\,Q_m(x).$$

The subtracted term begins with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{a_n}{b_m}x^{n-m}\cdot b_mx^m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_nx^n}$, so $x^n$ cancels, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\widetilde P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ or $\deg\widetilde P\le n-1$. Dividing by $Q_m$ gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{P_n(x)}{Q_m(x)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{a_n}{b_m}x^{n-m}+\frac{\widetilde P(x)}{Q_m(x)}.}$$

If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\widetilde P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ or $\deg\widetilde P<m$, you are done with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\widetilde P}$. Otherwise you make the same step with $\widetilde P$ instead of $P_n$. The degree drops by at least 1 each time, and after at most $n-m+1$ steps the procedure ends. The sum of all prefactors is $S_{n-m}$; the first one determines its degree $n-m$.

⁠How to read it: $S_{n-m}$ is the polynomial part, $T_k$ the remainder. $b_m\neq0$ is needed, because you divide by $b_m$. For $n<m$ there is nothing to divide; then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_n}$. The example $x^2:x$ shows the clean case $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Example

For $P:Q$, $2x\cdot Q$ cancels the term $2x^3$, then $-1\cdot Q$ cancels the term $-x^2$:

$$\begin{array}{lrrrr} & 2x^3 & -3x^2 & -11x & +6\\ -2x\cdot Q: & -2x^3 & +2x^2 & +4x & \\ \hline & & -x^2 & -7x & +6\\ +1\cdot Q: & & +x^2 & -x & -2\\ \hline & & & -8x & +4 \end{array}$$

So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x-1+\dfrac{-8x+4}{x^2-x-2}}$, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n-m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1<2}$. Check by (6.3) at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{5\cdot4+(-20)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P(3)}$.

QuestionWhy may the remainder have degree 1 when $Q$ has degree 2? With numbers the remainder is just a number.

The remainder is a polynomial, and its size is measured by the degree. “Smaller than the divisor” here means: smaller degree, that is, $k<m$. Only for the divisor $x-x_0$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ is the remainder a constant. That is exactly what Stage 8 uses.

Exercise 7 ★★ Division with remainder

(a) Compute $(x^4-3x^2+2x+5):(x^2+x-1)$ with remainder. Watch out for the missing power $x^3$. (b) Check at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$. (c) Limiting case: What does $(x+1):(x^2+1)$ give?

Solution

(a) With $x^4+0x^3-3x^2+2x+5$: Subtracting $x^2\cdot(x^2+x-1)$ leaves $-x^3-2x^2+2x+5$, subtracting $-x\cdot(x^2+x-1)$ leaves $-x^2+x+5$, subtracting $-1\cdot(x^2+x-1)$ leaves $2x+4$. Result: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-x-1}$, remainder $2x+4$. (b) Left $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{16-12+4+5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{13}$. Right $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\cdot5+8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{13}$. (c) Here $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1<m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, there is nothing to divide: polynomial part 0, remainder $x+1$.
