Stage 3: Backward: injective, surjective, bijective

What this stage is about:

Going forward, there is exactly one $y$ for each $x$. Going backward, this guarantee is missing: The equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ can have no solution, one, or many. This stage gives the three cases names.

The idea. Draw a horizontal line through the graph at height $y$. Every intersection point is a solution of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ (Fig. 4). For the parabola $h$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ has two solutions ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$), and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}$ has none. For every $y\in\mathcal W(f)$ there is by definition at least one solution; only, $\mathcal W(f)$ is often unknown.

Definition 3.1 (Injective, surjective, bijective)

A function $f:A\to B$ is called

  1. injective if for all $x,y\in A$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(y)\Rightarrow x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$;

  2. surjective if for every $y\in B$ there is an $x\in A$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$;

  3. bijective if $f$ is injective and surjective.

⁠How to read it: Injective means: Equal images have equal preimages, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ has at most one solution for each $y$, and the horizontal line meets the graph at most once. Surjective means $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal W(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{B}$: At least one solution for every $y\in B$. Bijective means: exactly one. Validity: These properties belong to the function together with $A$ and $B$, not to the formula:

Theorem 3.2 (Strictly monotonic implies bijective)

A strictly monotonic real function $f:\mathcal D(f)\to\mathcal W(f)$ is bijective.

Proof: Strictly monotonic implies bijective

$f$ is surjective by definition of $\mathcal W(f)$. For injectivity we show the equivalent contrapositive (Chapter 1): From $x\neq y$ follows $f(x)\neq f(y)$. Let $x<y$ (otherwise swap the names). If $f$ is strictly increasing, then $f(x)<f(y)$; if $f$ is strictly decreasing, then $f(x)>f(y)$. In both cases $f(x)\neq f(y)$.

⁠How to read it: The target here is exactly $\mathcal W(f)$. Why “strictly” is needed: On the horizontal piece in Fig. 3, many $x$ have the same image. The converse does not hold: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/x}$ on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ is bijective onto $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$, but not monotonic, because $-1<1$ gives $f(-1)<f(1)$, and $1<2$ gives $f(1)>f(2)$.

Examples

By Theorems 2.2 and 3.2, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^n}$ is bijective onto its range, namely $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal W(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbb{R}_0^+}$ for even $n$ on $\mathbb{R}_0^+$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal W(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbb{R}}$ for odd $n$ on $\mathbb{R}$. That every $y$ really is hit follows, as for $\sqrt y$, from the completeness of the real numbers (Chapter 1). Without monotonicity one computes directly: $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x-5}$ is injective, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2x-5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2y-5}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$, and surjective, because for given $y$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(y+5)/2}$ solves the equation.

⁠Limiting case: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ is bijective; every height is hit exactly at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$.

QuestionCan every non-injective function be made injective?

Yes, by shrinking the domain. That changes the function, and you must say which piece you keep: $h$ on $[2,\infty)$ and $h$ on $(-\infty,2]$ are both injective and have different inverses. This is also how $\sqrt y$ arises from $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}_0^+$.

Exercise 3 ★★ How many solutions?

(a) For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+6x+5}$: Choose $A$ with $0\in A$ and $B$ such that $g:A\to B$ is bijective, and give the solution of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$. (b) Prediction without calculation: Is $f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2n}$ injective? Surjective?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x+3)^2-4}$ is strictly increasing on $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{[-3,\infty)}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{B}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{[-4,\infty)}$. From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x+3)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y+4}$ and $x+3\ge0$ follows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3+\sqrt{y+4}}$. Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. (b) Injective, yes: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2m\Rightarrow n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m}$. Surjective, no: $3$ is odd and has no preimage.
