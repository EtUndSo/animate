Stage 8: Every zero splits off a linear factor

What this stage is about:

Now the question from the beginning can be answered: Why does a factor belong to every solution of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and how many solutions can there be?

The idea. Theorem 7.2 writes every polynomial as $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-x_0)\,Q(x)+c_0}$. Substituting $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_0}$ makes the first term vanish, because its factor $x-x_0$ is zero. What remains is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c_0}$. If $x_0$ is a zero, the remainder is zero, and $x-x_0$ is a factor of $P$. In short: The remainder of the division by $x-x_0$ is the function value at $x_0$.

Definition 8.1 (Zero and multiplicity)

$x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ is called a zero of the real function $f$ if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. $x_0$ is called an $m$-fold zero of the polynomial $P_n$ if there is a polynomial $Q_{n-m}$ with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-x_0)^m\,Q_{n-m}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{n-m}(x_0)\neq0.}$$
(8.1)

$m$ is called the multiplicity of the zero $x_0$.

⁠How to read it: $m$ counts how many times the factor $x-x_0$ is contained in $P_n$. We have $m\le n$, because the degree $n-m$ of $Q_{n-m}$ cannot be negative.

Theorem 8.2 (Linear factor)

Let $P_n$ be a polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ and $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$. Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-x_0)\,Q_{n-1}(x)\text{ with a polynomial }Q_{n-1}.}$$
(8.2)

Therefore $P_n$ has at most $n$ zeros, even if each is counted with its multiplicity.

Proof: Linear factor

“$\Rightarrow$”: In (7.3), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_n(x_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. “$\Leftarrow$”: Substituting $x_0$ gives the factor 0. Number: A product is zero if and only if one factor is zero. So every further zero of $P_n$ is a zero of $Q_{n-1}$. With each zero split off, the degree drops by 1, and after at most $n$ steps a constant $a_n\neq0$ without a zero remains.

⁠How to read it: The linear factor $x-x_0$ has degree 1 and is zero exactly at $x_0$. Why $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ is needed: $x^2+1$ has no real zero and no real linear factor. Consequence for the identity theorem: If two polynomials of degree $\le n$ agree at $n+1$ points, they are equal, because otherwise their difference would have too many zeros.

Two representations. Splitting off all zeros produces a form from which they can be read off directly:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_n(x-x_1)(x-x_2)\cdots(x-x_n),}$$
(8.3)

with $a_n\neq0$ and the zeros $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb{R}$. A multiple zero appears several times in the list. If a remaining factor has no real zero left, quadratic factors stay:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_n(x^2+\beta_1x+\gamma_1)\cdots(x^2+\beta_kx+\gamma_k)\,(x-x_{2k+1})\cdots(x-x_n),}$$
(8.4)

where $\beta_i^2-4\gamma_i<0$, so the quadratic factors have no real zeros. The fixed numbers $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_k,\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_k,x_{2k+1},\dots,x_n$ are $n$ in number, like the coefficients $a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}$. The $n-2k$ numbers $x_i$ are the zeros. Conversely, these numbers determine $P_n$ uniquely by multiplying out; for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ in (8.3),

$$\begin{aligned} a_3&=-(x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4), & a_2&=x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_1x_4+x_2x_3+x_2x_4+x_3x_4,\\ a_1&=-(x_1x_2x_3+x_1x_2x_4+x_1x_3x_4+x_2x_3x_4), & a_0&=x_1x_2x_3x_4. \end{aligned}$$
(8.5)

Not every real polynomial has the form (8.3), as $x^2+1$ shows. That the form (8.4) always exists is harder and is not proved here.

Going deeper, not needed for the rest: Why quadratic factors suffice

In the complex numbers (Chapter 2), every polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ has a zero (fundamental theorem of algebra). With real coefficients, along with $z$ also $\overline{z}$ is a zero, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x-z)(x-\overline{z})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-2\operatorname{Re}(z)\,x+|z|^2}$ is real. For $x^2+x+1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,\mathrm{i}}$.

Example

For $S$, the scheme at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ gives the row $1,-1,-1,-2,0$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-2)(x^3-x^2-x-2)}$. Again at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$: $1,1,1,0$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-2)^2(x^2+x+1)}$. The third time: $1,3,7$; the remainder $7\neq0$ ends the search. The multiplicity is 2, and because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1-4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3<0}$, the form (8.4) has been reached, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$.

⁠Limiting case: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_n(x-x_0)^n}$, the polynomial with only one zero.

What the multiplicity does. Near $x_0$, $Q_{n-m}(x)$ does not change its sign, since it is not zero there. The sign of $P_n$ is therefore determined by $(x-x_0)^m$: For odd $m$ it changes, and the graph crosses the axis. For even $m$ it does not change, and the graph only touches it (Fig. 9).

QuestionI find a zero. How do I know whether there are more?

The quotient tells you. After splitting off, a polynomial of lower degree is left, and every further zero of $P$ lies in it. At degree 2 the quadratic formula helps, for $P$, say, $2x^2+3x-2$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{25}$ and the zeros $\frac12$ and $-2$. At higher degree you keep trying in the quotient. With integer coefficients and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, only the divisors of $a_0$ come into question as integer zeros, because $a_0$ is, up to sign, the product of the zeros.

Exercise 9 ★★ Factoring

(a) Show that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ is a zero of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^4-x^3-3x^2+5x-2}$, and determine its multiplicity and the form (8.3). (b) Bring $x^3-8$ into the form (8.4). (c) Prediction: A polynomial of degree 5 contains two quadratic factors without a real zero. How many real zeros does it have?

Solution

(a) Scheme at 1: $1,0,-3,2,0$, then $1,1,-2,0$, then $1,2,0$, then $1,3$ with remainder $3\neq0$. Multiplicity 3, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-1)^3(x+2)}$. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ is a zero; the scheme with $1,0,0,-8$ gives $1,2,4,0$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^3-8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta^2-4\gamma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4-16}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-12<0}$. (c) The two factors together have degree 4, so one linear factor remains: exactly one real zero.
