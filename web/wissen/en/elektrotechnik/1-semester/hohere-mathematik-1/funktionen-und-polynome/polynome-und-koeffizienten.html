Stage 5: Polynomials and coefficients

What this stage is about:

The general theory is in place. Now comes the most important family of functions: polynomials, built only from addition and multiplication. The first problem is: The same polynomial can look quite different. When are two polynomials equal?

The idea. A polynomial is fixed by finitely many numbers. Near $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ you see them one after another: The value at zero is the last number, the slope there the next one, because the higher powers are tiny for small $|x|$ (Fig. 6). Two polynomials that are equal everywhere must therefore agree number by number.

Definition 5.1 (Polynomial)

A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is called a polynomial of degree $n\in\mathbb{N}_0$ if there are real numbers $a_0,\dots,a_n$ with $a_n\neq0$ such that

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\dots+a_1x+a_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=0}^na_ix^i.}$$
(5.1)

One writes $P_n:=f$ and $\deg P_n:=n$. The numbers $a_0,\dots,a_n$ are called the coefficients of $P_n$.

⁠How to read it: The index on $P_n$ names the degree. For all $x$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, so $a_0$ is the term without $x$. In the reference model $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-11}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$; for $S$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Always $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal D(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbb{R}}$, but $\mathcal W(f)$ can be smaller ($x^2$). A constant $c\neq0$ has degree 0. The function $0$ has no such representation and therefore no degree in this definition.

Theorem 5.2 (Identity theorem for polynomials)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{k=0}^na_kx^k}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{k=0}^nb_kx^k}$ be polynomials of degree $\le n$ (so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is allowed). Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_n(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q_n(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b_k\text{ for }k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,\dots,n.}$$
(5.2)
Proof: Identity theorem

“$\Leftarrow$”: Equal coefficients give equal values.

“$\Rightarrow$”: At $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b_0}$. Subtracting and dividing by $x\neq0$ gives for all $x\neq0$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_1+a_2x+\dots+a_nx^{n-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b_1+b_2x+\dots+b_nx^{n-1}.}$$

Here you may not substitute $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, because you divided by $x$. Instead you use small $x$: From the equation follows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_1-b_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x\bigl[(b_2-a_2)+(b_3-a_3)x+\dots+(b_n-a_n)x^{n-2}\bigr]}$. For $0<|x|\le1$, all powers of $|x|$ are at most 1, so the triangle inequality $|u+v|\le|u|+|v|$ gives

$$|a_1-b_1|\le C\,|x|\quad\text{with}\quad C:=\sum_{k=2}^n\bigl(|a_k|+|b_k|\bigr).$$

$C$ depends only on the coefficients, and $|x|$ may be arbitrarily small. So $|a_1-b_1|<\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. A number $a$ with $|a|<\varepsilon$ for all $\varepsilon>0$ is zero, because $|a|>0$ would give, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|a|/2}$, the contradiction $|a|<|a|/2$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b_1}$. Subtracting $a_0+a_1x$ and dividing by $x^2$ likewise gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b_2}$, and so on up to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b_n}$.

⁠How to read it: The theorem is the basis of comparing coefficients: To compare two polynomials as functions, you compare their coefficients. Polynomials of different degree are therefore always different. Why “for all $x$” is needed: $x^2-x$ and $0$ agree at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, but the coefficients do not.

Example

When is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{ax^2+bx+c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a(x-x_1)(x-x_2)}$ for all $x$? Multiplying out gives $ax^2-a(x_1+x_2)x+ax_1x_2$ on the right, so comparing coefficients gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-a(x_1+x_2),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\,x_1x_2.}$$
(5.3)

For $a\neq0$ and $D:=b^2-4ac\ge0$, these two equations are solved by the quadratic formula

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_{1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{-b+\sqrt D}{2a},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_{2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{-b-\sqrt D}{2a}.}$$
(5.4)

Substituting confirms it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-a(x_1+x_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-a\cdot\frac{-2b}{2a}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{ax_1x_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{4a}\bigl(b^2-D\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c}$. The number $D$ is called the discriminant. For $Q$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_{1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_{2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1+x_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-b/a}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1x_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c/a}$.

⁠Limiting cases: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_2}$. For $D<0$ there is no real zero, because then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{ax^2+bx+c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\bigl[(x+\frac{b}{2a})^2-\frac{D}{4a^2}\bigr]}$, and the bracket is positive. So $x^2+\beta x+\gamma$ has no real zero if and only if $\beta^2-4\gamma<0$.

QuestionWhy doesn't the proof simply substitute $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ after dividing by $x$? There is a polynomial there again, after all.

There are polynomials on the left and right, that is true. But the equation was derived only for $x\neq0$, and nothing about the point 0 follows from that immediately. The detour via small $|x|$ closes exactly this gap. Chapter 5 gives the concept behind it the name continuity.

Exercise 6 ★★ Comparing coefficients

(a) Determine $a,b,c$ such that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2x^2-x+3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a(x-1)^2+b(x-1)+c}$ for all $x$. (b) Let $x^3+a_2x^2+a_1x+a_0$ have the zeros $-1$, $2$, $4$. Determine the coefficients by multiplying out and express them in terms of the zeros.

Solution

(a) On the right stands $ax^2+(b-2a)x+(a-b+c)$. Comparison: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b-4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2-3+c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x+1)(x-2)(x-4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^3-5x^2+2x+8}$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-(x_1+x_2+x_3)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-x_1x_2x_3}$.
