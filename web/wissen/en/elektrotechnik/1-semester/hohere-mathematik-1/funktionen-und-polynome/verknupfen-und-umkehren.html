What this stage is about:

Stage 3 says when $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ has exactly one solution. This stage turns the solution into a function. For that, one must be able to apply functions one after the other.

The idea. The inverse function undoes what $f$ does: First $f$, then $g$ leads back to the start, and first $g$, then $f$ likewise (Fig. 5, left). New functions arise from functions in this way, just as new numbers arise from numbers by calculation.

Definition 4.1 (Combinations)

For real functions $f,g$ let $M:=\mathcal D(f)\cap\mathcal D(g)$ and $M':=\{x\in M:g(x)\neq0\}$. Then the sum, difference and product on $M$ and the quotient on $M'$ are defined by

$$(f\pm g)(x):=f(x)\pm g(x),$$
$$(f\cdot g)(x):=f(x)\cdot g(x),$$
$$\Bigl(\frac fg\Bigr)(x):=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}.$$
(4.1)

For $f:A\to B$ and $g:B\to C$, the composition or successive application $g\circ f:A\to C$ is defined by

$$(g\circ f)(x):=g\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\quad\text{for all }x\in A.$$
(4.2)

⁠How to read it: $g\circ f$ is read “$g$ after $f$”: $f$ acts first, $f(x)$ lies in $B$, and there $g$ may act. The graph is $\{(x,y)\in A\times C:\text{there is }z\in B\text{ with }f(x)=z,\ g(z)=y\}$. The order counts: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+1}$, we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(g\circ f)(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$. Sum and product are computed point by point, for example $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(h/b)(2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1/0.2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-5}$.

Theorem 4.2 (Characterization of injective, surjective, bijective)

Let $f:A\to B$ be a function, $A$ nonempty. Then:

  1. $f$ injective $\Leftrightarrow$ there is $g:B\to A$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(g\circ f)(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ for all $x\in A$ (I).

  2. $f$ surjective $\Leftrightarrow$ there is $g:B\to A$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ for all $y\in B$ (S).

  3. $f$ bijective $\Leftrightarrow$ there is exactly one function $g:B\to A$ with (I) and (S).

Proof: Characterization

(i) “$\Leftarrow$”: From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x_1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(x_2)}$ follows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(f(x_1))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(f(x_2))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_2}$. “$\Rightarrow$” as an idea: $g$ gives each $y\in\mathcal W(f)$ its only preimage and all other $y$ a fixed value from $A$.

(ii) “$\Leftarrow$”: For $y\in B$ set $z:=g(y)\in A$; then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(f\circ g)(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$, so $y\in\mathcal W(f)$. “$\Rightarrow$” as an idea: For each $y$, $g$ chooses one of its preimages.

(iii) “$\Leftarrow$”: (I) gives injective, (S) gives surjective. “$\Rightarrow$”: Bijective means that each $y$ has exactly one preimage $g(y)$; this $g$ satisfies (I) and (S). If $\hat g$ satisfies both as well, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat g(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat g\bigl(f(g(y))\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(y)}$ by (S) for $g$ and (I) for $\hat g$.

⁠How to read it: Memory aid: “Surjective” is about the images, so (S) stands for all $y\in B$. Why “bijective” is needed for uniqueness: $h:\mathbb{R}\to[-1,\infty)$ is only surjective, and both $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_1(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+\sqrt{y+1}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_2(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2-\sqrt{y+1}}$ satisfy (S). $h:[2,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ is only injective, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+\sqrt{y+1}}$ for $y\ge-1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c}$ for $y<-1$ satisfies (I) for every fixed $c\ge2$.

Definition 4.3 (Inverse function and root)

The function $g$ from Theorem 4.2(iii) is called the inverse function of $f$, written $f^{-1}:=g$. For all $x\in A$, $y\in B$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y\ \Leftrightarrow\ x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f^{-1}(y),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f\bigl(f^{-1}(y)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y.}$$
(4.3)

The inverse function of $x\mapsto x^n$ (on $\mathbb{R}_0^+$ for even $n$, on $\mathbb{R}$ for odd $n$) is called the $n$th root, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt[n]{y}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y^{1/n}}$.

⁠How to read it: The first statement says: $f^{-1}$ solves the equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$. Proof: From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ follows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(f(x))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(y)}$ by (I); from $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ follows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(g(y))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(x)}$ by (S). For the root the two other statements read $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt[n]{y^n}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\sqrt[n]{y})^n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$. Caution: $f^{-1}(y)$ is not $1/f(y)$. For $h$ on $[2,\infty)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h^{-1}(8)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/h(8)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/35}$.

The graph. By (4.3), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal G(f)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{(x,f(x))\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{(g(y),y):y\in B\}}$, while $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal G(g)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{(y,g(y)):y\in B\}}$. The pairs are swapped, and swapping the coordinates is the reflection in the angle bisector $\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:x=y\}$ (Fig. 5, right).

Theorem 4.4 (Monotonicity is inherited)

Let $f:A\to B$ be real, surjective and strictly monotonically increasing (decreasing). Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f^{-1}:B\to A}$ exists and is likewise strictly monotonically increasing (decreasing).

Proof: Monotonicity is inherited

For increasing $f$ (decreasing works the same way): $f$ is injective as in Theorem 3.2 and surjective, so $g$ exists. Let $y_1<y_2$. If $g(y_1)\ge g(y_2)$ held, applying $f$ would give the contradiction $y_1\ge y_2$. So $g(y_1)<g(y_2)$.

⁠How to read it and why the hypotheses are needed: Without “surjective,” $g$ is missing for some $y\in B$. Without monotonicity there is nothing to inherit either. Application: $\sqrt[n]{y}$ is strictly monotonically increasing. Limiting case: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f}$; the graph lies on the mirror axis.

QuestionWhy does (4.3) not say $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{y^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ for all $y$ in the case of the root?

Because the inversion belongs only to the function on $\mathbb{R}_0^+$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{(-3)^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, so in general $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{y^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|y|}$. The error arises when you forget the domain on which $f$ is invertible.

Exercise 4 ★★ Finding an inverse function

Let $f:[2,\infty)\to(2,5]$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{2x+1}{x-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+\dfrac{3}{x-1}}$. (a) Show that $f$ is strictly decreasing. (b) Determine $f^{-1}$. (c) Compare $f^{-1}(4)$ with $1/f(4)$. (d) Prediction: Is $f^{-1}$ increasing or decreasing? Check with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$.

Solution

(a) From $2\le x_1<x_2$ follows $0<x_1-1<x_2-1$, so $\frac{3}{x_1-1}>\frac{3}{x_2-1}$ and $f(x_1)>f(x_2)$. (b) From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+\frac{3}{x-1}}$ follows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x-1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{3}{y-2}}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+\frac{3}{y-2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{y+1}{y-2}}$ for $y\in(2,5]$. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}(4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac52}$ (check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\frac52)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6/1.5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$), whereas $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/f(4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13}$. (d) Decreasing by Theorem 4.4: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}(3)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4>f^{-1}(4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac52}$.

Exercise 5 ★★★ Inverting a chain

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x+1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^3}$ on $\mathbb{R}$. (a) Compute $(g\circ f)(1)$ and $(f\circ g)(1)$. (b) Show in general for bijective $f:A\to B$, $g:B\to C$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(g\circ f)^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}}$. Why does the order reverse? (c) Determine $(g\circ f)^{-1}(y)$ and check with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{27}$.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(g\circ f)(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(3)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{27}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}\bigl(g^{-1}(g(f(x)))\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f^{-1}(f(x))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$, likewise in the other order for $y\in C$. So (I) and (S) hold, and by Theorem 4.2(iii) there is only one such function. The way back must undo the last step first. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g^{-1}(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt[3]{y}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f^{-1}(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{y-1}{2}}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(g\circ f)^{-1}(y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\sqrt[3]{y}-1}{2}}$. At $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{27}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(3-1)/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$.
