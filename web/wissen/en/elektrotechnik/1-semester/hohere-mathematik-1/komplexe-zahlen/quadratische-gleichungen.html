What this stage is about:

The goal was a solution of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. This stage shows that $\mathbb{C}$ achieves more: Every quadratic equation is solvable there, even with complex coefficients.

The idea. Completing the square uses only field axioms and therefore works in $\mathbb{C}$. What remains is an equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D}$. In $\mathbb{R}$ it fails for $D<0$. In $\mathbb{C}$ it never fails: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D}$ splits into real part and imaginary part, that is, into two real equations for the two real unknowns of $w$.

Theorem 6.1 (Square roots in $\mathbb{C}$)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s+\mathrm{i}t\ne0}$ with $s,t\in\mathbb{R}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u+\mathrm{i}v}$ with real $u,v$, the equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D}$ is equivalent to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2-v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2uv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t}$. There are exactly two solutions $\pm w$, namely with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{|D|+s}2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{|D|-s}2.}$$
(6.1)

The signs of $u$ and $v$ must be chosen so that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2uv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t}$ holds. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is the only solution.

⁠How to read it: $s$ and $t$ are the real part and imaginary part of $D$. If $t>0$, $u$ and $v$ have the same sign; if $t<0$, opposite signs. Limiting case: For real $D<0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-D}$: The solutions are purely imaginary, so for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ they are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm\mathrm{i}}$.

Proof: Two real equations

We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u^2-v^2+2\mathrm{i}uv}$. Comparing real part and imaginary part gives the two equations. From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|w|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|w^2|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|D|}$ it also follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2+v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|D|}$. Adding and subtracting with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2-v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s}$ gives (6.1). The right-hand sides are nonnegative, because $|s|\le|D|$ (Stage 5, rule (v)). Conversely, $u,v$ from (6.1) satisfy the equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2-v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(2uv)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(|D|+s)(|D|-s)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|D|^2-s^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t^2}$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2uv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm t}$, and the choice of signs fixes $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2uv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t}$.

Theorem 6.2 (Quadratic equation in $\mathbb{C}$)

Let $p,q\in\mathbb{C}$ and $D:=p^2/4-q$. The equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2+pz+q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ has the solutions

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac p2\pm w}$$
$$\text{with}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D.}$$
(6.2)

For $D\ne0$ these are two solutions $z_+\ne z_-$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_++z_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_+z_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ there is only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p/2}$, a double solution. For real $p,q$: $D>0$ gives two real solutions, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ one real solution, $D<0$ the mutually conjugate solutions $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac p2\pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{-D}}$.

⁠How to read it: $D$ is called the discriminant. The solutions lie point-symmetrically about the center $-p/2$ (Fig. 6). Validity: The coefficient of $z^2$ must be 1. Otherwise divide by it first; in $\mathbb{C}$ this also works with a complex coefficient $\ne0$. Real equations of this kind describe oscillations: A conjugate pair of solutions stands for a damped oscillation.

Derivation: Completing the square

We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2+pz+q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(z+\frac p2)^2-\frac{p^2}4+q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(z+\frac p2)^2-w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(z+\frac p2-w)(z+\frac p2+w)}$. A product is zero only if one factor is zero (Stage 4). This gives (6.2), and it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_++z_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_+z_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{p^2}4-w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q}$. For real $p,q$, $D$ is real, and for $D<0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{-D}}$.

Example 1real coefficients

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2-6z+10}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-6}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9-10}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\pm\mathrm{i}}$. These are $z_1$ and $\overline{z_1}$. Check: sum $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$, product $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z_1|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q}$.

Example 2complex coefficients

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2-(4+3\mathrm{i})z+1+7\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-p/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+1.5\mathrm{i}}$ and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2+1.5\mathrm{i})^2-(1+7\mathrm{i})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.75+6\mathrm{i}-1-7\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.75-\mathrm{i}.}$$

So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.75}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|D|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{0.5625+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.25}$. From (6.1) follow $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25}$. Because $t<0$, $u$ and $v$ have opposite signs: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm(1-0.5\mathrm{i})}$. Thus $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+1.5\mathrm{i}\pm(1-0.5\mathrm{i})}$, that is, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3+\mathrm{i}}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+2\mathrm{i}}$. Check: sum $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4+3\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$, product $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1+7\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q}$.

⁠Limiting case: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, (6.2) gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p/2\pm p/2}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$, matching $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z(z+p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

QuestionWhy can't you calculate $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{-1}\cdot\sqrt{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{(-1)(-1)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$?

The rule $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{ab}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt a\sqrt b}$ from Chapter 1 holds only for $a,b\ge0$. For negative numbers the root is not defined: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ has two solutions of equal standing, and neither of them is “the” root. That is why this chapter writes $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D}$ and not $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt D}$.

Exercise 8 ★★ Solving quadratic equations

(a) Solve $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2+4z+13}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and check sum and product. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2+pz+q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ with real $p,q$ has the solution $2-5\mathrm{i}$. What is the second solution, and what are $p$ and $q$? (c) Solve $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2-3z+3-\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and check the result. (d) Solve $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}z^2+2z-2\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4-13}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-9}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2\pm3\mathrm{i}}$. Sum $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$, product $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4+9}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{13}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q}$. (b) With real coefficients the second solution is the conjugate one, $2+5\mathrm{i}$. Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-(2-5\mathrm{i})-(2+5\mathrm{i})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2-5\mathrm{i})(2+5\mathrm{i})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{29}$.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3-\mathrm{i}}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.25-3+\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.75+\mathrm{i}}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|D|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{0.5625+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.25}$, (6.1) gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. Because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1>0}$, $u$ and $v$ have the same sign: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm(0.5+\mathrm{i})}$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5\pm(0.5+\mathrm{i})}$, that is, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+\mathrm{i}}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\mathrm{i}}$. Check: sum $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$, product $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(2+\mathrm{i})(1-\mathrm{i})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2-2\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{i}-\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3-\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q}$.

(d) First bring the coefficient of $z^2$ to 1: Dividing by $\mathrm{i}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mathrm{i}}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2-2\mathrm{i}z-2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-2\mathrm{i})^2/4+2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1+2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm1}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{i}\pm1}$. Check for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+\mathrm{i}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}\cdot2\mathrm{i}+2+2\mathrm{i}-2\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2+2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Check for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1+\mathrm{i}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}\cdot(-2\mathrm{i})-2+2\mathrm{i}-2\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2-2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Outlook. A point of the plane can also be specified by absolute value and angle. Then multiplication multiplies the absolute values and adds the angles; the rotation from Stage 2 is the special case $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\mathrm{i}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and angle $90^\circ$. This polar representation, the exponential form and the $n$th roots need sine, cosine and the exponential function and therefore follow in Chapter 7.

Theorem 6.1 (Square roots in $\mathbb{C}$)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s+\mathrm{i}t\ne0}$ with $s,t\in\mathbb{R}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u+\mathrm{i}v}$ with real $u,v$, the equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D}$ is equivalent to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2-v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2uv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t}$. There are exactly two solutions $\pm w$, namely with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{|D|+s}2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{|D|-s}2.}$$
(6.1)

The signs of $u$ and $v$ must be chosen so that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2uv}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t}$ holds. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is the only solution.

Theorem 6.2 (Quadratic equation in $\mathbb{C}$)

Let $p,q\in\mathbb{C}$ and $D:=p^2/4-q$. The equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2+pz+q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ has the solutions

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac p2\pm w}$$
$$\text{with}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{D.}$$
(6.2)

For $D\ne0$ these are two solutions $z_+\ne z_-$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_++z_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_+z_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ there is only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-p/2}$, a double solution. For real $p,q$: $D>0$ gives two real solutions, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ one real solution, $D<0$ the mutually conjugate solutions $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac p2\pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{-D}}$.
