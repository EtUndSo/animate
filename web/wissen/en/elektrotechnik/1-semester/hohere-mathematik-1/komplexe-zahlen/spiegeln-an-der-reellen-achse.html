Stage 3: Reflecting across the real axis

What this stage is about:

You cannot yet divide by $x+\mathrm{i}y$. This stage introduces the mirror image of $z$, which turns any number into a real one by multiplication, and collects the rules of arithmetic.

The idea. $-\mathrm{i}$ also solves $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(-\mathrm{i})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. The rules of arithmetic cannot tell $\mathrm{i}$ and $-\mathrm{i}$ apart. That is why a correct calculation stays correct when $\mathrm{i}$ is swapped for $-\mathrm{i}$ everywhere. In the plane this swap is the reflection across the real axis (Fig. 3). If you multiply $z$ by its mirror image, the cross terms with $\mathrm{i}$ cancel.

Definition 3.1 (Complex conjugate)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$, the number $\overline{z}:=x-\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{C}$ is called the conjugate of $z$.

Theorem 3.2 (Rules of arithmetic in $\mathbb{C}$)

For all $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u+\mathrm{i}v}$ with real $x,y,u,v$:

  1. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z+w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Re}(z)+\operatorname{Re}(w)}$,

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Im}(z+w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Im}(z)+\operatorname{Im}(w)}$,

  3. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z+w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}+\overline{w}}$,

  4. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z\cdot w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}\cdot\overline{w}}$,

  5. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{\overline{z}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z}$,

  6. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+y^2}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Im}(z\overline{z})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z\overline{z})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+y^2}$,

  7. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z+\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\operatorname{Re}(z)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z-\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\mathrm{i}\operatorname{Im}(z)}$.

⁠How to read it: Real part, imaginary part and conjugation may be pulled into sums. Conjugation may also be pulled into products; that is the swap $\mathrm{i}\leftrightarrow-\mathrm{i}$. The real part may not: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z_1z_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z_1)\operatorname{Re}(z_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, because $-y_1y_2$ also enters the real part of the product. In electrical engineering, $z^*$ is often written instead of $\overline{z}$.

Proof: Rules of arithmetic

(i), (ii): Addition works separately, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z+w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x+u)+\mathrm{i}(y+v)}$.

(iii): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z+w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x+u)-\mathrm{i}(y+v)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-\mathrm{i}y)+(u-\mathrm{i}v)}$.

(iv): By (1.3), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{zw}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(xu-yv)+\mathrm{i}(xv+uy)}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{zw}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(xu-yv)-\mathrm{i}(xv+uy)}$. The same formula with $-y$ and $-v$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z}\,\overline{w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(xu-yv)+\mathrm{i}(-xv-uy)}$. The two agree.

(v): Change the sign of $y$ twice.

(vi): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x+\mathrm{i}y)(x-\mathrm{i}y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-\mathrm{i}xy+\mathrm{i}xy-\mathrm{i}^2y^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+y^2}$.

(vii): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x+\mathrm{i}y)+(x-\mathrm{i}y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x+\mathrm{i}y)-(x-\mathrm{i}y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\mathrm{i}y}$.

Example

For $z_1$, we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3-\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1\overline{z_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1+\overline{z_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot3}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1-\overline{z_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\mathrm{i}\cdot1}$. Check of (iv): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z_1z_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-7\mathrm{i}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z_1}\,\overline{z_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3-\mathrm{i})(1-2\mathrm{i})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3-6\mathrm{i}-\mathrm{i}+2\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-7\mathrm{i}}$.

⁠Limiting case: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2}$. Real numbers lie on the mirror axis and do not change.

QuestionIsn't $z\overline{z}$ the same as $z^2$?

Only for real $z$. In $z^2$ the cross terms add up to $2\mathrm{i}xy$; in $z\overline{z}$ they cancel. In the reference model, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3+4\mathrm{i}}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_2\overline{z_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$.

Exercise 3 ★ Real or purely imaginary?

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2-5\mathrm{i}}$. (a) Predict without calculating which of the numbers $z+\overline{z}$, $z-\overline{z}$, $z\overline{z}$, $z^2+\overline{z}^2$, $(z-\overline{z})^2$ are real and which are purely imaginary. Then calculate them. (b) Show for every $z\in\mathbb{C}$: $z$ is real if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z}$, and purely imaginary if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-z}$.

Solution

(a) Prediction: $z+\overline{z}$ is real, $z-\overline{z}$ purely imaginary, $z\overline{z}$ real, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2+\overline{z}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z^2+\overline{z^2}}$ real (sum of a number and its mirror image), $(z-\overline{z})^2$ real (square of a purely imaginary number). Calculation: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z+\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z-\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-10\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4+25}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{29}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4-20\mathrm{i}+25\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-21-20\mathrm{i}}$ we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2+\overline{z}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-42}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(z-\overline{z})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-10\mathrm{i})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-100}$.

(b) By rule (vii) from Stage 3, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z}$ is equivalent to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\mathrm{i}\operatorname{Im}(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, that is, to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Im}(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Likewise, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-z}$ is equivalent to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\operatorname{Re}(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, that is, to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.
