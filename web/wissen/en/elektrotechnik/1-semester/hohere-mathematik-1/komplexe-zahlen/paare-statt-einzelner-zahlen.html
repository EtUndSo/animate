What this stage is about:

In $\mathbb{R}$, the equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ has no solution. This stage builds a larger set of numbers in which the equation is solvable and which contains $\mathbb{R}$ unchanged.

The idea. Every extension of the numbers solves an equation that had no solution before: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ leads from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{Z}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\mathbb{R}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ the case is different. The parabola $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2}$ never reaches the height $-1$ (Fig. 1), because $x^2\ge0$ holds in $\mathbb{R}$ (Chapter 1). No point is missing between other points, as with $\sqrt2$; instead, every point of the number line is ruled out. The solution must lie beside the line. That is why a second coordinate is added: You calculate with pairs $(x,y)$. The pairs $(x,0)$ are to remain the real numbers, and $(0,1)$ is to have the square $(-1,0)$.

Definition 1.1 (Complex numbers)

The set of complex numbers is $\mathbb{C}:=\{(x,y):x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}$ with the addition $\oplus$ and the multiplication $\odot$:

$$\begin{aligned} (x_1,y_1)\oplus(x_2,y_2)&:=(x_1+x_2,\;y_1+y_2),\\ (x_1,y_1)\odot(x_2,y_2)&:=(x_1x_2-y_1y_2,\;x_1y_2+x_2y_1). \end{aligned}$$
(1.1)(1.2)

The zero element is $(0,0)$, the unit element is $(1,0)$, the imaginary unit is $\mathrm{i}:=(0,1)$.

⁠How to read it: $x$ and $y$ are real numbers without units, $\oplus$ and $\odot$ are new operations on pairs. Limiting case: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, (1.1) and (1.2) give the pairs $(x_1+x_2,0)$ and $(x_1x_2,0)$. So the pairs $(x,0)$ calculate exactly like the real numbers $x$. That is why one writes $x$ instead of $(x,0)$, $+$ instead of $\oplus$, $\cdot$ instead of $\odot$, and $\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}$ holds.

Theorem 1.2 (Imaginary unit and standard form)

  1. We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$.

  2. Every complex number has exactly one standard form $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ with $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$.

  3. In standard form, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x_1+\mathrm{i}y_1)+(x_2+\mathrm{i}y_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1+x_2)+\mathrm{i}(y_1+y_2)}$ and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x_1+\mathrm{i}y_1)(x_2+\mathrm{i}y_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1x_2-y_1y_2)+\mathrm{i}(x_1y_2+x_2y_1).}$$
(1.3)

⁠How to read it: Nobody has to memorize the formula (1.3): Multiply out the parentheses, substitute $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, sort. The minus in $x_1x_2-y_1y_2$ is the $\mathrm{i}^2$. In mathematics the imaginary unit is called $\mathrm{i}$, in electrical engineering $\mathrm{j}$, because $i$ is the current there. This chapter writes an upright $\mathrm{i}$. Validity: $x$ and $y$ must be real. Otherwise the representation is not unique: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{i}\cdot0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0+\mathrm{i}\cdot1}$.

Proof: Calculating with pairs

(i) By (1.2), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}\cdot\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0,1)\odot(0,1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0\cdot0-1\cdot1,\;0\cdot1+0\cdot1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-1,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$.

(ii) For real $y$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0,1)\odot(y,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0\cdot y-1\cdot0,\;0\cdot0+y\cdot1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0,y)}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x+\mathrm{i}y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x,0)\oplus(0,y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x,y)}$. The pair determines $x$ and $y$ uniquely.

(iii) By (ii), the right-hand sides are exactly the pairs from (1.1) and (1.2).

Example

Sum and product of the reference model:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1+z_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3+1)+\mathrm{i}(1+2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4+3\mathrm{i},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1z_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3+\mathrm{i})(1+2\mathrm{i})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3+6\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{i}+2\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+7\mathrm{i}.}$$

The pair formula (1.2) gives the same: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(3\cdot1-1\cdot2,\;3\cdot2+1\cdot1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,7)}$. The powers of $\mathrm{i}$ repeat with period 4: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. So only the remainder of $n$ on division by 4 counts, for example $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^{10}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{i}^8\,\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$.

QuestionDoes $\mathrm{i}$ really exist, or is it a calculating trick?

On the number line there is no $\mathrm{i}$; that much of the doubt is right. But $\mathrm{i}$ is the pair $(0,1)$, and (1.2) is an unambiguous rule for pairs. “Imaginary” is only a historical name.

Exercise 1 ★ Calculating in standard form

Bring to the form $x+\mathrm{i}y$: (a) $(4-\mathrm{i})(2+3\mathrm{i})$, (b) $(1+\mathrm{i})^2$ and from it $(1+\mathrm{i})^4$, (c) $\mathrm{i}^{27}+\mathrm{i}^{42}$.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{8+12\mathrm{i}-2\mathrm{i}-3\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11+10\mathrm{i}}$. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1+\mathrm{i})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+2\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\mathrm{i}}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1+\mathrm{i})^4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2\mathrm{i})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4}$. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{27}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\cdot6+3}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^{27}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{i}^3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mathrm{i}}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{42}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\cdot10+2}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^{42}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. The sum is $-1-\mathrm{i}$.
