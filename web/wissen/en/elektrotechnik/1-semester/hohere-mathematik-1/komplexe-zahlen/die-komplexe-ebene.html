Stage 2: The complex plane

What this stage is about:

A pair is a point. This stage draws the complex numbers in the plane and shows what addition and multiplication by $\mathrm{i}$ mean there.

The idea. You plot $x$ to the right and $y$ upward. The horizontal real axis is $\mathbb{R}$; on the vertical imaginary axis lie the purely imaginary numbers $\mathrm{i}y$. Every complex number is a point or, equivalently, an arrow (vector) from the origin to this point. Addition works separately for each coordinate: The arrow of $z_2$ is attached to the tip of $z_1$. Both orders together form a parallelogram, and the sum is its fourth corner (Fig. 2, left). In AC analysis these arrows are called phasors, and voltages add in exactly this way.

Definition 2.1 (Real part, imaginary part, Gaussian plane)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ with $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{:\operatorname{Re}(z)}$ is called the real part and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{:\operatorname{Im}(z)}$ the imaginary part of $z$. The complex plane with the coordinates $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ is also called the Gaussian plane. We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{w}$ if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Re}(w)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Im}(z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{Im}(w)}$.

⁠How to read it: Both parts are real: The imaginary part of $3+\mathrm{i}$ is 1, not $\mathrm{i}$. So an equation in $\mathbb{C}$ stands for two real equations. The difference $z-w$ is the arrow from $w$ to $z$, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w+(z-w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z}$. In the reference model, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1-z_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2-\mathrm{i}}$.

Theorem 2.2 (Multiplication by $\mathrm{i}$)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-y+\mathrm{i}x}$. The arrow of $z$ is thereby rotated by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about the origin.

⁠How to read it: The point $(x,y)$ becomes $(-y,x)$. Two rotations give the half turn, that is, the sign change: This is the mechanism behind $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. A quarter turn leaves the number line. That is why $\mathrm{i}$ could not lie on it.

Proof: Quarter turn

We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}(x+\mathrm{i}y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{i}x+\mathrm{i}^2y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-y+\mathrm{i}x}$. The arrow of $z$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the horizontal leg $x$ and the vertical leg $y$, both counted with sign. The quarter turn makes $x$ to the right into $x$ upward and $y$ upward into $y$ to the left. So the rotated arrow ends at $(-y,x)$.

Example

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}z_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1+3\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^2z_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3-\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-z_1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^3z_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-3\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{i}^4z_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z_1}$ (Fig. 2, right).

⁠Limiting case: A real factor $r$ does not rotate, it stretches: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r(x+\mathrm{i}y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{rx+\mathrm{i}ry}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ it reverses the arrow, like $\mathrm{i}$ applied twice.

QuestionIsn't the complex plane just the ordinary coordinate plane?

As a set and for addition, yes. The difference is multiplication: (1.2) turns two points into a point again, and this makes division possible (Stage 4). Only that turns the plane into a number system.

Exercise 2 ★★ Geometry with numbers

Given are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4+2\mathrm{i}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+4\mathrm{i}}$. (a) Compute the fourth corner $S$ of the parallelogram with the sides $PQ$ and $PR$. Show that both diagonals have the same midpoint. (b) Rotate $Q$ about the point $P$ by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise. Hint: The arrow from $P$ to $Q$ is $Q-P$. (c) Limiting case: What does your formula from (b) give for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P}$?

Solution

(a) From $P$ start the arrows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q-P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3+\mathrm{i}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R-P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+3\mathrm{i}}$. The fourth corner is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P+(Q-P)+(R-P)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q+R-P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5+5\mathrm{i}}$. The midpoints of the diagonals are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(P+S)/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(6+6\mathrm{i})/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3+3\mathrm{i}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(Q+R)/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(6+6\mathrm{i})/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3+3\mathrm{i}}$.

(b) You rotate the arrow $Q-P$ and attach it at $P$ again: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P+\mathrm{i}(Q-P)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{i}(3+\mathrm{i})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+\mathrm{i}-1+3\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\mathrm{i}}$. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P+\mathrm{i}\cdot0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P}$: The center of rotation stays where it is.
