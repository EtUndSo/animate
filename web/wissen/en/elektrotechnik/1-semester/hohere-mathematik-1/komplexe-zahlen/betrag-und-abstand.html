Stage 5: Absolute value and distance

What this stage is about:

Complex numbers cannot be ordered, but they can be measured. This stage carries distance and absolute value over from Chapter 1 to the plane and shows which rules survive.

The idea. In $\mathbb{R}$, $|x|$ measures the distance from the origin. In the plane the Pythagorean theorem takes over: The segment from 0 to $z$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the legs $|x|$ and $|y|$ (Fig. 5, left). The distance between two points is the hypotenuse over the differences of their coordinates. The result is real, so “greater than” is allowed again here.

Definition 5.1 (Distance and absolute value)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u+\mathrm{i}v}$ with real $x,y,u,v$, let

$$d(z,w):=\sqrt{(x-u)^2+(y-v)^2},$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|:=d(z,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.}$$
(5.1)

⁠How to read it: Under the root stands a real number greater than or equal to zero, and $\sqrt a$ is the nonnegative solution of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a}$ (Chapter 1). The distance $d(z,w)$ depends only on the difference: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(z,w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z-w|}$. Limiting case: For real $z$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{x^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|x|}$, the absolute value from Chapter 1.

Theorem 5.2 (Rules for absolute value and distance)

For all $z,w,\tilde z\in\mathbb{C}$:

  1. $|z|\ge0$ and ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\Leftrightarrow z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$), likewise $d(z,w)\ge0$ and ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(z,w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\Leftrightarrow z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{w}$),

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(z,w)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d(w,z)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\overline{z}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|{-z}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|}$,

  3. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2}$, so for $z\ne0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}\cdot1/|z|^2}$,

  4. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z\cdot w|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|\cdot|w|}$,

  5. $\max\{|\operatorname{Re}(z)|,|\operatorname{Im}(z)|\}\le|z|\le|\operatorname{Re}(z)|+|\operatorname{Im}(z)|$,

  6. $|z+w|\le|z|+|w|$ and $d(z,w)\le d(z,\tilde z)+d(\tilde z,w)$ (triangle inequality),

  7. $|z-w|\le|z|+|w|$, $\bigl||z|-|w|\bigr|\le|z-w|$ and $\bigl||z|-|w|\bigr|\le|z+w|$ (reverse triangle inequality).

⁠How to read it: (iv): The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values. (v): The hypotenuse is at most as long as both legs together, and each leg is at most as long as the hypotenuse. (vi): A detour via $\tilde z$ is never shorter. Validity: The rules hold without restriction, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z+w|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|+|w|}$ is not a rule: Equality occurs when the arrows point in the same direction, as for $z_2$ and $2z_2$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3\sqrt5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt5+2\sqrt5}$.

Proof: Rules for the absolute value

(i), (ii): A sum of squares is zero if and only if both squares are zero, and sign changes do not alter it.

(iii): Stage 3, rule (vi), and (4.1).

(iv): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|zw|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{zw\,\overline{zw}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z\overline{z}\,w\overline{w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2|w|^2}$ by (iii) and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\overline{zw}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}\,\overline{w}}$. Both absolute values are nonnegative, so the root may be taken.

(v): $x^2\le x^2+y^2$ and $y^2\le x^2+y^2$ give the left inequality, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2+y^2\le x^2+2|x||y|+y^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(|x|+|y|)^2}$ the right one.

(vi): With (iii) and the conjugation rules,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z+w|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(z+w)(\overline{z}+\overline{w})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2+z\overline{w}+\overline{z}w+|w|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z\overline{w})+|w|^2,}$$

because $\overline{z}w$ is the conjugate of $z\overline{w}$. By (v) and (iv), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{Re}(z\overline{w})\le|z\overline{w}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z||w|}$. So $|z+w|^2\le(|z|+|w|)^2$. The distance form follows from $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z-w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(z-\tilde z)+(\tilde z-w)}$.

(vii): $-w$ instead of $w$ in (vi) gives the first inequality. From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|(z-w)+w|\le|z-w|+|w|}$ follows $|z|-|w|\le|z-w|$, with the roles swapped $|w|-|z|\le|z-w|$, together the second. $-w$ instead of $w$ in that gives the third.

Example

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z_1|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{10}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.162}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z_2|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt5}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.236}$. The triangle inequality: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z_1+z_2|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|4+3\mathrm{i}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\le5.398}$. The product: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z_1z_2|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|1+7\mathrm{i}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{50}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{10}\cdot\sqrt5}$. The distance: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d(z_1,z_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|2-\mathrm{i}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt5}$. The reverse inequality: $0.926\le2.236$. Rule (v) for $z_1$: $3\le3.162\le4$.

Point sets. Because $|z-c|$ is the distance from $z$ to $c$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z-c|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{r}$ describes the circle about $c$ with radius $r$, and $|z-c|\le r$ the disk. Conditions on $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ or $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ cut out half-planes. Such inequalities are allowed because both sides are real.

QuestionWhy is $x^2+y^2$ under the root and not $z^2$?

For real $z$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{z^2}}$, and that is tempting. In $\mathbb{C}$, $z^2$ is in general not real and can be negative, for example $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(2\mathrm{i})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4}$. The absolute value is meant to be a length, and Pythagoras adds squares of real lengths. The correct form is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{z\overline{z}}}$.

Exercise 6 ★★ Point sets

Sketch in the complex plane: (a) $\{z:|z-1-\mathrm{i}|\le2\}$, (b) $\{z:|z-2|=|z+2\mathrm{i}|\}$, (c) $\{z:\operatorname{Re}((1+\mathrm{i})z)>1\}$. Hint for (b): Square both sides and substitute $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm{i}y}$.

Solution

(a) $|z-(1+\mathrm{i})|\le2$ is the disk about $1+\mathrm{i}$ with radius 2, including the boundary.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x-2)^2+y^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+(y+2)^2}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-4x+4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4y+4}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-x}$. This is the line through the origin with slope $-1$. Geometrically it consists of all points equally far from $2$ and $-2\mathrm{i}$: the perpendicular bisector of the connecting segment.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1+\mathrm{i})(x+\mathrm{i}y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x-y)+\mathrm{i}(x+y)}$. The condition reads $x-y>1$, that is, $y<x-1$. This is the half-plane below the line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x-1}$, without the line.

Exercise 7 ★★★ Parallelogram and unit circle

(a) Show $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z+w|^2+|z-w|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2|z|^2+2|w|^2}$ and check the equation with $z_1$ and $z_2$. What does it say about the diagonals of the parallelogram? (b) Show: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ implies $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}}$. Check this with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6+0.8\mathrm{i}}$. (c) Predict where $1/(2z)$ lies for this $z$, and calculate it.

Solution

(a) As in the proof of the triangle inequality, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z\pm w|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2\pm2\operatorname{Re}(z\overline{w})+|w|^2}$. On adding, the cross terms drop out. Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{25+5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{30}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot10+2\cdot5}$. The squares of the two diagonals of a parallelogram together equal the squares of all four sides.

(b) From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\overline{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ follows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6+0.8\mathrm{i}}$ we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|z|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.36+0.64}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(0.6+0.8\mathrm{i})(0.6-0.8\mathrm{i})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.36+0.64}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$.

(c) Prediction: Reflect and divide by $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|2z|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, so the reciprocal lies on the arrow of $\overline{z}$ at absolute value $0.5$. Calculation: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/(2z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\overline{z}/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.3-0.4\mathrm{i}}$.
