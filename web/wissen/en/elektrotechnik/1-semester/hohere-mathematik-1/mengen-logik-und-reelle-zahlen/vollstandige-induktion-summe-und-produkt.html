Stage 5: Mathematical induction, sum and product

What this stage is about:

Statements about all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ cannot be proved by trial. This stage provides the method and with it a clean definition of sum, product and power.

The idea. Every natural number arises from 1 by repeated counting on. If you show a statement for 1 and show that it carries over from every $n$ to $n+1$, the chain reaches every number (Fig. 5).

Theorem 5.1 (Mathematical induction)

Let $\mathcal A(n)$ be statements with $n\in\mathbb{N}$. If (1) $\mathcal A(1)$ is true (base case) and (2) for arbitrary $n$ the implication $\mathcal A(n)\Rightarrow\mathcal A(n+1)$ is true (induction step, $\mathcal A(n)$ is called the induction hypothesis), then $\mathcal A(n)$ is true for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ (induction conclusion).

⁠Second version: Let $n_0\in\mathbb{N}$ be fixed. If $\mathcal A(n_0)$ is true and for every $n\ge n_0$

$$\bigl(\mathcal A(n_0)\wedge\mathcal A(n_0+1)\wedge\dots\wedge\mathcal A(n)\bigr)\Rightarrow\mathcal A(n+1),$$

then $\mathcal A(n)$ is true for all $n\ge n_0$.

⁠How to read it: The second version allows a later start and all earlier cases as hypothesis. Why both parts: Without a base case: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathcal A(n):=(n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n+1)}$ has a valid step (add 1 on both sides) but is always false. Without a step: $n^2-n+41$ is prime for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,\dots,40}$, but for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{41}$ it is $41^2$.

QuestionDoesn't the step assume what is to be proved?

No. You assume $\mathcal A(n)$ for an arbitrary but fixed $n$ and show only the implication $\mathcal A(n)\Rightarrow\mathcal A(n+1)$. That $\mathcal A(n)$ is true follows only from the base case and the chain.

Proof: Why induction works

$\mathbb{N}$ has this property: If $M\subset\mathbb{N}$ with $1\in M$ and $n\in M$ always implies $n+1\in M$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbb{N}}$. That is the construction of $\mathbb{N}$ itself. Set $M:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\mathcal A(n)\text{ true}\}$. For the second version let $\mathcal B(n):=\mathcal A(n_0)\wedge\dots\wedge\mathcal A(n)$. Then the hypothesis gives $\mathcal B(n)\Rightarrow\mathcal B(n+1)$, and the same property of $\mathbb{N}$, started at $n_0$, gives $\mathcal B(n)$ for all $n\ge n_0$.

Definition 5.2 (Sum, product, power: recursive)

For $x_1,x_2,\ldots\in\mathbb K$ one sets

$$\sum_{i=1}^{1}x_i:=x_1,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_i:=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\Bigr)+x_{n+1},$$
$$\prod_{i=1}^{1}x_i:=x_1,\quad\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}x_i:=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_i\Bigr)\cdot x_{n+1}.$$
(5.1)

If all $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$, then $x^n:=\prod_{i=1}^nx$. For $x\ne0$ and $n\in\mathbb{N}$ one sets $x^0:=1$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^{-n}:=(x^n)^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\frac1x)^n}$. Also $n!:=\prod_{i=1}^ni$ and $0^0:=1$.

⁠How to read it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1+\dots+x_n}$; the dots are now explained by the recursion, and by Theorem 5.1 the value is fixed for every $n$. $x^0:=1$ is forced because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^n\cdot x^0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^{n+0}}$ is supposed to hold. $0^0:=1$ is a convention: With it, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ extends to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x+y)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2y^0+2x^1y^1+x^0y^2}$ stays correct for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Theorem 5.3 (Rules for sum, product, power)

In every field:

  1. The value of $\sum_{i=1}^nx_i$ and $\prod_{i=1}^nx_i$ does not depend on the order of the $x_i$.

  2. Product of sums: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^nx_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^Ny_j\Bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^Nx_iy_j\Bigr)}$.

  3. Power laws for $x,y\ne0$, $p,q\in\mathbb{Z}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^px^q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^{p+q}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(x^p)^q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^{p\cdot q}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(xy)^p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^py^p}$.

⁠How to read it: (i) is induction over (A1), (A2), (M1), (M2). (ii) says “each with each”; that is (AM) repeated. For $p,q\in\mathbb{N}$, (iii) is renumbering: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^px^q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\prod_{i=1}^{p+q}x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^{p+q}}$. Why $x\ne0$: $0^{-1}$ does not exist; for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ only exponents $n\ge0$ are allowed, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0^n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ for $n\ge1$. For $x,y>0$ the laws also hold with rational exponents, for example $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^{1/2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt x}$ (Chapter 5).

Example 1Sum of the odd numbers

Claim: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^n(2i-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n^2}$. Base case: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot1-1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1^2}$. Step: If the formula holds for $n$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(2i-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n^2+2(n+1)-1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n^2+2n+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(n+1)^2}$. Conclusion: It holds for all $n$. In the same way one proves $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^ni}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{n(n+1)}2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^ni^3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}4}$ (Exercise 6).

Example 2late start

$n!>2^n$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n\ge n_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$. Base case: $24>16$. Step: $(n+1)!=(n+1)\,n!>(n+1)\,2^n\ge2\cdot2^n$, because $n+1\ge2$.

⁠Limiting case: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, $6<8$; the start at 4 is necessary.

Example 3all earlier cases

Every $n\ge2$ is a product of primes. Base case: 2 is prime. Step: If $n+1$ is not prime, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ab}$ with $2\le a,b\le n$. Here $\mathcal A(n)$ alone is not enough; you need $\mathcal A(a)$ and $\mathcal A(b)$.

Exercise 6 ★★ Practicing induction

  1. Prove $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^ni^3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}4}$ and check $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$.

  2. Calculate $\prod_{i=1}^5(1+\frac1i)$. Predict the formula for $\prod_{i=1}^n(1+\frac1i)$ and prove it.

  3. Find the error: “All cables have the same color. Base case: One cable has one color. Step: From $n+1$ cables take one away; the remaining $n$ have the same color. Take a different one away, likewise. So all have the same color.”

Solution

(a) Base case: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1\cdot4}4}$. Step: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}4+(n+1)^3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(n+1)^2(n^2+4n+4)}4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(n+1)^2(n+2)^2}4}$. Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1+8+27}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{36}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{9\cdot16}4}$. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot\frac32\cdot\frac43\cdot\frac54\cdot\frac65}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$. Conjecture: $n+1$. Base case: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1+\frac11}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$. Step: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(n+1)(1+\frac1{n+1})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n+2}$. (c) The step fails at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$: Of two cables, taking one away leaves a different one each time, and the remainders have no cable in common. The step must hold for every $n$.
