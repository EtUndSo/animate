What this stage is about:

Often an expression cannot be calculated, only estimated. This stage provides the five standard estimates.

The idea. Almost everything follows from one fact: A square is not negative. Writing out $(a-b)^2\ge0$ gives $2ab\le a^2+b^2$. Young, the means and Cauchy-Schwarz are this line, cleverly rewritten. Only Bernoulli needs induction.

Theorem 9.1 (Five inequalities)

In $\mathbb{R}$:

  1. Bernoulli: For $x>-1$, $x\ne0$ and $n\ge2$, $(1+x)^n>1+nx$.

  2. Young: For $\varepsilon>0$ and $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, $|xy|\le\frac\varepsilon2x^2+\frac1{2\varepsilon}y^2$.

  3. Means: For $x,y\ge0$, $\sqrt{xy}\le\frac{x+y}2$. On the left is the geometric mean, on the right the arithmetic mean.

  4. Cauchy-Schwarz: For $x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_n\in\mathbb{R}$, with $\|\vec x\|:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}\ge0$,

    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^n|x_iy_i|\le\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ny_i^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\|\vec x\|\cdot\|\vec y\|.}$$
    (9.1)

  5. Minkowski: With $\vec x+\vec y:=(x_1+y_1,\dots,x_n+y_n)$,

    $$\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i+y_i)^2}\le\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2}+\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ny_i^2},\quad\text{that is,}\quad\|\vec x+\vec y\|\le\|\vec x\|+\|\vec y\|.$$
    (9.2)

⁠How to read it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1,\dots,x_n)}$ is a vector, a list of $n$ numbers, and $\|\vec x\|$ its norm. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ this is, by Pythagoras, the length of the arrow from the origin to the point $(x_1,x_2)$. Then (9.2) is the triangle inequality for arrows, and in Chapter 8 (9.1) turns into the dot product. In Young, $\varepsilon$ is an exchange rate: A small $x$ term costs a large $y$ term. Why the hypotheses: Bernoulli: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ gives $-27>-11$, false, so $x>-1$ is needed. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ there is equality. Means: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ gives $1\le-1$, false. Young: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ makes the right side negative.

Proof: The five inequalities

⁠Bernoulli, induction from $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$. Base case: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1+x)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+2x+x^2>1+2x}$, because $x^2>0$ by Theorem 4.2 (d). Step: Because $1+x>0$, the hypothesis may be multiplied by $1+x$ by (O4): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1+x)^{n+1}>(1+nx)(1+x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+(n+1)x+nx^2>1+(n+1)x}$.

⁠Young. $\sqrt\varepsilon$ exists by Theorem 7.2, and $\sqrt\varepsilon>0$. Squares are not negative:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0\le\Bigl(\sqrt\varepsilon\,x\pm\frac1{\sqrt\varepsilon}\,y\Bigr)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varepsilon x^2\pm2xy+\frac1\varepsilon y^2.}$$

This gives $\mp2xy\le\varepsilon x^2+\frac1\varepsilon y^2$. Because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|xy|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\max\{xy,-xy\}}$, the claim follows after division by 2.

⁠Means. Young with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ for $\sqrt x$ and $\sqrt y$: $\sqrt x\sqrt y\le\frac12x+\frac12y$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt x\sqrt y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{xy}}$.

⁠Cauchy-Schwarz. If all $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ or all $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, we get $0\le0$. Otherwise $\|\vec x\|>0$ and $\|\vec y\|>0$, and we set $\xi_i:=x_i/\|\vec x\|$, $\eta_i:=y_i/\|\vec y\|$. Dividing (9.1) by $\|\vec x\|\|\vec y\|$, it remains to show $\sum_{i=1}^n|\xi_i\eta_i|\le1$. We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_i\xi_i^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{\|\vec x\|^2}\sum_ix_i^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, likewise $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_i\eta_i^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. By the means for $\xi_i^2$, $\eta_i^2$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{i=1}^n|\xi_i\eta_i|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{\xi_i^2\eta_i^2}\le\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\xi_i^2+\eta_i^2}2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12+\frac12}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.}$$

⁠Minkowski. Let $s_i:=x_i+y_i$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\vec x+\vec y}$. With (AM), then $a\le|a|$, then (9.1) for the lists $x_i,s_i$ and $y_i,s_i$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\|\vec s\|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_ix_is_i+\sum_iy_is_i\le\sum_i|x_is_i|+\sum_i|y_is_i|\le\bigl(\|\vec x\|+\|\vec y\|\bigr)\|\vec s\|.}$$

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\|\vec s\|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, the left side of (9.2) is 0. Otherwise divide by $\|\vec s\|$.

Example

Bernoulli: $1.01^{10}>1.1$, exactly $1.1046$. For small $x$, $1+nx$ is a good approximation: 1 % more voltage means roughly 2 % more power in a resistor, exactly 2.01 %. For large $x$ it is crude: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2^{10}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1024}$ versus 11. Young with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8}$: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ we get $16\le34$, for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ we get $16\le16$. Cauchy-Schwarz with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,4)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,2)}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3+8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11\le5\sqrt5}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11.18}$. Minkowski: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\|(4,6)\|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{52}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{7.21\le5+\sqrt5}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{7.24}$.

⁠Limiting case: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ both means are equal; in the semicircle the height stands at the center.

QuestionWhen do I use which inequality?

Power of a number close to 1: Bernoulli. Estimating a product: Young, means. Sum of products: Cauchy-Schwarz. Root of a sum: Minkowski.

Exercise 10 ★★★ Estimating

  1. Show $2^{n-1}\ge n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$. (Stage 7 needs this inequality.)

  2. Estimate $0.995^{20}$ from below and compare with the exact value.

  3. A rectangle has area 16. Use the means to show that the perimeter is at least 16. For which sides does equality hold?

  4. Check Cauchy-Schwarz and Minkowski for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,2,2)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2,-1,2)}$.

  5. Show: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{t\,\vec x}$ with $t\in\mathbb{R}$, equality holds in (9.1).

Solution

(a) For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,2}$ we get $1\ge1$ and $2\ge2$. For $n\ge3$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2^{n-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1+1)^{n-1}>1+(n-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n}$ by Bernoulli. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.005}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.995^{20}>1-0.1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.9}$. Exactly $0.9046$; the bound lies 0.5 % below it. (c) Sides $a,b>0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{ab}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16}$: Perimeter $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2(a+b)\ge2\cdot2\sqrt{ab}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16}$. Equality for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, the square. (d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\|\vec x\|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{1+4+4}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\|\vec y\|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{4+1+4}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$. Cauchy-Schwarz: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2+2+4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8\le9}$. Minkowski: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec x+\vec y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,1,4)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\|\vec x+\vec y\|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{26}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5.10\le6}$. (e) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_i|x_i\cdot tx_i|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|t|\sum_ix_i^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|t|\,\|\vec x\|^2}$. On the right: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\|t\vec x\|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{t^2\sum_ix_i^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|t|\,\|\vec x\|}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\|\vec x\|\,\|\vec y\|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|t|\,\|\vec x\|^2}$.
