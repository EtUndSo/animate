Stage 7: Nested intervals and roots

What this stage is about:

The axiom guarantees boundaries. This stage turns that into a construction method and uses it to prove that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ has exactly one positive solution in $\mathbb{R}$.

The idea. You trap the number you seek between two fractions and halve the interval again and again until it is arbitrarily small (Fig. 7). Each interval lies in the previous one. Completeness guarantees that there is a point that lies in all of them. In $\mathbb{Q}$ this point can be missing. An interval is $[a,b]:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\le x\le b\}$; the nonnegative numbers are $\mathbb{R}_0^+:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\ge0\}$.

Theorem 7.1 (Nested intervals)

For numbers $a_n,b_n\in\mathbb{R}$, $n\in\mathbb{N}$, suppose that $a_1\le a_2\le a_3\le\dots\le b_3\le b_2\le b_1$ and

$$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists n\in\mathbb{N}:\ 0\le b_n-a_n<\varepsilon.$$
(7.1)

Then there is exactly one $x\in\mathbb{R}$ with $a_n\le x\le b_n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

⁠How to read it: $\varepsilon$ (Greek epsilon) is an arbitrarily small positive number. (7.1) means: The intervals $[a_n,b_n]$ become smaller than any given length. Why the hypotheses: Without (7.1), $x$ is not unique: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac1n}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+\frac1n}$, all of $[0,1]$ lies in all the intervals. In $\mathbb{Q}$ the theorem does not hold: The fractions $a_n,b_n$ from Fig. 7 satisfy all the conditions, but no fraction lies in all the intervals.

Proof: Nested intervals

⁠Existence. For arbitrary $n,k$, let $m$ be the larger of the two numbers. Then $a_n\le a_m\le b_m\le b_k$. So every $b_k$ is an upper bound of $A:=\{a_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$. By Theorem 6.5 (i), $x:=\sup A$ exists. We have $a_n\le x$ because $x$ is an upper bound, and $x\le b_k$ because $x$ is the least one.

⁠Uniqueness. If $x<\tilde x$ lay in all the intervals, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_n-a_n\ge\tilde x-x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{:\varepsilon>0}$ for all $n$, contradicting (7.1).

Theorem 7.2 (Square root)

For every $y\in\mathbb{R}_0^+$ there is exactly one $x\in\mathbb{R}_0^+$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$. One writes $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt y}$ (“square root of $y$”). For $x,y\ge0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{xy}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt x\sqrt y}$.

⁠How to read it: The root is the nonnegative solution, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{4}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ and not $-2$. The equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ has two solutions: $\sqrt2$ and $-\sqrt2$, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(-1)(-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. The product rule needs $x,y\ge0$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{(-1)(-1)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, but $\sqrt{-1}$ does not exist in $\mathbb{R}$. Limiting case $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: The only solution is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Proof: Existence for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, uniqueness, product rule

Start with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ and halve as in Fig. 7. All $a_n$, $b_n$ are fractions, so by Theorem 6.1 never $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_n^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$. That gives $a_n^2<2<b_n^2$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b_n-a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2^{1-n}\le\frac1n}$ because $2^{n-1}\ge n$ (Exercise 10). By Theorem 6.5 (vi), (7.1) holds, so there is $x$ with $a_n\le x\le b_n$ for all $n$.

Why $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$: From $a_n\le x\le b_n$, Theorem 4.2 (e) gives $a_n^2\le x^2\le b_n^2$. The number 2 also lies in $[a_n^2,b_n^2]$. This interval has length $(b_n-a_n)(b_n+a_n)\le4\cdot\frac1n$. So the distance between $x^2$ and 2 is smaller than any positive number and therefore zero. For other $y>0$ one starts with $[0,y+1]$; if a midpoint $m$ hits exactly $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Uniqueness: From $u>v\ge0$ it follows that $u^2>v^2$; two different nonnegative numbers have different squares.

⁠Product rule: $\sqrt x\sqrt y\ge0$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\sqrt x\sqrt y)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{xy}$ (M1, M2), and the nonnegative solution is unique.

Example

After six steps, $1.40625\le\sqrt2\le1.4375$, width $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2^{-5}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.03125}$. Decimal numbers are the same idea with ten parts instead of two: Each digit narrows the interval to one tenth. That is why every real number has a representation (0.1).

QuestionSurely it is enough to read $\sqrt2$ off a calculator. Why the effort?

For any numerical value, that is enough. But the calculator shows a fraction, $1.414213562$, whose square is not 2. The question here is: Is there a number whose square is exactly 2 at all? In $\mathbb{Q}$ no, in $\mathbb{R}$ yes, and this difference later carries limits and continuity.

Exercise 8 ★★★ The square root of 3

  1. Carry out three halving steps for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, starting with $[1,2]$. How wide is the result?

  2. Prove $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb{Q}$. Hint: A number that is not divisible by 3 has the form $3k\pm1$.

  3. At which point does the same proof fail for $\sqrt4$?

Solution

(a) Midpoint $1.5$: $2.25<3$, so $[1.5, 2]$. Midpoint $1.75$: $3.0625>3$, so $[1.5, 1.75]$. Midpoint $1.625$: $2.640625<3$, so $[1.625, 1.75]$. Width $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac18}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.125}$. (b) Assumption: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac pq}$, $p,q\in\mathbb{N}$, not both divisible by 3. Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3q^2}$. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3k\pm1}$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3(3k^2\pm2k)+1}$, not divisible by 3. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3k}$, hence $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3k^2}$, and likewise $q$ is divisible by 3. Contradiction. (c) From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4q^2}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2k}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q^2}$. That says nothing about the divisibility of $q$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac21}$.
