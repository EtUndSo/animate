What this stage is about:

All axioms have the form “for all $x$ there is a $y$.” This stage clarifies how to read and negate such sentences, and how to turn a negation into a proof.

The idea. “For all $x$ in $\{x_1,x_2,\dots\}$, $\mathcal A(x)$ holds” is a long and: $\mathcal A(x_1)\wedge\mathcal A(x_2)\wedge\dots$ “There is an $x$” is a long or. When negating, De Morgan turns and into or. That is why negation turns “for all” into “there is.”

Definition 2.1 (Quantifiers)

$\forall$ means “for all” (universal quantifier), $\exists$ “there is at least one” (existential quantifier), $\exists_1$ “there is exactly one,” $\nexists$ “there is no.”

⁠How to read it: True examples in the reference model are $\forall x\in A:x\le12$; $\exists x\in A:x>5$ (for instance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$); $\exists_1x\in A\cap B:x>2$ (namely $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$); $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\nexists x\in B:x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$. Divisibility is also a quantifier statement: $m\mid p$ (“$m$ divides $p$”) means $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\exists n\in\mathbb{N}:m\cdot n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p}$, so $6\mid12$ because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{6\cdot2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12}$, and $7\nmid12$. The set of primes is $\{p\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}:\nexists m\in\{2,\dots,p-1\}\text{ with }m\mid p\}$.

Theorem 2.2 (Negation rule)
$$\neg\bigl(\forall x\in M:\mathcal A(x)\bigr)\Leftrightarrow\bigl(\exists x\in M:\neg\mathcal A(x)\bigr),\quad \neg\bigl(\exists y\in N:\mathcal B(y)\bigr)\Leftrightarrow\bigl(\forall y\in N:\neg\mathcal B(y)\bigr).$$
(2.1)

⁠How to read it: Swap the quantifier, negate the statement; with nested quantifiers, one after the other. In the reference model, the negation of $\forall n\in\mathbb{N}:\mathcal E(n)$ is the false statement $\exists n\in\mathbb{N}:(n\ge7\wedge n\le5)$. Proof idea: $\neg(\mathcal A(x_1)\wedge\mathcal A(x_2)\wedge\dots)$ is by De Morgan $\neg\mathcal A(x_1)\vee\neg\mathcal A(x_2)\vee\dots$; the second rule arises the same way. Limiting case $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\emptyset}$: “For all $x\in\emptyset$” is always true, “there is $x\in\emptyset$” always false.

QuestionDo five examples suffice to prove “for all”?

No. An example only checks one $x$. By (2.1), on the other hand, a single counterexample refutes a “for all” statement. Conversely, “there is” is proved with one example.

Examplenested quantifiers

Let $H$ be the devices of a household, $F$ the fuses, and let $K(g,s)$ mean “device $g$ is connected to fuse $s$.” Then

$$\mathcal P:=\bigl(\forall g\in H\ \exists s\in F:K(g,s)\bigr),$$
$$\mathcal Q:=\bigl(\exists s\in F\ \forall g\in H:K(g,s)\bigr)$$

say different things (Fig. 2): In $\mathcal P$, $s$ may depend on the device; in $\mathcal Q$, one fuse serves all. From $\mathcal Q$ follows $\mathcal P$; the converse is false. The order of different quantifiers must not be swapped. The negations are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\neg\mathcal P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\exists g\ \forall s:\neg K(g,s))}$, one device is connected to no fuse, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\neg\mathcal Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\forall s\ \exists g:\neg K(g,s))}$, every fuse leaves out at least one device.

Theorem 2.3 (Indirect proof)

If $\neg\mathcal A\Rightarrow\mathcal B$ is true and $\mathcal B$ is false, then $\mathcal A$ is true.

⁠How to read it: You assume the opposite of the claim (contradiction hypothesis) and reason until something false comes out. A look at the table shows the mechanism: If $\neg\mathcal A$ were true, $\mathcal B$ would have to be true as well. So $\neg\mathcal A$ is false. To show an implication $\mathcal A\Rightarrow\mathcal B$ indirectly, the assumption by De Morgan is $\mathcal A\wedge\neg\mathcal B$. Example: There is no smallest positive rational number. Assumption: $r$ is the smallest. Then $r/2$ is rational, positive and smaller than $r$. That is false, so there is no such $r$.

Why axioms are needed. Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{N:N\notin N\}}$ be the set of all sets that do not contain themselves (Russell's paradox, 1903). If $M\in M$ is true, $M$ does not satisfy the condition, so $M\notin M$. If $M\notin M$ is true, $M$ satisfies the condition, so $M\in M$. Both are impossible; the construction is inadmissible. Just as contradictory is the sentence “This sentence is false.” Such findings triggered the foundational crisis of mathematics, from Hilbert's demand for a consistent structure (1900) to Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931). The lesson: Certainty exists only relative to agreed assumptions. That is why numbers are built on axioms.

Exercise 3 ★★ Negating and proving indirectly

  1. Negate: “Every circuit contains a component that gets warm.”

  2. Negate $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists n\in\mathbb{N}:\frac1n<\varepsilon$. Does the statement become true or false if the quantifiers are swapped?

  3. Show indirectly: If $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.

Solution

(a) “There is a circuit in which no component gets warm.” (b) Negation: $\exists\varepsilon>0\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}:\frac1n\ge\varepsilon$. Swapped: $\exists n\in\mathbb{N}\ \forall\varepsilon>0:\frac1n<\varepsilon$ is false, because for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1n}$ we get $\frac1n<\frac1n$. The original statement, by contrast, is true, as Stage 6 shows. (c) Assumption: $n^2$ odd and $n$ even, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2k}$. Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot2k^2}$ is even, a contradiction.
