What this stage is about:

The field axioms say nothing about “greater.” What assumptions lie behind calculating with inequalities?

The idea. Real numbers lie on a line according to size. One only has to fix which numbers are positive. Then “$x$ greater than $y$” is the same as “$x-y$ is positive.” Multiplication by a negative number reflects the line at zero, which is why the order flips (Fig. 4).

Definition 4.1 (Totally ordered field)

A field $\mathbb K$ with a relation “$>0$” is called totally ordered if:

  • ⁠(T1) Every $x\in\mathbb K$ satisfies exactly one of the relations $x>0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $(-x)>0$.

  • ⁠(T2) $x>0\wedge y>0\Rightarrow x+y>0$.

  • ⁠(T3) $x>0\wedge y>0\Rightarrow x\cdot y>0$.

One sets $x>y:\Leftrightarrow x-y>0$, $x<y:\Leftrightarrow y>x$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x\ge y:\Leftrightarrow(x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y\vee x>y)}$, and likewise $x\le y$.

⁠How to read it: $x>0$ means “$x$ is positive.” (T2) and (T3): Sum and product of positive numbers are positive. $x<0$ is synonymous with $(-x)>0$: $x$ is negative.

Theorem 4.2 (Calculating with inequalities)

In a totally ordered field, for all $x,y,z,u,v$:

(O1) Trichotomyexactly one of the relations $x<y$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$, $x>y$ holds
(O2) Transitivity$x<y\wedge y<z\Rightarrow x<z$
(O3) Monotonicity of addition$x<y\Rightarrow x+z<y+z$
(O4) Monotonicity of multiplication$x<y\wedge z>0\Rightarrow xz<yz$
(a) Reflecting$x<y\Leftrightarrow-y<-x$
(b) Adding$x<y\wedge u<v\Rightarrow x+u<y+v$
(c) Signs$xy>0\Leftrightarrow\bigl((x>0\wedge y>0)\vee(x<0\wedge y<0)\bigr)$
(d) Squares$x\ne0\Rightarrow x\cdot x>0$; in particular $1>0$
(e) Squaringfor $x\ge0$, $y\ge0$: $x^2\ge y^2\Leftrightarrow x\ge y$

⁠How to read it: An inequality tolerates any addition, but only multiplication by positive numbers. For $z<0$ it flips: $x<y\wedge z<0\Rightarrow xz>yz$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, (O4) becomes $0<0$, false. If an ordered field contained an $i$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i\cdot i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, then by (d) $-1>0$ and $1>0$, hence by (T2) $0>0$. That is why the complex numbers from Chapter 2 cannot be ordered. Instead of (T1) to (T3) one could equivalently take (O1) to (O4) as axioms.

QuestionMay I square an inequality?

Only if both sides are not negative; that is (e). Without this assumption: $-3<2$, but $9>4$. Squaring reflects the negative part of the line into the positive one, like times $-1$ in Fig. 4.

Proof: Order rules from (T1) to (T3)

(O1): (T1) for $x-y$. (O2): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(y-x)+(z-y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z-x}$ is positive by (T2). (O3): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(y+z)-(x+z)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y-x}$. (O4): $(y-x)z>0$ by (T3), so with (AM) $yz-xz>0$. (a): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(-x)-(-y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y-x}$. (b): $x+u<y+u$ and $u+y<v+y$ by (O3), then (O2).

(c) “$\Leftarrow$”: For $x,y>0$ this is (T3). For $x,y<0$ we have $(-x)(-y)>0$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(-x)(-y)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{xy}$. “$\Rightarrow$” indirectly: Let $xy>0$, but suppose the pair $(x,y)$ does not belong to the two cases on the right. Then four cases remain. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{xy}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, contradicting (T1). For $x<0<y$, (O4) gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{xy<0\cdot y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, contradicting $xy>0$. Likewise for $y<0<x$. (d): For $x\ne0$, $x>0$ or $x<0$, and by (c) $x\cdot x>0$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\ne0}$ it follows that $1>0$.

(e) “$\Leftarrow$”: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$ there is nothing to show. From $x>y\ge0$ it follows that $x\cdot x>y\cdot x\ge y\cdot y$ by (O4) with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{y}$. “$\Rightarrow$” as contraposition: From $0\le x<y$ it follows likewise that $x\cdot x\le x\cdot y<y\cdot y$.

Example

For $x\ne0$, $x$ is positive if and only if $x^{-1}$ is positive, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x\cdot x^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1>0}$ and (c). Therefore: Solve $\frac1x>2$. For $x>0$ we may multiply by $x$: $1>2x$, so $0<x<\frac12$. For $x<0$ the left side is negative; there is no solution.

⁠Limiting case: At $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$ we get $2>2$, false; the boundary does not belong.

Exercise 5 ★★ Inequalities

  1. Show $x<y\wedge z<0\Rightarrow xz>yz$ from (O4) and (a).

  2. For which real $x\ne1$ does $\frac3{x-1}>1$ hold? Distinguish two cases.

Solution

(a) From $z<0$ it follows that $-z>0$, by (O4) $-xz<-yz$, by (a) $yz<xz$. (b) Case $x>1$: Multiplying by $x-1>0$ gives $3>x-1$, so $1<x<4$. Case $x<1$: The left side is negative; no solution. Result: $1<x<4$.
