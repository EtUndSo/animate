Stage 6: The completeness axiom

What this stage is about:

$\mathbb{Q}$ satisfies all the axioms so far. Yet something is missing: the number whose square is 2. This stage shows that and names the axiom that separates $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$.

The idea. $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{Z}$ have visible gaps. $\mathbb{Q}$ seems to lie without gaps: Between two fractions $p/q<u/v$ there is always another, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac12(p/q+u/v)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p/q+\frac12(u/v-p/q)}$. Yet there are points that no fraction hits (Fig. 6). Completeness means: Every such boundary is a number.

Theorem 6.1 (The square root of 2 is not a fraction)

There is no $r\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$.

Proof: Indirect

Assumption: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac pq}$ with $p,q\in\mathbb{N}$ (the sign does not matter), reduced far enough that $p$ and $q$ are not both even. From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2q^2}$, so $p^2$ is even. By the contraposition from Stage 1, $p$ is even, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2k}$. Substituting: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4k^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2q^2}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2k^2}$, and likewise $q$ is even. Then $p$ and $q$ are both even, contradicting the reduction.

⁠How to read it: So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ is not solvable in $\mathbb{Q}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ the proof fails, as it must (Exercise 8).

Definition 6.2 (Bound, supremum, infimum)

Let $\mathbb K$ be totally ordered and $M\subset\mathbb K$.

  1. $M$ is called bounded above if there is $S\in\mathbb K$ with $x\le S$ for all $x\in M$. Every such $S$ is called an upper bound. Correspondingly bounded below and lower bound with $R\le x$. Both together are called bounded.

  2. $S$ is called the supremum of $M$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sup M}$, if $S$ is an upper bound and for every upper bound $\tilde S$ we have $S\le\tilde S$ (least upper bound).

  3. $R$ is called the infimum of $M$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\inf M}$, if $R$ is a lower bound and for every lower bound $\tilde R$ we have $\tilde R\le R$ (greatest lower bound).

  4. If $S$ lies in $M$, it is called the maximum ($\max M$); if $R$ lies in $M$, it is called the minimum ($\min M$).

⁠How to read it: Bounds stand outside and are not unique: $\{2,3,5,7\}$ has the upper bound 7, but also 100. Here $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sup}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\max}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{7}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\inf}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\min}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$. $\mathbb{N}$ has the lower bound 1 and no upper one (Theorem 6.5).

QuestionIsn't the supremum the largest element?

Only if it lies in the set. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{2-\frac1n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{1,\frac32,\frac53,\dots\}}$ has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\min M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sup M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, but no maximum, because $2\notin M$. In the reference model, $D$ is bounded above, for example by $1.5$. In $\mathbb{Q}$, $D$ nevertheless has no supremum: For every upper bound there is a smaller one.

Definition 6.3 (Completeness axiom (V))

A totally ordered field $\mathbb K$ is called complete if every nonempty set $M\subset\mathbb K$ that is bounded below has a greatest lower bound $\inf M\in\mathbb K$.

⁠How to read it: What matters is “$\in\mathbb K$.” $\mathbb{Q}$ is not complete: $\{x\in\mathbb{Q}:x>0,\ x^2>2\}$ is bounded below and nonempty, but has no greatest lower bound in $\mathbb{Q}$. The candidate would be $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb{Q}$. Why “nonempty” and “bounded”: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\emptyset}$, every number is a lower bound; there is no greatest one. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbb{Z}}$ has no lower bound.

Theorem 6.4 (Main theorem)

There is essentially exactly one totally ordered, complete field that contains $\mathbb{Q}$. It is the field of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$; its order agrees on $\mathbb{Q}$ with the usual one.

⁠How to read it: The proof goes too far here; the meaning is enough: The three groups of axioms fix $\mathbb{R}$ uniquely up to renaming, which is why one may speak of the real numbers. This replaces the provisional definition. The field $\{0,1\}$ satisfies only the field axioms, $\mathbb{Q}$ also the order axioms, only $\mathbb{R}$ all three groups. Equivalent to (V) is the requirement that every Cauchy sequence converges (Chapter 4).

Theorem 6.5 (Consequences of completeness)

In $\mathbb{R}$:

  1. Every nonempty set $M$ that is bounded above has a supremum, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sup M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\inf(-M)}$ with $-M:=\{-x:x\in M\}$.

  2. Supremum and infimum of a set are unique.

  3. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sup M}$, then for every $y<S$ there is an $x\in M$ with $y<x\le S$. The corresponding statement holds for the infimum.

  4. $\mathbb{N}$ is not bounded above.

  5. Theorem of Archimedes: For all $x,y>0$ there is an $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $nx>y$.

  6. For every $x>0$ there is an $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $0<\frac1n<x$ (Eudoxus).

⁠How to read it: (iii): Just below the supremum there is always an element of the set. (v): With enough small steps $x$ you get above any height $y$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.001}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1000}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,000\,001}$ suffices. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, (v) does not hold, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{nx}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. (vi): For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.003}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{334}$ suffices. One can also demand (v) as an axiom. That is less than (V), because $\mathbb{Q}$ satisfies it.

Proof: Consequences

(i) With $M$, $-M$ is nonempty too, and if $S$ is an upper bound of $M$, then $-S$ is a lower bound of $-M$ by Theorem 4.2 (a). (V) gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\inf(-M)}$. Because reflecting translates upper bounds into lower bounds and flips the order, $-R$ is the least upper bound of $M$.

(ii) If $S_1$, $S_2$ are suprema, both are upper bounds. So $S_1\le S_2$ because $S_1$ is the least, and likewise $S_2\le S_1$. By (O1), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S_2}$.

(iii) Indirectly: If there were $y<S$ without such an $x$, then $x\le y$ for all $x\in M$ (negation, (2.1)). Then $y$ would be an upper bound smaller than $S$, contradicting the definition of $\sup M$. That $x\le S$ holds is because $S$ is an upper bound.

(iv) Indirectly: Otherwise $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sup\mathbb{N}}$ would exist by (i). By (iii) there is $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $S-1<n$. Then $n+1>S$ and $n+1\in\mathbb{N}$, contradicting “upper bound.”

(v) Indirectly: Let $nx\le y$ for all $n$. Because $x^{-1}>0$, (O4) gives $n\le yx^{-1}$ for all $n$, contradicting (iv). (vi): $y:=1$ in (v) gives $nx>1$, so $x>\frac1n>0$.

Exercise 7 ★★ Determining bounds

Determine supremum, infimum, maximum and minimum in $\mathbb{R}$, where they exist, and justify: (a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{x\in\mathbb{Q}:x^2\le4\}}$, (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{\frac{(-1)^n}n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}}$, (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{x\in\mathbb{Q}:x^2<3\}}$. Which of these quantities are missing in (c) if one searches only in $\mathbb{Q}$?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{x\in\mathbb{Q}:-2\le x\le2\}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sup}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\max}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\inf}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\min}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}$. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{M_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\{-1,\frac12,-\frac13,\frac14,\dots\}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sup}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\max}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\inf}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\min}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sup M_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\inf M_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\sqrt3}$, no maximum and no minimum, because $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb{Q}$ (Exercise 8). In $\mathbb{Q}$, supremum and infimum are missing too: For every rational upper bound there is a smaller one.
