From the ground up: Valve, AC voltage and thermal energy

An AC voltage changes its sign periodically. Here it is sinusoidal, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm e(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat u\sin(2\pi ft)}$, with the peak value $\hat u$ (largest magnitude) and the frequency $f$ (oscillations per second, unit Hz). One oscillation lasts $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/f}$, so 20 ms at 50 Hz. Lowercase letters stand for time-dependent quantities.

Fig. 1 shows the effect of a diode. The mesh gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\mathrm D+u_\mathrm a}$. If $u_\mathrm e$ is positive, the diode conducts, around 0.7 V remains across it, and the rest is across the resistor. If $u_\mathrm e$ is negative, no current flows. Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and the diode bears the whole voltage. The AC voltage becomes a voltage with only one sign. This circuit is called a half-wave rectifier. Its peak is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{12\,\mathrm{V}-0.7\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11.3}$ V.

For the why, the chapter needs two energy quantities. The electronvolt (eV) is the energy an electron gains by passing through 1 V (Chapter 1): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{eV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{e\cdot1\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.602\cdot10^{-19}}$ J. Thermal energy arises because atoms and electrons move randomly at every temperature. Its measure is $k_\mathrm BT$ with the absolute temperature $T$ in K and the Boltzmann constant $k_\mathrm B$. At 300 K, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k_\mathrm BT}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.14\cdot10^{-21}\,\mathrm{J}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{25.85}$ meV. Collisions distribute this energy unevenly: few particles have a lot, many have little. In equilibrium the Boltzmann distribution holds:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{fraction of particles with at least the energy }W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-W/(k_\mathrm BT)}.}$$
(0.1)

For every additional $k_\mathrm BT$ of energy the fraction drops by a factor of 2.718; at $10\,k_\mathrm BT$ it is $4.5\cdot10^{-5}$. The same law describes how air gets thinner with altitude.
