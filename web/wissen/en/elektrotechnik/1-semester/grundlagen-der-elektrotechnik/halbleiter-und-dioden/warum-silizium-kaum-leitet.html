Stage 1: Why silicon hardly conducts

What this stage is about:

Metal conducts, glass does not, pure silicon hardly at all. The difference lies in the number of mobile electrons. This stage explains it from the allowed energies in the crystal.

The idea. In a single atom, electrons have only certain energies, the energy levels. In a crystal (regular lattice of atoms) the shells overlap, and the electrons belong to the whole crystal. No two electrons may occupy the same state. That is why each level splits into as many closely spaced levels as the crystal has atoms. This creates a band of allowed energies. Between the bands lies the forbidden zone: there are no states there. A field can accelerate an electron only if a free state lies just above it. So a full band does not conduct, no matter how many electrons it contains.

Definition 1.1 (Bands)

The valence band (VB) is the top band that is fully occupied at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. The conduction band (CB) lies above it and is empty at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. The band gap $W_\mathrm g$ is the smallest energy that lifts an electron from the VB into the CB. The Fermi energy $W_\mathrm F$ separates occupied from empty states at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. At $T>0$ it is the energy at which a state would be occupied half of the time; it may lie in the gap even though there is no state there.

⁠How to read it: Silicon has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.12}$ eV, germanium 0.67 eV, diamond 5.5 eV. Three cases: In a metal, the top occupied band is only partly filled, free states lie directly above the electrons, and it conducts at every temperature. In an insulator, $W_\mathrm g$ is so large that no electron gets across. A semiconductor is an insulator with a small gap: at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ it insulates; when excited by heat, light or strong fields, electrons get into the CB.

An electron that moves into the CB leaves a free place in the VB. A neighboring electron moves in, and the place wanders. The hole (defect electron) behaves like a mobile particle with the charge $+e$. Both count as charge carriers: electrons in the CB and holes in the VB. If an electron falls back into a hole, both disappear; this is called recombination.

Theorem 1.2 (Fraction of electrons in the conduction band)

In a pure semiconductor, $W_\mathrm F$ lies in the middle of the gap. For the states at the edge of the CB, this gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{occupation}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-W_\mathrm g/(2k_\mathrm BT)}.}$$
(1.1)
Derivation: Why half the gap counts

Every electron in the CB leaves a hole in the VB, so the numbers of both are equal. With similar numbers of states in both bands, this is possible only if $W_\mathrm F$ is the same distance $W_\mathrm g/2$ from both band edges. In (0.1) the energy above $W_\mathrm F$ counts, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{W_\mathrm g/2}$.

Example

At 300 K, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm g/(k_\mathrm BT)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{43.3}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{-21.7}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.9\cdot10^{-10}}$. The measured density of free electrons is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n_\mathrm i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$. That is one per $5\cdot10^{12}$ atoms, because silicon has $5\cdot10^{22}$ atoms per cm$^3$. Copper has $8.5\cdot10^{12}$ times more free electrons (Chapter 2).

⁠Limiting cases: For $T\to0$, (1.1) goes to zero: silicon insulates. Every 10 K of warming doubles the fraction. That is why the resistance of a semiconductor falls with temperature (Chapter 2).

QuestionWhy does a full band not conduct even though it is full of electrons?

Because current needs motion in a preferred direction. For that, an electron would have to move into a state with slightly more energy. In a full band, every such state is occupied. Two electrons could only swap places, and that changes nothing. The first hole makes the band conductive.

Exercise 1 ★ Comparing three materials

  1. Give $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.12}$ eV in joules. What multiple of $k_\mathrm BT$ is that at 300 K?

  2. Calculate (1.1) for silicon, germanium and diamond. Which pure material conducts best?

  3. Silicon warms from 300 K to 330 K. By what factor does the fraction grow?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1.12\cdot1.602\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.79\cdot10^{-19}}$ J, which is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1.12\,\mathrm{eV}/25.85\,\mathrm{meV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{43.3}$ times $k_\mathrm BT$. (b) Silicon $3.9\cdot10^{-10}$, germanium $2.4\cdot10^{-6}$, diamond $6.3\cdot10^{-47}$. Germanium conducts best, with around 6000 times the occupation of silicon; diamond has practically no free electrons. (c) With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{28.44}$ mV at 330 K the fraction is $2.8\cdot10^{-9}$, 7.2 times as much.
