What this stage is about:

The equation is in place. This stage reads it as a curve, adds breakdown, and shows what else can be built from the properties of the junction.

The idea. The p-side is called the anode, the n-side the cathode; both carry metal contacts. The triangle of the circuit symbol points in the forward direction. In a linear plot there seems to be a knee at 0.6 V (Fig. 6). It is due to the scale, because an exponential function has no threshold. On an axis up to 10 mA the curve reaches 1 % of the final value at 0.595 V, on an axis up to 10 µ A already at 0.417 V. On a logarithmic plot it is a straight line.

Breakdown. At a large reverse voltage, the field in the region becomes so strong that carriers release new pairs through collisions (avalanche effect) or electrons pass directly from the VB into the CB (Zener effect). The reverse current rises steeply; this is breakdown. It does no harm in itself. A diode is destroyed only by the heat $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U\,I}$ if nothing limits the current.

Zener diode. It is built for operation in breakdown and has a precisely defined voltage $U_\mathrm Z$ (Fig. 7 left). In the forward direction it behaves like a pn diode. Beyond $U_\mathrm Z$ the characteristic curve is almost vertical, and its small resistance is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Delta U/\Delta I}$. A series resistor absorbs fluctuations of the source: the voltage across the component barely changes, the current changes. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\Omega}$, a current change of 18 mA causes only 0.18 V.

Varactor diode. The carrier-free region is an insulating layer between two conducting regions, so it is a capacitor (Chapter 3) with the area $A$ and the spacing $w$. The junction capacitance falls with the reverse voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-U}$ because the region grows:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_\mathrm{j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\varepsilon A}{w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{C_0}{\sqrt{1+U_\mathrm R/U_\mathrm{D0}}}.}$$
(5.1)

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{mm^2}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{241}$ pF; at 25 V it is 40 pF and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.6}$ µ m. Validity: abrupt junction, operation in the reverse direction. Diodes with a particularly strong effect are called varactor diodes. They are used to tune the frequency of resonant circuits by voltage, for example in radio receivers. A resonant circuit of coil and capacitor oscillates at a frequency that depends on $C$.

Photodiode. Light consists of energy packets, the photons, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{h\,c/\lambda}$ ($h$: Planck constant, $c$: speed of light, $\lambda$: wavelength). If $W\ge W_\mathrm g$, a photon lifts an electron into the CB (internal photoelectric effect). For silicon this requires $\lambda\le1107$ nm. The field of the region separates the pair: the electron runs to the n-side, the hole to the p-side. This is a current from cathode to anode through the diode, that is, in the reverse direction, and it is proportional to the light power.

Theorem 5.1 (Illuminated diode)

The characteristic curve shifts downward by the photocurrent $I_\mathrm{Ph}$. The equivalent circuit is a current source in parallel with the diode:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm S\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{U/U_T}-1\bigr)-I_\mathrm{Ph},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{open circuit }(I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0):\ U_\mathrm L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_T\ln\Bigl(\frac{I_\mathrm{Ph}}{I_\mathrm S}+1\Bigr).}$$
(5.2)

⁠How to read it: As a light sensor, the photodiode is operated in the reverse direction; then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_\mathrm{Ph}}$. A solar cell is a large-area photodiode that operates at $U>0$ and $I<0$: the product $U\,I$ is negative, so it delivers power. Ten times the light raises $I_\mathrm{Ph}$ tenfold, but $U_\mathrm L$ by only 60 mV. The reason is the logarithm in (5.2). The voltage depends mainly on the material, around 0.5 V in operation for silicon. In the model with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ nA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.505}$ V, 0.536 V and 0.564 V at 0.3 A, 1 A and 3 A. Limiting case: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{Ph}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ gives (4.1) and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

QuestionBut the diode only conducts from 0.6 V on. Isn't that a threshold?

In a circuit with currents around 1 mA it looks that way: at 0.4 V only 52 nA flow, barely measurable. But the current grows steadily from 0 V on, by a factor of 10 per 60 mV. There is no voltage at which something switches. Only the magnitude becomes important for the circuit at some point.

Exercise 5 ★★ Holding a voltage

A Zener diode with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5.1}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\Omega}$ is connected through $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{220\,\Omega}$ to a source that fluctuates between 10 V and 14 V.

  1. How large is the current at both ends if the diode holds exactly 5.1 V?

  2. By how much does the diode voltage really change? By what factor has the fluctuation become smaller?

  3. What power does the diode convert at 14 V?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(10\,\mathrm{V}-5.1\,\mathrm{V})/220\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{22.3}$ mA and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(14\,\mathrm{V}-5.1\,\mathrm{V})/220\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40.5}$ mA. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{r_\mathrm Z\Delta I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\Omega\cdot18.2\,\mathrm{mA}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.18}$ V. More precisely, $R$ divides with $r_\mathrm Z$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4\,\mathrm{V}\cdot10/230}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.17}$ V. The fluctuation is 23 times smaller. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5.1\,\mathrm{V}\cdot40.5\,\mathrm{mA}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.21}$ W.

Exercise 6 ★★ Solar cell

A cell has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ nA and, in full sun, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{Ph}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ A.

  1. Calculate $U_\mathrm L$. How does $U_\mathrm L$ change when the light falls to one third?

  2. Calculate $I$ and the delivered power at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5}$ V.

  3. How many cells in series are needed to charge a 14 V battery at 0.5 V each?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{25.85\,\mathrm{mV}\cdot\ln(3\cdot10^{9}+1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.564}$ V. At 1 A, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.536}$ V, only 28 mV less. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-9}\,\mathrm{A}\cdot(\mathrm{e}^{19.34}-1)-3\,\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25\,\mathrm{A}-3\,\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2.75}$ A. The delivered power is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.5\,\mathrm{V}\cdot2.75\,\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.37}$ W. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{14\,\mathrm{V}/0.5\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{28}$ cells.
