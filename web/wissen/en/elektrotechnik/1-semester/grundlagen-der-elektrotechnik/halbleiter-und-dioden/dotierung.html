Stage 2: Doping

What this stage is about:

Pure silicon has too few charge carriers, and their number depends strongly on temperature. This stage fixes the type and number of carriers with foreign atoms.

The idea. Silicon has four valence electrons (electrons of the outermost shell). Each atom shares a pair of electrons with each of its four neighbors; this is the covalent bond. If an atom is replaced by phosphorus with five valence electrons, four bind and the fifth is left over (Fig. 3). It is held to the phosphorus core by only 45 meV, which is 1.7 times $k_\mathrm BT$. Heat almost always frees it, it becomes a free electron in the CB, and a stationary $\mathrm P^+$ ion remains. Boron has only three valence electrons. One bond stays open, and a neighboring electron jumps in, likewise with 45 meV. The stationary $\mathrm B^-$ ion remains, and the hole wanders off.

Definition 2.1 (n-type and p-type doping)

A donor (phosphorus) gives up an electron, an acceptor (boron) takes one up. $N_\mathrm D$ donors per cm$^3$ create an n-region, $N_\mathrm A$ acceptors per cm$^3$ a p-region:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{n-region: }n}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ N_\mathrm D,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{p-region: }p}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ N_\mathrm A,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{everywhere: }n\,p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n_\mathrm i^2.}$$
(2.1)

⁠How to read it: $n$ and $p$ are the densities of electrons and holes in 1/cm$^3$. The more frequent kind is called the majority carrier, the rarer one the minority carrier. Typical values are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{15}}$ to $10^{19}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Doping does not charge the crystal: every free electron has a stationary $\mathrm P^+$ belonging to it. In the n-region the Fermi energy rises by $k_\mathrm BT\ln(n/n_\mathrm i)$ above the middle of the gap. Validity: room temperature (all foreign atoms ionized) and $N\gg n_\mathrm i$.

Derivation: Why the product is constant

Heat generates pairs of electron and hole at a rate that depends only on $T$ and the material. Recombination requires the two to meet; its rate is $c\,n\,p$. In equilibrium both rates are equal, so $n\,p$ is constant. In pure silicon $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n_\mathrm i}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n\,p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n_\mathrm i^2}$. More electrons mean more recombination and fewer holes.

Example

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$ there is one foreign atom per $5\cdot10^{6}$ silicon atoms, and yet $n$ rises to a million times $n_\mathrm i$. The holes fall to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{20}/10^{16}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$. Majority and minority are separated by the factor $10^{12}$. $W_\mathrm F$ rises by $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{25.85\,\mathrm{meV}\cdot\ln10^{6}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.36}$ eV.

⁠Limiting case: For $N_\mathrm D\to0$, (2.1) again gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n_\mathrm i}$.

QuestionIs n-type silicon negatively charged and p-type silicon positively charged?

No, both are neutral. “n” and “p” name the sign of the mobile charges, not that of the piece. In the n-region, every free electron is matched by a stationary positive ion. Only at the pn junction do the two separate (Stage 3).

Exercise 2 ★★ Counting carriers

  1. Silicon receives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\cdot10^{15}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$. How many silicon atoms are there per foreign atom? How large are $n$ and $p$?

  2. How large are $n$ and $p$ at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{N_\mathrm A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$?

  3. Prediction: a piece receives equal numbers of donors and acceptors. What happens to the charge carriers?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{5\cdot10^{22}/5\cdot10^{15}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{7}}$ atoms per foreign atom, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\cdot10^{15}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{20}/5\cdot10^{15}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{500\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$. (c) Every donor electron falls onto an acceptor. No additional carriers arise, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n_\mathrm i}$. The crystal behaves like pure silicon even though it is full of ions. This is called compensation.
