Stage 9: Rectifiers and charge pump

What this stage is about:

The half-wave rectifier wastes every second half-wave. This stage uses both, and it shows how to raise a voltage above the source voltage with diodes and capacitors.

The idea. The bridge rectifier (Graetz circuit) consists of four diodes in a diamond (Fig. 11). They switch the load in each half-wave so that the current always passes through it from top to bottom. With positive voltage on the left, it runs through D$_1$, the load and D$_3$. Meanwhile D$_2$ has its cathode at the top on the high potential and its anode at the low input, so it blocks, and likewise D$_4$. In the other half-wave the pairs swap.

Theorem 9.1 (Bridge rectifier)

In the model with forward voltage, since the current always passes through two diodes in series,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{cases}|u_\mathrm e|-2U_\mathrm F&|u_\mathrm e|>2U_\mathrm F\\0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}}$$
(9.1)

A blocked diode bears at most $\hat u-U_\mathrm F$.

⁠How to read it: For $|u_\mathrm e|<2U_\mathrm F$ neither pair conducts, and $u_\mathrm a$ stays zero. At the lowered peak you can read off twice the forward voltage. Validity: resistive load. Input and output have no common reference point.

Example

The peak is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{12\,\mathrm{V}-1.4\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10.6}$ V; for the half-wave rectifier it is 11.3 V. The gap lasts 0.74 ms per half-wave, which is 7.4 % of the time. The mean value is 6.29 V; for the half-wave rectifier it is only 3.48 V.

⁠Limiting cases: For $U_\mathrm F\to0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|u_\mathrm e|}$. For $\hat u<1.4$ V the output stays zero.

QuestionThis still pulses strongly. Is this already DC voltage?

Not yet. A capacitor in parallel with the load (smoothing) charges to the peak and feeds the load during the gap. In 10 ms it loses $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I\,\Delta t/C}$ (Chapter 3). With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10.6}$ mA and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{470}$ µ F that is 0.23 V. The diodes then conduct only briefly at the peak.

Charge pump. It produces a higher voltage from a DC voltage $U_\mathrm e$, without a coil. The diodes are treated as ideal here (Fig. 12). Step 1: The changeover switch connects the bottom plate of $C_1$ to ground. $C_1$ charges through D$_1$ to $U_\mathrm e$. Step 2: The changeover switch connects the bottom plate to $U_\mathrm e$. The voltage across $C_1$ cannot jump (Chapter 3), so the top plate jumps to $2U_\mathrm e$. D$_1$ blocks because its cathode is now higher, D$_2$ conducts, and $C_1$ gives charge to the output capacitor $C_2$. After a few cycles, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2U_\mathrm e}$. In practice, small resistors limit the current peaks. With further pump stages on the same scheme, the voltage rises further.

Derivation: Voltage after $k$ cycles for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{C_2}$

In the first step, $C_2$ also charges to $U_\mathrm e$ through both diodes. In the pump step, $C_1$ with $2U_\mathrm e$ and $C_2$ with $U_\mathrm a$ are connected. Charge is conserved, and equal capacitors end at the mean value: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm a'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2U_\mathrm e+U_\mathrm a)/2}$. The distance to the final value $2U_\mathrm e$ halves per cycle: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm a(k)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2U_\mathrm e-U_\mathrm e\,2^{-k}}$.

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ V this gives 7.5 V, 8.75 V, 9.38 V, and so on up to 10 V. With real diodes you lose $U_\mathrm F$ twice: the final value is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2U_\mathrm e-2U_\mathrm F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8.6}$ V. Limiting case: If current flows at the output, charge is missing per cycle, and $U_\mathrm a$ stays below the final value.

Exercise 11 ★★ Power supply

A bridge is connected to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{24}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.7}$ V.

  1. How large is the peak of $u_\mathrm a$? What reverse voltage must each diode withstand?

  2. For what fraction of the time is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$?

  3. The load draws 100 mA. Which $C$ limits the fluctuation to 1 V? How large would it have to be for the half-wave rectifier, and why?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{24\,\mathrm{V}-1.4\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{22.6}$ V, reverse voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{24\,\mathrm{V}-0.7\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{23.3}$ V. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ for $|\sin|<1.4/24$, that is, 3.34° around each zero crossing, which is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot3.34^\circ/180^\circ}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.7}$ % of the time. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I\,\Delta t/\Delta U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100\,\mathrm{mA}\cdot10\,\mathrm{ms}/1\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1000}$ µ F. With the half-wave rectifier, replenishment comes only every 20 ms, so 2000 µ F.

Exercise 12 ★★ Counting pumps

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{C_2}$, ideal diodes.

  1. How large is $U_\mathrm a$ after 1, 2 and 3 cycles?

  2. After how many cycles is 99 % of the final value reached?

  3. What final value do real diodes with 0.7 V give? What remains at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5}$ V, and what follows from this for low-voltage circuits?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm a(k)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6\,\mathrm{V}-3\,\mathrm{V}\cdot2^{-k}}$: 4.5 V, 5.25 V, 5.63 V. (b) $3\,\mathrm{V}\cdot2^{-k}\le0.06\,\mathrm{V}$ requires $2^k\ge50$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{6\,\mathrm{V}-1.4\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.6}$ V. At 1.5 V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3\,\mathrm{V}-1.4\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.6}$ V remain, hardly more than the input. At low voltages the diodes are therefore replaced by switches.
