What this stage is about:

The meter movement ends at 50 µ A and 100 mV. This stage turns it into an ammeter up to 10 mA and a voltmeter up to 10 V and shows where $R_\mathrm{iA}$ and $R_\mathrm{iV}$ come from.

The idea. The meter movement tolerates only 50 µ A. If it is to show 10 mA, the rest must flow past it: a shunt (parallel resistor) lies in parallel and takes over 9.95 mA. If it is to show 10 V, only 100 mV may be across it: a series resistor takes over 9.9 V (Fig. 2). At full-scale deflection, the movement sees the same as before; only the labeling of the scale changes.

Theorem 2.1 (Shunt and series resistor)

Let an instrument have the internal resistance $R_\mathrm{old}$ and the full-scale value $I_\mathrm{old}$ or $U_\mathrm{old}$, respectively. For the new full-scale value $I_\mathrm{new}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{new}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\alpha\,U_\mathrm{old}}$, it needs

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{Shunt}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{old}\,\frac{I_\mathrm{old}}{I_\mathrm{new}-I_\mathrm{old}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{old}\,(\alpha-1).}$$
(2.1)

The new internal resistance is $R_\mathrm{old}\parallel R_\mathrm{Shunt}$ or $R_\mathrm{old}+R_\mathrm V$, respectively.

⁠How to read it: $\alpha>1$ is the factor of the extension, without a unit. $\parallel$ means parallel: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_a\parallel R_b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_aR_b/(R_a+R_b)}$. The shunt is small compared with $R_\mathrm{old}$, the series resistor large. Validity: The deflection must be proportional to the current, and the resistors must be accurate, because their error enters the reading.

Derivation: Current divider and voltage divider

Shunt and instrument are in parallel; $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/R}$ is the conductance. At full-scale deflection, the current divider gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{I_\mathrm{old}}{I_\mathrm{new}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_\mathrm{Shunt}}{R_\mathrm{Shunt}+R_\mathrm{old}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{G_\mathrm{old}}{G_\mathrm{Shunt}+G_\mathrm{old}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad R_\mathrm{Shunt}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{old}}{I_\mathrm{new}}\,(R_\mathrm{Shunt}+R_\mathrm{old}).}$$

Solving for $R_\mathrm{Shunt}$ gives (2.1). The series resistor and the instrument are in series. The voltage divider gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{old}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{new}\,\frac{R_\mathrm{old}}{R_\mathrm{old}+R_\mathrm V} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_\mathrm{old}+R_\mathrm V}{R_\mathrm{old}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_\mathrm V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{old}\,(\alpha-1).}$$

Example

For 10 mA: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{Shunt}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\cdot\frac{50\,\mu\text{A}}{9.95\,\mathrm{mA}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10.05\,\Omega}$, in parallel with 2 k$\Omega$ this gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{iA}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\Omega}$. For 10 V: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\cdot99}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{198}$ k$\Omega$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{iV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{200}$ k$\Omega$.

⁠In short: at full-scale deflection, 100 mV drop across the ammeter in every range, and 50 µ A flow through the voltmeter in every range. The internal resistances follow directly: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{iA}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100\,\mathrm{mV}/10\,\mathrm{mA}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\Omega}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{iV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\mathrm{V}/50\,\mu\text{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{200}$ k$\Omega$. So a sensitive current range has a large internal resistance, and so does a high voltage range. The reciprocal of 50 µ A, 20 k$\Omega$/V, is printed on pointer instruments as a characteristic value.

⁠Limiting case: For $I_\mathrm{new}\to I_\mathrm{old}$, $R_\mathrm{Shunt}\to\infty$; for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Without an extension, no component is needed.

Several ranges. A multirange meter (a device with several ranges) switches between them (Fig. 3). In the ammeter, each further switch places a shunt in parallel; in the voltmeter, each further open switch brings in a series resistor. Equation (2.1) holds step by step: the arrangement so far is the instrument, and its internal resistance is called $R_\mathrm{old}$.

QuestionDoes the shunt make the instrument more sensitive?

No, less sensitive. The meter movement still needs 50 µ A for full-scale deflection, and the shunt ensures that it takes 10 mA to get there.

Exercise 2 ★★ Range switch

The meter movement gets the circuits from Fig. 3. (a) Calculate $R_1$, $R_2$, $R_3$ for the current ranges 250 µ A, 1 mA, 10 mA and the internal resistance in each case. (b) Calculate $R_1$, $R_2$, $R_3$ for the voltage ranges 1 V, 10 V, 100 V. (c) Check both parts with the statement from “In short”.

Solution

(a) With (2.1) step by step:

$$\begin{aligned} R_1&=2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\cdot\tfrac{50}{250-50}=500\,\Omega, & R^*&=2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\parallel500\,\Omega=400\,\Omega,\\ R_2&=400\,\Omega\cdot\tfrac{250}{1000-250}=133.3\,\Omega, & R^{**}&=400\,\Omega\parallel133.3\,\Omega=100\,\Omega,\\ R_3&=100\,\Omega\cdot\tfrac{1}{10-1}=11.11\,\Omega, & R^{***}&=100\,\Omega\parallel11.11\,\Omega=10\,\Omega. \end{aligned}$$

In parallel, the three shunts give 10.05 $\Omega$, like the single shunt in the example.

(b) Each step has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\cdot9}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{18}$ k$\Omega$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\cdot9}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{180}$ k$\Omega$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R^{**}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{200}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{200\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\cdot9}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.8}$ M$\Omega$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R^{***}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ M$\Omega$.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{250\,\mu\text{A}\cdot400\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{mA}\cdot100\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\mathrm{mA}\cdot10\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$ mV. For the voltmeter, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{V}/20\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\mathrm{V}/200\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100\,\mathrm{V}/2\,\mathrm{M\Omega}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{50}$ µ A.
