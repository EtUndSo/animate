Stage 7: The unbalanced bridge

What this stage is about:

If a resistance changes only slightly or continuously, you do not readjust. The deviation from zero is then the measuring signal. This stage shows why the smallest changes become visible this way.

The idea. An instrument alone has to display the entire voltage, of which the change is a tiny part. The bridge subtracts the large common voltage. Only the change remains, and it fits in a small measuring range. Because the reading error is tied to the full-scale value, it falls with the range.

Theorem 7.1 (Bridge voltage)

Between the midpoints there is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_4-U_2}$ with the voltages $U_2$ across $R_2$ and $U_4$ across $R_4$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q\left(\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}-\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\right)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q\,\frac{R_1R_4-R_2R_3}{(R_1+R_2)(R_3+R_4)}.}$$
(7.1)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R+\Delta R}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm q}{4}\cdot\frac{\Delta R/R}{1+\Delta R/(2R)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm q}{4}\cdot\frac{\Delta R}{R}}$$
$$\text{for }\Delta R\ll R.$$
(7.2)

⁠How to read it: The numerator in (7.1) becomes zero at balance. $\Delta R/R$ is the relative change, without a unit. Validity: The voltmeter draws no appreciable current. Limiting cases: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. $\Delta R\to\infty$ gives $U_\mathrm d\to U_\mathrm q/2$: the right midpoint is then at the plus terminal.

Derivation: Small change

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q/2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q(R+\Delta R)/(2R+\Delta R)}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q\,\frac{2(R+\Delta R)-(2R+\Delta R)}{2(2R+\Delta R)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm q\,\Delta R}{4R+2\Delta R}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm q}{4}\cdot\frac{\Delta R/R}{1+\Delta R/(2R)}.}$$

Examplestrain gauge

A strain gauge is a thin metal track that is glued onto a component. If the component stretches, the track becomes longer and thinner, and its resistance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\rho\,l/A}$ rises. The relative change in resistance is about twice the strain. At 2 ‰ strain, $R_4$ grows by 0.4 % from 350 $\Omega$ to 351.4 $\Omega$. Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.500}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9\,\mathrm{V}\cdot351.4/701.4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.509}$ V, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8.98}$ mV. The approximation gives 9.00 mV, which deviates by 0.2 %. In the 10 M$\Omega$ voltmeter, 0.9 nA flow; compared with 12.9 mA in the branches, this is a share of $7\cdot10^{-8}$: negligible.

Why this is more accurate. Measured directly, the instrument would have to distinguish 4.509 V from 4.500 V, that is, reliably display three decimal places in the 10 V range. But its reading error of $\pm100$ mV is eleven times the change being sought. On the bridge, the 100 mV range with $\pm1$ mV is enough, a hundredth of that (Fig. 9). Two advantages are added. If the source fluctuates by 1 %, $U_4$ changes by 45 mV, five times the signal, whereas $U_\mathrm d$ changes by only 0.09 mV. And if all four resistors change by the same factor, for instance through heat, $U_\mathrm d$ stays the same.

QuestionCouldn't you measure $U_4$ and subtract 4.5 V in your head?

You can calculate that, but the $\pm100$ mV reading error remains in the result. The bridge subtracts before the instrument measures. That is why the instrument may have a small range.

⁠From here on: Unless stated otherwise, meters count as ideal in all the following parts: ammeters with $R_\mathrm i\to0$, voltmeters with $R_\mathrm i\to\infty$.

Exercise 8 ★★★ Two strain gauges

The right branch now contains two strain gauges: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R-\Delta R}$ is compressed, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R+\Delta R}$ stretched, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R}$. (a) Calculate $U_\mathrm d$ in general and for the example. (b) Why is the result exactly linear in $\Delta R$? (c) What changes if all four resistors change by 1 % through heat?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_3+R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2R}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q(R+\Delta R)/(2R)}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q/2}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12U_\mathrm q\,\Delta R/R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.5\,\mathrm{V}\cdot0.004}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{18.0}$ mV, twice as much as with one gauge.

(b) The sum $R_3+R_4$ stays $2R$. The current in the right branch therefore stays 12.9 mA, and $U_4$ grows exactly with $R_4$. With one gauge, the sum grows as well, which produces the denominator in (7.2).

(c) Nothing: in (7.1), a common factor of all the resistors cancels.
