Chapter 1: Charge, Field and Potential

Key points

Overview: Charge, Field and Potential

Terms
  • voltage – A small rectangular battery is labeled 9 V. This figure is a voltage.
  • charge – It does not say how much energy the battery contains, but how much energy each unit of charge gives up as it passes from one terminal to the other: 9 joules per coulomb.
  • electric field – An electric field determines, for every location, what force acts on a charge there.
  • potential, reference point – The potential determines, for every location, how much energy per coulomb a charge has there relative to a freely chosen zero point, the reference point.
  • resistor – A resistor is a component in which passing charge gives up energy as heat (Chapter 2).

From the ground up: What electrical engineering is and where charge comes from

Terms
  • electrical engineering – The discipline of electrical engineering is the engineering science that moves electric charges in a controlled way in order to transmit energy and process information.
  • atom, protons, neutrons, electrons – An atom has a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons, around which electrons move (Fig. 1).
  • neutral – An atom contains as many protons as electrons; seen from outside, their charges cancel, and it is neutral.
  • ion – An atom with a surplus charge is called an ion.

Stage 1: Quantities, force and work

Terms
  • qualitative – The idea. Someone coming in from the snow calls a room warm; someone coming out of the sauna calls it cool. Such judgments are qualitative.
  • quantitative – A thermometer shows both of them 21 °C; that is quantitative: a number on a scale, independent of who is measuring.
  • Ohm's law – Physics states its laws in terms of such quantities, for example Ohm's law $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\cdot I}$ (Chapter 2).
  • physical quantity – A physical quantity is a measurable property, the product of numerical value and unit.
  • SI system of units
  • base quantities
  • current – An electric current is moving charge; one ampere transports one coulomb per second.
  • SI prefixes – SI prefixes scale a numerical value by powers of ten.
  • force – Force and work. A force deforms a body that is held in place or accelerates a movable one.
  • Energy – Energy is the capacity to do work.
  • vectors – Force and displacement are vectors: quantities with magnitude and direction, written with an arrow, $\vec F$.
  • Velocity, acceleration – Velocity is the change of position per time, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{d} s/\mathrm{d} t}$, acceleration the change of velocity per time, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{d} v/\mathrm{d} t}$.
  • work – The work done by a force along a path is …
  • kinetic energy – A body with the velocity $v$ carries the kinetic energy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm{kin}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12m\,v^2}$.
  • gravitational constant – ⁠How to read it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[F]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm N}$; at the surface of the Earth, about 1 N acts on 100 g. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\gamma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.674\cdot10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}}$ is the gravitational constant.
  • scalar product – The product $\vec F\cdot\Delta\vec s$ is called the scalar product; its result is a number.
  • line integral – The integral is called a line integral: the path is divided into short straight pieces and their contributions are summed.

Stage 2: Charge

Terms
  • electron deficit, electron excess – A body with an electron deficit is positively charged, one with an electron excess negatively.
  • elementary charge – The elementary charge $e$ is the smallest charge occurring freely.
  • conservation of charge – In a closed system, the sum of all charges stays constant (conservation of charge).
  • conductor – Conductors and insulators. In a conductor such as metal, at least one electron per atom can wander through the entire body.
  • insulator – In an insulator such as glass or rubber, the electrons stay with their atoms.
Formulas

Terms

  • point charges – Validity: The bodies must be small compared with $r$ (point charges) and at rest.
  • superposition – Several charges act independently, and their forces add as vectors (superposition).

Stage 4: The field strength

Terms
  • test charge, electric field strength – If a small test charge $Q$ is brought to a location and the force $\vec F$ on it is measured, the electric field strength there is …
Formulas
  • Definition 4.1 (Electric field strength) (4.1)
    $$\vec E:=\lim_{Q\to0}\frac{\vec F}{Q},$$
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{hence}\quad\vec F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\cdot\vec E.}$$
  • Theorem 4.2 (Field of point charges) (4.2)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{Q_0}{r^2}\cdot\frac{\vec r}{r},}$$
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{|Q_0|}{r^2},}$$
Key relationships
  • the force on a charge is its charge times the field at its location.

Stage 5: Field lines and conductors

Terms
  • electrostatic induction – In metal, the free electrons move until no force acts on them any more. This displacement is called electrostatic induction.
  • field line – A field line is a curve whose tangent at each point has the direction of $\vec E$; the density of the lines shows the strength of the field.
  • sources, sinks – Field lines begin on positive charges (sources) and end on negative ones (sinks), never in empty space.
  • shielding – A charge-free cavity enclosed by the conductor is field-free (shielding).

Stage 8: Voltage and ground

Stage 10: Limit of the picture: magnetism and induction

Terms
  • magnetic field – In 1820 Ørsted saw a compass needle deflect next to a current-carrying wire: moving charge produces a magnetic field $H$.
  • coil – In 1831 Faraday reversed the idea: a magnet that he moved in a coil, a wire wound into turns, produced a voltage at its ends.
  • induction – In 1831 Faraday reversed the idea: a magnet that he moved in a coil, a wire wound into turns, produced a voltage at its ends. This is called induction.
  • electromagnetism – In 1864 Maxwell combined everything into electromagnetism.
  • curl field – The mechanism of induction is a new field: a changing magnetic field produces an electric field with closed lines, a curl field.
  • right-hand rule – The direction of the magnetic field is given by the right-hand rule: the thumb points in the conventional current direction, the fingers in the direction of the field.
  • conventional current direction – The conventional current direction runs from plus to minus; the electrons in the wire move the opposite way (Chapter 2).
  • magnetic flux – ⁠How to read it: The magnetic flux $\Phi$ measures how much magnetic field passes through the coil area, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[\Phi]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{V\,s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{Wb}}$ (weber).
  • flux density – The flux density here is the measure of the density of the field lines.
Formulas
  • Theorem 10.1 (Law of induction) (10.1)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{ind}(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-N\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d} t}.}$$

Chapter 2: Current, Resistance and Kirchhoff's Laws

Key points

Overview: Current, Resistance and Kirchhoff's Laws

Terms
  • current – A current is moving charge, measured as charge per time.
  • resistor – A resistor is a component that slows this motion and converts its energy into heat.
  • Ohm's law – Ohm's law links voltage and current at a component.
  • node rule – The node rule says that no current disappears at a junction.
  • mesh rule – The mesh rule says that the voltages along a closed path add up to zero.

Stage 1: Current is moving charge

Terms
  • charge density – The charge density $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Delta Q/\Delta V}$ is the charge per volume.
  • electric current – The electric current (current for short) is the charge that passes through an area per time.
  • circuit – Second, the circuit must be closed, that is, a path that returns to itself.
  • steady – A current that is constant in time in a closed circuit is called steady.
Formulas
  • Definition 1.1 (Current and current density) (1.1) (1.2)
    $$\begin{aligned} i&=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}=\frac{\mathrm{d} Q}{\mathrm{d} t},\qquad I=q\,v\,A,\qquad [I]=\mathrm{C/s}=\mathrm A,\\ j&=\frac IA=q\,v,\qquad\vec j=q\,\vec v. \end{aligned}$$

Stage 2: Slow electrons, immediate current

Terms
  • crystal lattice – The positive ions left behind form a regular crystal lattice.
  • mobility – $\mu$ is the mobility, the drift velocity per field strength, in $\mathrm{m^2/(V\,s)}$.
  • mean free path – In this time, the electron covers on average the mean free path $\lambda$.
Formulas
  • Theorem 2.1 (Drift velocity) (2.1)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\vec v_\mathrm{max}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{e\,|\vec E|}{m_\mathrm e}\,T,}$$
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec v_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mu\,\vec E,}$$
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mu}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{e\,T}{2\,m_\mathrm e}.}$$
Key relationships
  • The drift velocity is the current divided by the free charge per meter. Limiting cases: Without a field, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Twice the current gives twice the drift. Many collisions ($T\to0$) give $\mu\to0$: the material does not conduct.

Stage 3: Ohm's law from field and collisions

Terms
  • conductivity, resistivity – ⁠How to read it: $\sigma$ is the conductivity in S/m, $\rho$ the resistivity in $\Omega\,\mathrm m$.
  • conductance – $G$ is the conductance in S (siemens), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{S}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\Omega^{-1}}$.
  • linear, homogeneous – Validity: The conductor must be linear ($\vec j$ proportional to $\vec E$) and homogeneous (the same material everywhere).
  • power – The power, the energy per time, is therefore $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I^2}$.
Formulas
  • Theorem 3.1 (Ohm's law) (3.1) (3.2)
    $$\begin{aligned} \vec j&=\sigma\,\vec E,\qquad\sigma=n\,e\,\mu,\qquad\rho=\frac1\sigma,\\ U&=R\,I,\qquad R=\frac{L}{\sigma\,A}=\rho\,\frac LA,\qquad G=\frac1R=\frac IU. \end{aligned}$$

Stage 4: Real resistors: values and temperature

Terms
  • Metal-film or carbon-film resistors – Types of construction. Metal-film or carbon-film resistors carry a thin layer on a ceramic body.
  • Wire-wound resistors – Wire-wound resistors withstand high power and temperature and are suited to small values.
  • SMD – SMD resistors (Surface Mounted Device) are soldered directly onto the circuit board.
  • E series – An E series E$N$ contains $N$ values per decade; according to DIN IEC 60063 there are E3, E6, E12, E24, E48, E96 and E192.
  • temperature coefficient – The temperature coefficient $\alpha$ has the unit 1/K: copper $3.9\cdot10^{-3}$, tungsten $4.5\cdot10^{-3}$, carbon $-0.5\cdot10^{-3}$, constantan around $10^{-5}$.
  • PTC, cold conductor – For $\alpha>0$, the material is called PTC (positive temperature coefficient) or cold conductor: it conducts better when cold.
  • NTC, hot conductor – For $\alpha<0$, it is called NTC (negative temperature coefficient) or hot conductor.
  • Compensated alloys – Compensated alloys such as constantan: the collisions with the irregularly embedded impurity atoms predominate and hardly depend on temperature.
Formulas
  • Definition 4.1 (E series) (4.1)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\sqrt[N]{10}\,\bigr)^{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt[N]{10^{i}},}$$
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,1,\dots,N-1.}$$
  • Theorem 4.2 (Temperature dependence) (4.2)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R(\vartheta)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R(\vartheta_0)\,\bigl(1+\alpha_{\vartheta_0}\,(\vartheta-\vartheta_0)\bigr).}$$

Stage 5: Two-terminal elements and reference arrows

Terms

  • characteristic curve – In the steady state (time-independent), that is its characteristic curve $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(U)}$.

  • passive sign convention
  • active sign convention
  • conventional current direction – The conventional current direction is the direction in which positive charge would move: outside the source from plus to minus.
Formulas
  • Definition 5.1 (Two-terminal element, black box, network) (5.1)

  • Definition 5.2 (Sign conventions) (5.2)

Stage 6: Nodes, branches, meshes

Terms
  • node – A node is a point at which the current branches.
  • branch – A branch connects two nodes via two-terminal elements, and a current flows in it.
  • graph – The graph is the representation made of nodes and branches.
  • mesh – A mesh is a closed path in which each node is connected to exactly two neighboring branches.
  • tree – A tree is a connected subgraph that contains all nodes and forms no mesh.
  • link branches – The remaining branches are called link branches.
  • linearly independent – Meshes are called linearly independent if they can be ordered so that each contains at least one branch missing from the previous ones.
  • complete – They form a complete system: no further mesh adds anything new.

Stage 9: Series connection and voltage divider

Terms
  • series connection – For $N$ resistors in a series connection, …
  • total resistance – ⁠How to read it: $R_\mathrm{tot}$ is the total resistance, the one resistor that replaces the chain.
  • unloaded – Validity: The divider rule holds only when unloaded: no current may be drawn at the tap, otherwise $I_1\neq I_2$ (Stage 10).
  • digital-to-analog converter – Application: digital-to-analog converter. A digital-to-analog converter (D/A converter) turns a number into a voltage.
  • bits – Three bits (binary digits $b_0$, $b_1$, $b_2$) give the numbers $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ to 7. A chain of $R/2$, seven $R$ and $R/2$ has the total resistance $8R$ and divides $U_\mathrm{ref}$ into eight steps.
  • buffer – A buffer, an amplifier with the factor 1, outputs the voltage without drawing current from the chain, so that the divider stays unloaded.
  • comparator – A comparator, a device that reports which of two voltages is higher, compares the input voltage with every tap, all at the same time.

Stage 1: Why silicon hardly conducts

  • energy levels – The idea. In a single atom, electrons have only certain energies, the energy levels.

  • ideal op amp – The ideal op amp has $r_\mathrm{e}\to\infty$ (no input current), $A\to\infty$ at all frequencies, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{a}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ (the output current adjusts itself so that all node and mesh equations are satisfied) and no noise.

Stage 4: Negative feedback

Terms
  • Feedback – The idea. Feedback means that part of the output quantity gets back to the input.
  • positive feedback – If it counteracts the cause, it is negative feedback; if it reinforces the cause, it is positive feedback.
  • non-inverting amplifier – In the non-inverting amplifier, $U_\mathrm{E}$ is at the plus input (Fig. 6).
  • voltage follower – This voltage follower does not amplify but isolates: input resistance 100 G$\Omega$, output resistance 0.75 m$\Omega$.
Formulas
  • Theorem 4.1 (Amplifier with negative feedback) (4.1)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{E}}{1+A\beta},}$$
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{A}}{U_\mathrm{E}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{A}{1+A\beta}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1\beta\cdot\frac{1}{1+\dfrac1{A\beta}}.}$$
  • Theorem 4.1 (Amplifier with negative feedback) (4.2)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\Delta G}{G}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{1+A\beta}\cdot\frac{\Delta A}{A}.}$$
Key relationships
  • The gain is the reciprocal of the fed-back fraction. Limiting cases: $A\to\infty$ gives $U_\mathrm{D}\to0$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/\beta}$. $\beta\to0$ gives $G\to A$; without feedback, the open loop remains.
