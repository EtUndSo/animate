Stage 6: Several inputs, several stages

What this stage is about:

The rule was for one input and one op amp. This stage shows that it handles several inputs and whole chains without a new idea, because all the equations remain linear.

The idea. In the summing amplifier, several resistors lead to the minus input (Fig. 8). The node stays at 0 V, so each source sees only its own resistor and drives $U_{\mathrm{E}i}/R_{\mathrm{E}i}$, independently of the others. The virtual ground decouples the sources. All the currents together flow through $R_\mathrm{F}$, the feedback resistor. In the difference amplifier, one voltage arrives at each input. Here the superposition from Chapter 5 helps: you calculate each source on its own, and the other becomes a wire.

Theorem 6.1 (Summing amplifier, difference amplifier, chain)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{F}\sum_i\frac{U_{\mathrm{E}i}}{R_{\mathrm{E}i}},}$$
(6.1)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1}\cdot\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}\,U_\mathrm{E2}-\frac{R_2}{R_1}\,U_\mathrm{E1}.}$$
(6.2)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2}$, what remains is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_2}{R_1}\,(U_\mathrm{E2}-U_\mathrm{E1})}$. Stages in a chain multiply: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G_1\,G_2}$.

⁠How to read it: In (6.2), $U_\mathrm{E1}$ is at the minus input through $R_1$ and $U_\mathrm{E2}$ at the divider $R_3$, $R_4$ ahead of the plus input; $R_2$ leads back. Validity: Op amp in the linear range. With superposition only the sum has to lie within the limits; the partial results are calculation quantities.

Derivation: Difference amplifier by superposition

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ no current flows through $R_3$, $R_4$, and the plus input is at 0 V. This is the inverting amplifier: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-(R_2/R_1)\,U_\mathrm{E1}}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ the source becomes a wire, so $R_1$ stays connected to ground and must not be dropped, because the output drives current through it via $R_2$. This is the non-inverting amplifier with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_+}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{E2}\,R_4/(R_3+R_4)}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(1+R_2/R_1\bigr)\varphi_+}$. The sum is (6.2).

Examples

⁠Summing amplifier with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{F}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{E1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{E2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{E3}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ k$\Omega$ and $0.5$ V, $-0.2$ V, $0.1$ V: the currents are 50, $-20$ and 5 µ A, together 35 µ A, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1.4}$ V. Difference amplifier with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.00}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.05}$ V: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_+}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.8\cdot2.05\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.64}$ V. Through $R_1$ flows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(2.00-1.64)\,\mathrm{V}/10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{36}$ µ A, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.64\,\mathrm{V}-36\,\mu\text{A}\cdot40\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.2}$ V. Superposition gives $-8.0\,\mathrm{V}+8.2\,\mathrm{V}$, the same. The common mode of about 2 V is gone. Chain of two inverting amplifiers with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}$: 0.5 V becomes $-2$ V, then $+4$ V, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+8}$.

⁠Limiting case: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{E2}}$ gives 0.

Procedure for larger circuits. First determine the potentials of the inputs, then calculate stage by stage from left to right. In a chain, each output is the input of the next stage, a potential with a fixed value.

QuestionIn Chapter 6, two-ports in a chain loaded each other. Why may I multiply here?

Because the output resistance of the first stage is almost zero. The 10 k$\Omega$ of the second stage load 3.75 m$\Omega$, and the voltage loss is $4\cdot10^{-7}$ of the value. The loop of the first stage compensates for it.

Exercise 6 ★★★ Summing amplifier and unequal resistors

(a) A summing amplifier has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{F}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ k$\Omega$ and the input resistors 10, 20 and 5 k$\Omega$. How large is $U_\mathrm{A}$ for 1 V, $-2$ V and 0.5 V? (b) On the difference amplifier from the example, $R_4$ deviates upward by 1 % (40.4 k$\Omega$). Calculate the factors in front of $U_\mathrm{E2}$ and $U_\mathrm{E1}$. (c) What output voltage arises at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{E2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ V? (d) What is $U_\mathrm{A}$ now at 2.00 V and 2.05 V? Why must a difference amplifier have tightly toleranced resistors?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-(2\cdot1+1\cdot(-2)+4\cdot0.5)\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}$ V. (b) In front of $U_\mathrm{E2}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{5\cdot40.4/50.4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.0079}$, in front of $U_\mathrm{E1}$: 4. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\,\mathrm{V}\cdot0.0079}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{15.9}$ mV instead of 0. (d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4.0079\cdot2.05\,\mathrm{V}-8\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.216}$ V instead of 0.200 V. The 8 % error comes almost entirely from the common mode. A resistor with 1 % deviation makes the rejection $4/0.0079$, that is, 54 dB, worse than the op amp itself.
