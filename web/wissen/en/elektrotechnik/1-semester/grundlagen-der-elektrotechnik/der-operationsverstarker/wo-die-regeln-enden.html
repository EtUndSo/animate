What this stage is about:

Up to here the op amp was ideal except for $A$ and the limits. This stage orders the real deviations by size and summarizes the procedure.

The idea. Real deviations act in two ways. Some shift the point the loop regulates to: the transistors T1 and T2 are never exactly equal, so the rest point is not $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{off}}$. The others reduce the reserve $A\beta$. Above all the frequency: so that the loop does not oscillate, an internal capacitance limits the bandwidth. The voltage across it cannot follow fast signals, and $A$ falls.

The offset is the input voltage at which the output becomes zero. In the loop, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_+-\varphi_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{off}}$ establishes itself, and the divider enlarges this by $1/\beta$. The op amp's own noise adds in quadrature to the sensor from Stage 1: 2.31 µ V instead of 1.82 µ V, and the SNR falls by 2.1 dB to 52.7 dB.

Definition 10.1 (Common-mode rejection)

The common-mode rejection CMRR (common mode rejection ratio) compares the gain of the difference with that of the common mode:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{CMRR}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{A}{A_\mathrm{cm}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{CMRR}_\mathrm{dB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20\lg\frac{A}{A_\mathrm{cm}}.}$$
(10.1)

Ideally $\mathrm{CMRR}\to\infty$; usual values are 60 dB ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{cm}/A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-3}}$) to 120 dB ($10^{-6}$).

⁠How to read it: Mechanism: a real T3 has a finite $r_\mathrm{CE}$. If the common mode rises, E rises, and the total current grows by $U_\mathrm{cm}/r_\mathrm{CE}$. In the differential stage from Stage 2 with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40}$ k$\Omega$, each collector changes with the factor $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{C}/(2r_\mathrm{CE})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0375}$ against 57.7 for the difference: 63.7 dB. In the reference model it is 90 dB, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{cm}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.16}$. On the difference amplifier from Stage 6, 2 V of common mode acts like $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\,\mathrm{V}/31600}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{63}$ µ V of difference, which is 0.32 mV at the output, 0.16 % of the measured 0.2 V.

The frequency. Above $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_\mathrm{T}/A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ Hz, $A$ falls by a factor of 10 per decade, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|A(f)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ f_\mathrm{T}/f}$. At the transition frequency $f_\mathrm{T}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|A|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. The loop works as long as $|A(f)|\,\beta\gg1$. The reserve is used up at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\beta\,f_\mathrm{T}}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|G(f)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1/\beta}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_G)^2}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\beta\,f_\mathrm{T},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G\,f_G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_\mathrm{T}.}$$
(10.2)

⁠How to read it: With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ the bandwidth reaches 200 kHz. The product of gain and bandwidth is fixed: with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ it would be 1 MHz, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$ only 10 kHz. The time constant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau_G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.80}$ µ s from Stage 4 is the same value: $1/(2\pi\cdot200\,\mathrm{kHz})$. Limiting case: For $f\gg f_G$, $|G|\to|A|$; the feedback no longer acts.

Going deeper, not needed for the rest: Slew rate

The internal charging current is limited. That is why the output has a maximum speed, the slew rate, typically 0.5 V/µ s. The step from Fig. 5 thus really takes about 10 µ s instead of a few µ s. A sine wave with 11 V amplitude stays undistorted only up to about 7.2 kHz, even though the bandwidth is much larger.

The calculation scheme

  1. ⁠No feedback (comparator): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{A,max}}$ or $U_\mathrm{A,min}$, $U_\mathrm{D}\ne0$.

  2. ⁠Check the sign: Raise the output in your mind. If $U_\mathrm{D}$ falls, it is negative feedback; if it rises, positive feedback.

  3. ⁠Negative feedback: Assume $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and calculate $U_\mathrm{A}$ with the node rule and Ohm's law. If it lies within the limits, the calculation is valid. Otherwise the op amp is overdriven: $U_\mathrm{A}$ is at its limit, $U_\mathrm{D}\ne0$, and you calculate the network with this fixed $U_\mathrm{A}$.

  4. ⁠Positive feedback: As a rule, $U_\mathrm{A}$ at the limit, $U_\mathrm{D}\ne0$. The thresholds follow from the sign change of $U_\mathrm{D}$.

  5. ⁠Accuracy: $1/(A\beta)$ at the signal frequency, $U_\mathrm{off}/\beta$ and $I_\mathrm{B}R_2$ at the output.

Exercise 11 ★★★ Measuring amplifier

A sensor with 10 mV is to be amplified to about 1 V using V: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$ k$\Omega$, non-inverting. (a) How large are $\beta$, $G$ and the deviation due to $A$? (b) Up to what frequency does the circuit reach? How large is $|G|$ at 5 kHz? (c) How large is the offset at the output, and what share of the signal is it? How much does $I_\mathrm{B}$ add? (d) Up to what input does the output reach? (e) An integrator with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R\,C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ ms has the same offset. How fast does its output run at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and when does it hit its limit?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/101}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{101}$, deviation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{101/A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.10}$ %. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{MHz}/101}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9.9}$ kHz. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|G(5\,\mathrm{kHz})|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{101/\sqrt{1+(5/9.9)^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{90.2}$. Such a circuit already loses a tenth at 5 kHz. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{101\cdot1\,\mathrm{mV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{101}$ mV, that is, 10 % of the signal of 1.01 V. In addition $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{B}R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ mV. (d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{11\,\mathrm{V}/101}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{109}$ mV. (e) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{mV}/10\,\mathrm{ms}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.1}$ V/s, at the limit after 110 s. A real integrator therefore needs a reset switch or a large resistor in parallel with $C$.
