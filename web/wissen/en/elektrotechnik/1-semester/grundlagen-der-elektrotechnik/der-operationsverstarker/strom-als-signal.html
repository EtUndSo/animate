What this stage is about:

So far the voltage was the signal. But many sources supply a current. This stage converts current into voltage and shows that the op amp can emulate a negative resistance.

Current into voltage. A photodiode supplies a current that hardly depends on its voltage. In the transimpedance amplifier it flows directly into the virtual ground and has to pass through $R$ (Fig. 10 left):

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R\,I_\mathrm{E},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\Delta U_-}{\Delta I_\mathrm{E}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R}{1+A}.}$$
(8.1)

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ M$\Omega$, 2 µ A becomes $-2$ V. The node moves by only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\,\mathrm{V}/A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ µ V. This is the small input resistance of 10 $\Omega$, a value for changes (differential resistance). The source sees almost a short circuit, and its internal resistance plays no role.

A negative resistance. At positive voltage a resistor draws current in the direction of the reference arrow (passive sign convention, Chapter 2). The negative impedance converter (NIC) instead pushes current out (Fig. 10 right). Terminal 1 is at the plus input, and from there an $R$ leads to the output. From the output an equal $R$ leads to the minus input, and from there $R_2$ to ground. From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_+}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_-}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_2}$.

Theorem 8.1 (NIC)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_1}{I_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_2.}$$
(8.2)

The rule holds only as long as the resistance $R_\mathrm{i}$ that acts at terminal 1 with respect to ground is smaller than $R_2$.

⁠How to read it: $I_1$ counts into terminal 1, $U_1$ with respect to ground. $U_2$ and $I_2$ belong to $R_2$. $R_\mathrm{i}$ is everything hanging on terminal 1 from outside, for example source and load in parallel. Validity: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_1(1+R/R_2)}$ must lie within the limits.

Derivation: Why the sign flips and when the rule holds

Through $R_2$ flows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_2/R_2}$, and no current goes into the input, so it also flows through the lower $R$: the output is at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_2+R\,I_2}$. The upper $R$ has the same voltage and carries the same current, from the output to terminal 1. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_1/I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_2}$.

There are two feedback paths here: the lower $R$ gives negative feedback, the left one positive feedback. If $U_\mathrm{A}$ changes, $\varphi_-$ changes by the fraction $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2/(R_2+R)}$ and $\varphi_+$ by $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta_+}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{i}/(R_\mathrm{i}+R)}$. Negative feedback prevails only for $\beta_->\beta_+$, that is, for $R_\mathrm{i}<R_2$.

Example

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ k$\Omega$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.2}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.2}$ mA, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-5}$ k$\Omega$. The energy comes from the supply of the op amp. Application: A current source with 1 mA and internal resistance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ k$\Omega$ delivers only 0.909 mA to 1 k$\Omega$, because part of it goes through $R_\mathrm{q}$. In parallel with it, a NIC with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{q}}$: the conductance $1/R_\mathrm{q}-1/R_2$ vanishes, and the load gets 1 mA. The real source acts like an ideal one. The NIC supplies exactly the 0.1 mA that $R_\mathrm{q}$ branches off.

⁠Limiting case: Without a load, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2}$, both feedback paths are equally strong, the loop gain is zero, and the rule no longer holds.

Exercise 9 ★★★ Compensating a current source

A NIC with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ k$\Omega$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.7}$ k$\Omega$ is in parallel with a source with 2 mA and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.7}$ k$\Omega$. The load is 1 k$\Omega$. (a) What current does the load get without the NIC, and what with it? (b) Where is the op amp output? (c) Up to what load resistance does the output's 11 V suffice? (d) Why is the circuit stable with a load but not without one?

Solution

(a) Without the NIC $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\,\mathrm{mA}\cdot4.7/5.7}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.65}$ mA, with the NIC 2 mA. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.426}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\mathrm{V}+10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\cdot0.426\,\mathrm{mA}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.26}$ V. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_1(1+R/R_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.13\,U_1\le11}$ V gives $U_1\le3.52$ V, so $R_\mathrm{L}\le1.76$ k$\Omega$. (d) With a load, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{q}\parallel1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{825\,\Omega<R_2}$, and negative feedback prevails. Without a load, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2}$, and the two feedback paths cancel each other.
