What this stage is about:

So far one resistor turned voltage into current and a second turned current into voltage. This stage inserts other components without changing the rule. That is how the op amp got its name: it carries out mathematical operations.

The idea. If the plus input is at ground, the node rule at the minus input says: the current of the input component flows entirely through the feedback component, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_\mathrm{fb}}$. The input component sees $U_\mathrm{E}$ and supplies a current. The feedback component sees $U_\mathrm{A}$ and has to carry the same current. Which function arises is determined solely by their current-voltage laws (Fig. 9).

Theorem 7.1 (Operations)

With the plus input at ground and an ideal op amp,

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Integrator: }&u_\mathrm{A}(t)=u_\mathrm{A}(0)-\frac{1}{R\,C}\int_0^t u_\mathrm{E}(t')\,\mathrm{d} t', \\ \text{Differentiator: }&u_\mathrm{A}(t)=-R\,C\,\frac{\mathrm{d} u_\mathrm{E}}{\mathrm{d} t}, \\ \text{Logarithmic amplifier: }&U_\mathrm{A}=-U_T\,\ln\frac{U_\mathrm{E}}{R\,I_\mathrm{S}}, \\ \text{Exponential amplifier: }&U_\mathrm{A}=-R\,I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{E}/U_T}. \end{aligned}$$
(7.1)(7.2)(7.3)(7.4)

⁠How to read it: $R\,C$ is a time constant in s, $I_\mathrm{S}$ the saturation reverse current of the diode, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{26}$ mV. Validity: (7.3) requires $U_\mathrm{E}>0$ and $U_\mathrm{E}/R\gg I_\mathrm{S}$, (7.4) requires $U_\mathrm{E}\gg U_T$, and all require an output within its limits.

Derivation: Four node rules

Integrator: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\mathrm{E}/R}$ flows through $R$. It charges the capacitor, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C\,\mathrm{d} u_C/\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{i}$. The left plate is firmly at 0, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-u_C}$. Differentiator: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{C\,\mathrm{d} u_\mathrm{E}/\mathrm{d} t}$ through the capacitor at the input, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R\,i}$. Logarithmic amplifier: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{E}/R}$ flows through the diode, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{F}/U_T}}$ with the diode voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{F}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-U_\mathrm{A}}$. Solving gives (7.3). Exponential amplifier: $U_\mathrm{E}$ is across the diode, and its current $I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{E}/U_T}$ flows through $R$.

Examples

⁠Integrator with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ nF, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R\,C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$ µ s. At $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ V a fixed current of 100 µ A flows. Unlike in the RC circuit from Chapter 3, it does not decrease during charging, because the voltage across $R$ stays at 1 V. $u_\mathrm{A}$ falls steadily by 10 V/ms and reaches $-11$ V after 1.1 ms. A square wave of $\pm1$ V at 1 kHz becomes a triangle wave of $\pm2.5$ V. Differentiator with the same $R\,C$: a triangle wave of $\pm1$ V at 1 kHz rises by 4 V/ms, and $u_\mathrm{A}$ becomes a square wave of $\pm0.4$ V. For a sine wave its magnitude grows with frequency, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat u_\mathrm{A}/\hat u_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\pi f\,R\,C}$: 0.63 at 1 kHz, 62.8 at 100 kHz. That is why it amplifies high-frequency noise (Stage 1) particularly strongly. Logarithmic amplifier with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ k$\Omega$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{S}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.9\cdot10^{-13}}$ A: 0.1 V, 1 V, 10 V give $-0.462$ V, $-0.522$ V, $-0.582$ V. Each decade (factor of 10) adds $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T\ln10}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{59.9}$ mV. Exponential amplifier with the same values: 0.5 V gives $-0.427$ V, and every 18 mV more doubles the magnitude.

⁠Limiting cases: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ leaves the integrator standing; it holds $u_\mathrm{A}(0)$. At $U_\mathrm{E}<0$ the diode of the logarithmic amplifier blocks, the feedback is interrupted, and the output runs to its limit: the rule holds only as long as the return path conducts.

Multiplying. Because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ln x+\ln y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\ln(x\,y)}$, a chain multiplies in analog fashion: two logarithmic amplifiers, a summing amplifier, an exponential amplifier. At the output, up to a fixed factor, stands the product $U_x\,U_y$. Because $U_T$ and $I_\mathrm{S}$ depend on temperature, in practice the diodes are replaced by transistors with additional temperature compensation. Before digital computers, such circuits formed calculating machines, hence the name operational amplifier.

Exercise 7 ★★ Integrator

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ nF, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{A}(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. (a) How large is $R\,C$? Where is $u_\mathrm{A}$ after 10 ms at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.2}$ V? (b) When does the output hit its limit? (c) A square wave of $\pm0.5$ V at 100 Hz is applied. What is the amplitude of the triangle wave? What happens at 20 Hz?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R\,C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ ms. $u_\mathrm{A}$ rises at 200 V/s, to $+2$ V after 10 ms. (b) After $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{11\,\mathrm{V}/(200\,\mathrm{V/s})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{55}$ ms. (c) A half period of 5 ms changes $u_\mathrm{A}$ by 2.5 V, so the amplitude is 1.25 V. At 20 Hz the swing is 12.5 V. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{A}(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and a positive start, the triangle wave reaches $-11$ V and hits the limit.

Exercise 8 ★★ Logarithmic amplifier

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{S}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.9\cdot10^{-13}}$ A. (a) Calculate $U_\mathrm{A}$ for 1 V and 2 V. Predict the difference without using $I_\mathrm{S}$. (b) How far does $U_\mathrm{A}$ sweep when $U_\mathrm{E}$ runs from 10 mV to 10 V? (c) At 330 K instead of 300 K, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{28.6}$ mV. By how much does that alone shift $U_\mathrm{A}$ at a current of 100 µ A?

Solution

(a) 1 mA gives $-0.582$ V, 2 mA gives $-0.600$ V. The difference is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T\ln2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{18}$ mV, without $I_\mathrm{S}$. (b) Three decades: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3\cdot59.9\,\mathrm{mV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{180}$ mV. (c) $U_\mathrm{F}$ grows from 0.522 V to 0.574 V, that is, by 52 mV. In reality $I_\mathrm{S}$ also rises strongly, which is why temperature compensation is needed.
