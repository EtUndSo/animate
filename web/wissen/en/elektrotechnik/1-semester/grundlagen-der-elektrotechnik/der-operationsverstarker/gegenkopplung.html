What this stage is about:

Without feedback the op amp is a switch. This stage shows why a fed-back part of the output brings the difference of the inputs almost to zero, and why the gain then no longer depends on $A$.

The idea. Feedback means that part of the output quantity gets back to the input. If it counteracts the cause, it is negative feedback; if it reinforces the cause, it is positive feedback. With an op amp the path to the minus input gives negative feedback, because every voltage there acts with a negative sign.

In the non-inverting amplifier, $U_\mathrm{E}$ is at the plus input (Fig. 6). The divider of $R_2$ and $R_1$ reports one fifth of the output to the minus input. Now apply $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ V. At first $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ V. The target value $A\,U_\mathrm{D}$ would be 100 kV, and the output runs upward. At 4 V output, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.8}$ V, so 200 mV are still missing. At 4.9 V, 20 mV are missing, at 4.99 V only 2 mV, and the target has shrunk to 200 V. The output catches up with its target only when the two are equal: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A\,U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{A}}$. That happens at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\,\mathrm{V}/100000}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{50}$ µ V. A drop would also be corrected immediately: if the output falls, $\varphi_-$ sinks, $U_\mathrm{D}$ grows, and the output rises.

The small difference is thus not a basic law of the component but the result of the loop. It cannot be zero, because without a difference there would be no output.

Theorem 4.1 (Amplifier with negative feedback)

If the fraction $\beta$ of the output is fed to the minus input and $U_\mathrm{E}$ is at the plus input, then in the linear range

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{E}}{1+A\beta},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{A}}{U_\mathrm{E}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{A}{1+A\beta}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1\beta\cdot\frac{1}{1+\dfrac1{A\beta}}.}$$
(4.1)

For $A\beta\gg1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/\beta}$ with the relative deviation $-1/(A\beta)$. A change in $A$ acts weakened by the factor $1+A\beta$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\Delta G}{G}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{1+A\beta}\cdot\frac{\Delta A}{A}.}$$
(4.2)

With the divider, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_1/(R_1+R_2)}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+R_2/R_1}$.

⁠How to read it: $\beta$ is the fed-back fraction, between 0 and 1, here 0.2. $G$ is the gain of the whole circuit. $A\beta$ is the loop gain: a small change at $U_\mathrm{D}$ is amplified by $A$ and reported back weakened by $\beta$. Here $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20000}$; this is the reserve that keeps the deviation small. Validity: $U_\mathrm{A,min}<U_\mathrm{A}<U_\mathrm{A,max}$, input current small compared with the divider current, $A$ at the frequency under consideration (Stage 10).

Derivation: The loop in equilibrium and in motion

Equilibrium: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A\,U_\mathrm{D}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{E}-\beta\,U_\mathrm{A}}$. Substituting gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{E}-A\beta\,U_\mathrm{D}}$, and from this (4.1). Differentiating $G$ with respect to $A$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{d} G/\mathrm{d} A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/(1+A\beta)^2}$; dividing by $G$ yields (4.2).

Motion: inside the op amp, a current proportional to $U_\mathrm{D}$ charges a capacitance. The output therefore cannot jump but runs after its target value: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau\,\mathrm{d} U_\mathrm{A}/\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A\,U_\mathrm{D}-U_\mathrm{A}}$, here with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A/(2\pi f_\mathrm{T})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{15.9}$ ms. This is the equation of the RC circuit from Chapter 3. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{E}-\beta\,U_\mathrm{A}}$ it becomes

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d} U_\mathrm{A}}{\mathrm{d} t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A\,U_\mathrm{E}-(1+A\beta)\,U_\mathrm{A}.}$$

The factor in front of $U_\mathrm{A}$ is negative, so every deviation decays: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G\,U_\mathrm{E}\,(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_G})}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau_G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tau/(1+A\beta)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.80}$ µ s. The loop is 20001 times faster than the op amp alone.

Example

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20000}$, 1 V at the input becomes $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.99975}$ V; the deviation from the ideal 5 V is 0.005 % $=1/(A\beta)$. The specimens scatter by a factor of four:

$A$$U_\mathrm{D}$$U_\mathrm{A}$Deviation from 5 V
5000099.99 µ V4.99950 V$-0.0100$ %
10000050.00 µ V4.99975 V$-0.0050$ %
20000025.00 µ V4.99988 V$-0.0025$ %

A change in $A$ of 300 % shifts $G$ by 0.0075 %. You trade gain for accuracy: 100000 becomes 5, but in return two resistors determine the result.

⁠In short: The gain is the reciprocal of the fed-back fraction. Limiting cases: $A\to\infty$ gives $U_\mathrm{D}\to0$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/\beta}$. $\beta\to0$ gives $G\to A$; without feedback, the open loop remains.

Input and output. At the plus input only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{50}$ µ V lies across $r_\mathrm{e}$, so 50 pA flows, one two-millionth of the divider current of 100 µ A. The input resistance thus rises to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{e}(1+A\beta)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ G$\Omega$. At the output, the loop acts against any loading: if a load pulls $U_\mathrm{A}$ down a little, $U_\mathrm{D}$ grows, and the internal voltage rises until the loss is almost compensated. The output resistance falls to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{a}/(1+A\beta)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.75}$ m$\Omega$.

The voltage follower. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $R_1\to\infty$, the output is connected directly to the minus input, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A/(1+A)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.99999}$. This voltage follower does not amplify but isolates: input resistance 100 G$\Omega$, output resistance 0.75 m$\Omega$. A voltmeter of 1 M$\Omega$ on a source with 1 V and 100 k$\Omega$ internal resistance shows 0.909 V (Chapter 4). With a voltage follower in front it shows 0.99999 V.

QuestionWhy not simply use an amplifier with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ instead of buying 100000 and discarding almost all of it?

Because its 5 would depend on $B$, $U_\mathrm{Early}$ and the temperature: a 1 % change inside would be 1 % in the result. With the reserve $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20000}$ this becomes $5\cdot10^{-5}$ %.

QuestionJust now the output is too low, so it jumps up, and then it is too high. Shouldn't it swing back and forth forever?

It would if it jumped to $A\,U_\mathrm{D}$ in one step: the error would be multiplied by 20000 on every trip around the loop. But the output does not jump. The internal capacitance limits its speed; it runs continuously and brakes as $U_\mathrm{D}$ shrinks. So it arrives without overshoot (derivation above).

Exercise 4 ★★ Scatter and reserve

The amplifier is to have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11}$, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ k$\Omega$. (a) Choose $R_2$. How large are $\beta$ and $A\beta$? (b) How large is $U_\mathrm{D}$ at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5.5}$ V? (c) Calculate $G$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{50000}$ and $200000$. (d) What is the minimum $A$ that suffices for 0.1 % deviation? (e) What happens at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ V? How large is $U_\mathrm{D}$ then?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1+R_2/R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$ k$\Omega$. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/11}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0909}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9091}$. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{5.5\,\mathrm{V}/100000}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{55}$ µ V. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10.99758}$ and $10.99940$, a difference of 0.016 %. (d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/(1+1/(A\beta))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.999}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{999}$, so $A\ge10989$. (e) By calculation 22 V; the output is at 11 V. Then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\beta\cdot11\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ V; the rule does not hold.
