Stage 2: The differential amplifier

What this stage is about:

A disturbance on both lines should vanish, and the difference between them should be amplified. This takes a circuit whose output depends only on the difference of two voltages.

The idea. Two identical transistors T1 and T2 hang with their emitters on the common node E (Fig. 2). Below them sits T3. Its base is at the fixed voltage $U_\mathrm{bias}$, so it acts as a current source (Chapter 10) and draws the fixed current $I_0$ out of the node. The base currents are small, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C1}+I_\mathrm{C2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_0}$ holds. The sum is fixed; only the division can change.

If both bases rise by the same amount, E rises with them, because T3 holds the current fixed and with it $U_\mathrm{BE}$. Nothing changes. This common part is called the common-mode voltage, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{cm}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\varphi_++\varphi_-)/2}$. If only the base of T2 rises, T2 draws more current. T1 gets less by exactly this amount. More voltage drops across the right collector resistor, less across the left one, and the voltage between the collectors swings.

Theorem 2.1 (Differential amplifier)

T1 has its base at the input $\varphi_-$, T2 at the input $\varphi_+$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_+-\varphi_-}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{a1}-U_\mathrm{a2}}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_0}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-U_\mathrm{D}/U_T}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_0}{1+\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{D}/U_T}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{C}\,(I_\mathrm{C2}-I_\mathrm{C1}).}$$
(2.1)

For $|U_\mathrm{D}|\ll U_T$ this is a straight line:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A_\mathrm{D}\,U_\mathrm{D},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S\,R_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_0\,R_\mathrm{C}}{2\,U_T}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}}{U_T}.}$$
(2.2)

⁠How to read it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{C}/U_T}$ is the transconductance of one transistor at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_0/2}$. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_0R_\mathrm{C}/2}$ is the quiescent voltage across one collector resistor. $\varphi_+$ is called the non-inverting input because the output follows it in the same direction, $\varphi_-$ the inverting input. Validity: identical transistors, both in the active region, base currents small compared with $I_0$.

Derivation: Division and gain

Both emitters are at the same potential, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{BE2}-U_\mathrm{BE1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{D}}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{BE}/U_T}}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C2}/I_\mathrm{C1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{D}/U_T}}$. Together with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C1}+I_\mathrm{C2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_0}$ this gives (2.1). For small changes: the sum is fixed, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta I_\mathrm{C1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\Delta I_\mathrm{C2}}$. Both are $S$ times the change in their own $U_\mathrm{BE}$, so the changes are equal and opposite. Their difference is $U_\mathrm{D}$, so each junction carries $\pm U_\mathrm{D}/2$. Thus $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta I_\mathrm{C2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S\,U_\mathrm{D}/2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2R_\mathrm{C}\,\Delta I_\mathrm{C2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S\,R_\mathrm{C}\,U_\mathrm{D}}$.

Example

At rest, 1 mA flows in each branch, and the collectors are at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{12\,\mathrm{V}-3\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$ V. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{38.5}$ mS, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\,\mathrm{V}/26\,\mathrm{mV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{115}$. At $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ mV, $\pm19.2$ µ A moves between the branches, each collector moves by 57.7 mV, and together that makes $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{115}$ mV. A disturbance of 100 mV on both inputs changes nothing about this.

⁠Limiting cases: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ gives $I_0/2$ per branch. At $|U_\mathrm{D}|\gg U_T$ everything flows through one transistor, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}\to\pm R_\mathrm{C}I_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm6}$ V. The linear range is only a few $U_T$ wide: at 26 mV the straight line gives 3.0 V, the exact curve 2.77 V.

Input. An input signal $U_\mathrm{D}$ lies across two BE junctions in series, so the input resistance is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\,r_\mathrm{BE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2B/S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10.4}$ k$\Omega$. With a total current of 20 µ A instead of 2 mA it is 1.04 M$\Omega$, and each base draws only 50 nA. That is where $r_\mathrm{e}$ and $I_\mathrm{B}$ in the reference model come from.

From the differential amplifier to the op amp. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}/U_T}$ stays at a few hundred, because $U_{R_\mathrm{C}}$ cannot exceed the supply. A current mirror (Chapter 10) in place of the resistors brings both current changes to one side and acts there like the parallel connection of two $r_\mathrm{CE}$. This gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S\,r_\mathrm{CE}/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{Early}/(2U_T)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1400}$. Behind it follow a voltage amplifier stage and an emitter follower for the low output resistance. Together that makes $10^5$ to $10^6$. This value depends on $B$, $U_\mathrm{Early}$ and the temperature: large, but imprecise.

QuestionWhy is there only half the difference across each transistor if I raise only one base?

Because node E goes along by half. T2 wants more current, but the sum is fixed, so E rises until T1 gives up exactly that much. The fixed sum demands equal and opposite changes of $U_\mathrm{BE}$. Putting the whole difference on one junction gives twice the true $A_\mathrm{D}$.

Exercise 2 ★★ Differential stage

A differential stage made of transistors T operates with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ k$\Omega$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12}$ V. (a) Determine $S$, $A_\mathrm{D}$ and the quiescent potential of the collectors. (b) How large is $U_\mathrm{A}$ at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ mV? How much current moves? (c) Both inputs rise by 1 V. What happens to E, the currents and $U_\mathrm{A}$? (d) Predict: Does $A_\mathrm{D}$ change if $I_0$ is doubled and $R_\mathrm{C}$ halved?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5\,\mathrm{mA}/26\,\mathrm{mV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{19.2}$ mS, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{96.2}$, collectors at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{12\,\mathrm{V}-2.5\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9.5}$ V. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{192}$ mV. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S\,U_\mathrm{D}/2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{19.2}$ µ A moves. (c) E rises by 1 V, $U_\mathrm{BE}$ and the currents stay the same, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. (d) No: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}/U_T}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.5}$ V stays the same.

Summary

Terms
  • common-mode voltage – Nothing changes. This common part is called the common-mode voltage, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{cm}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\varphi_++\varphi_-)/2}$.
  • non-inverting input, inverting input – $\varphi_+$ is called the non-inverting input because the output follows it in the same direction, $\varphi_-$ the inverting input.
