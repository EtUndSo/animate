Stage 1: From four-pole to two-port

What this stage is about:

A network with four terminals has three independent currents and three independent voltages. This stage shows which condition turns these into two currents and two voltages.

The idea. A four-pole is a network with four terminals. The node rule (Chapter 2) holds for the whole network as for one large node: One terminal current is fixed by the other three. The mesh rule likewise ties together the voltages between the terminals. Now if a two-terminal element, say a source, is connected to terminals 1 and 1$'$, a further statement is added: A two-terminal element has only two connections. Every charge it gives off at one it takes back at the other. The current that enters the network at terminal 1 must return at terminal 1$'$. The terminal pair behaves like a single access with one current and one voltage (Fig. 1 left).

Definition 1.1 (Four-pole, port, two-port)

At a four-pole with the terminals 1, 1$'$, 2, 2$'$, let all terminal currents be counted toward the network. It always holds that

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1+I_{1'}+I_2+I_{2'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{11'}+U_{1'2'}-U_{22'}+U_{21}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.}$$
(1.1)

A terminal pair is a port if the port condition holds. A four-pole with two ports is a two-port:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{1'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_1,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{2'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_2.}$$
(1.2)

Its port quantities are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_{11'}}$, $I_1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_{22'}}$ and $I_2$.

⁠How to read it: (1.1) leaves three currents and three voltages free. The first equation is the node rule, the second the mesh rule around the loop $1\to1'\to2'\to2\to1$. (1.2) removes one current each. The voltage $U_{1'2'}$ also drops out of the description: No current flows between the ports on the outside, so no power is transferred through it. Whether a four-pole becomes a two-port is decided by what is connected to it: If a two-terminal element is connected to each terminal pair, (1.2) holds automatically. A branch running outside from port to port breaks it (Fig. 1 right). The historical name four-pole lives on in four-pole theory.

A two-port is a conceptually closed unit, accessible only through its ports. Its properties are fixed by the relations between the port quantities, and these can be calculated or measured. Usually a source is connected to port 1, the input, and a load to port 2, the output. Energy then flows in the forward direction $1\to2$; the opposite direction is called the reverse direction. The two-port is a basic structure: Controlled sources are two-ports, transformers, amplifiers and transistors have three or four connections, large two-ports can be built from elementary two-ports, and two-ports joined together form a two-port again. The simplest examples are the voltage divider and the current divider (Chapter 2).

Structure and classification. If a two-port has a continuous line from terminal 1$'$ to 2$'$, the ground line, it is called unbalanced (Fig. 2 left). It is really a three-pole, an improper four-pole. Network models of transistors and amplifiers are of this kind. A two-port that, like the bridge circuit, has no such line and is built as a mirror image about a center line is called balanced. By their behavior, two-ports are divided into linear time-invariant, time-varying and nonlinear ones. This chapter deals with the linear time-invariant ones.

Example

In the operation of the T two-port (Stage 4), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{250}$ mA flows in and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{1'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-250}$ mA; the load draws 50 mA, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-50}$ mA and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{2'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+50}$ mA. The sum in (1.1) is zero.

⁠Limiting case: If port 2 is open, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_{2'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and to the outside the two-port acts like a two-terminal element at port 1.

QuestionThe ground line connects 1$'$ and 2$'$. Doesn't current flow from port to port there after all?

Yes, on the inside. In the ground line of the T two-port, 50 mA runs from 2$'$ to the foot of $R_\mathrm{b}$. But the port condition only speaks of what happens at the terminals. Inside, any current may flow, as long as each terminal current comes back at its partner terminal.

Exercise 1 ★ Counting at the terminals

At a four-pole you measure $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{30}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{1'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-30}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-10}$ mA, and also $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{11'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{1'2'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{22'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ V.

  1. What is $I_{2'}$? Are both terminal pairs ports?

  2. What is $U_{21}$?

  3. How many currents would you have to measure at least to know all the terminal currents, for the four-pole and for the two-port?

Solution

(a) From (1.1): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{2'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-(30-30-10)\,\mathrm{mA}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+10}$ mA. We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{1'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{2'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_2}$, so both pairs are ports. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{21}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-U_{11'}-U_{1'2'}+U_{22'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-6\,\mathrm{V}-0\,\mathrm{V}+2\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4}$ V. (c) For the four-pole three; the fourth follows from the node rule. For the two-port two; the others follow from the port condition.
