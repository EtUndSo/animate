What this stage is about:

The second choice: The voltages are the causes. This stage carries everything over and shows why the short circuit now takes over the role of the open circuit.

The idea. If the voltages are the causes, one voltage must vanish so that the other part remains alone. A voltage is zero when a wire bridges the port. The open circuit sets a current to zero, the short circuit a voltage. Which test is the right one follows from the choice of causes alone.

Definition 5.1 (Admittance form, Y form)

The admittance form, also conductance form, takes the voltages as causes:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11}U_1+Y_{12}U_2,\quad I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{21}U_1+Y_{22}U_2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{in short}\quad\mathbf I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf Y\cdot\mathbf U,\quad\mathbf Y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}Y_{11}&Y_{12}\\Y_{21}&Y_{22}\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(5.1)

The Y parameters follow from two short-circuit tests:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{11}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{I_1}{U_1}\right|_{U_2=0},\quad Y_{21}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{I_2}{U_1}\right|_{U_2=0},\quad Y_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{I_1}{U_2}\right|_{U_1=0},\quad Y_{22}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{I_2}{U_2}\right|_{U_1=0}.}$$
(5.2)

For a load with the conductance $Y_\mathrm{L}$ at port 2, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-Y_\mathrm{L}U_2}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{Y_{21}}{Y_{22}+Y_\mathrm{L}}\,U_1,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_1}{U_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11}-\frac{Y_{12}Y_{21}}{Y_{22}+Y_\mathrm{L}},}$$
(5.3)

with the limiting cases

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{1,\mathrm{SC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{1,\mathrm{OC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11}-\frac{Y_{12}Y_{21}}{Y_{22}}.}$$
(5.4)

⁠How to read it: $Y$ stands for admittance, the reciprocal of impedance; here these are real conductances in siemens, typically micro- to millisiemens, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{ii}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G_{ii}}$. $Y_{11}$ is the short-circuit input conductance, $Y_{22}$ the short-circuit output conductance. $Y_{21}$ is the short-circuit transfer conductance in the forward direction; for the transistor it is called transconductance, in general transadmittance: the influence of the input voltage on the output current. $Y_{12}$ is the same conductance in the reverse direction, the influence of the output voltage on the input current. The equivalent circuit (Fig. 6 left) is the counterpart of the Z form: A sum of currents is a parallel connection of a conductance and a voltage-controlled current source (VCCS). (5.3) arises as in Stage 4, only with the node rule at the right and left nodes instead of the mesh rule.

Example

On the T two-port, 9 V is applied to port 1, and port 2 is short-circuited. The wire puts $R_\mathrm{c}$ in parallel with $R_\mathrm{b}$, the total resistance is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{15\,\Omega+10\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{25\,\Omega}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{360}$ mA. This current divides in half, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-180}$ mA, negative because it flows out of port 2. With 9 V at port 2 and a short circuit at port 1, the total resistance is $20\,\Omega+15\,\Omega\parallel20\,\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{315}$ mA and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-180}$ mA. This gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf Y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}40&-20\\-20&35\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{mS}.}$$

Check with the operation from Stage 4: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40\,\mathrm{mS}\cdot7.75\,\mathrm{V}-20\,\mathrm{mS}\cdot3\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{250}$ mA. The load $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16.67}$ mS gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40\,\mathrm{mS}-400\,\mathrm{mS^2}/51.67\,\mathrm{mS}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{32.26}$ mS, which is $1/(31\,\Omega)$.

⁠Limiting cases: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{1,\mathrm{SC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40}$ mS is $1/(25\,\Omega)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{1,\mathrm{OC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{28.57}$ mS is $1/(35\,\Omega)$, as in Stage 4.

Characteristic curves. The Y equations can also be drawn. The input characteristic is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_1(U_1)}$ at fixed $U_2$, the transfer characteristic $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_2(U_1)}$ at fixed $U_2$, the output characteristic $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_3(U_2)}$ at fixed $U_1$. For a linear two-port they are straight lines (Fig. 7): Their slope is a Y parameter, and the fixed value shifts them in parallel. Each value of the fixed parameter gives one line; together they form a family of curves.

QuestionWhy is $Y_{11}$ not $1/Z_{11}$? Both describe the input, after all.

Both describe the input, but with different terminations: $Z_{11}$ applies with the output open, $Y_{11}$ with it short-circuited. In the T two-port, these are 35 $\Omega$ and 25 $\Omega$. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/Y_{11}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{11}}$ holds only if $Z_{12}$ or $Z_{21}$ is zero.

Exercise 5 ★★ A $\pi$ network

A $\pi$ network has the shunt conductances $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ mS at the input and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ mS at the output and the series conductance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40}$ mS.

  1. Determine $\mathbf Y$ from short-circuit tests. Which currents flow with 2 V at port 1?

  2. Calculate $Y_{1,\mathrm{OC}}$ and check the result with a direct resistance calculation.

  3. Predict: What is $I_1$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ V, and in which direction does it flow?

Solution

(a) Port 2 shorted: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{11}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G_1+G_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{50}$ mS, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{21}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-G_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-40}$ mS. Port 1 shorted: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{22}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G_2+G_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{60}$ mS, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-40}$ mS. At 2 V: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-80}$ mA. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{1,\mathrm{OC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{50\,\mathrm{mS}-1600\,\mathrm{mS^2}/60\,\mathrm{mS}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{23.33}$ mS, which is 42.86 $\Omega$. Directly: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{100\,\Omega\parallel(25\,\Omega+50\,\Omega)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{42.86\,\Omega}$. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{12}U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-200}$ mA. Port 1 is short-circuited; the current runs from port 2 through $G_2$ and leaves the two-port at terminal 1.
