Chapter 1: Charge, Field and Potential

Overview: Charge, Field and Potential

What this is about
The reference model for this chapter

  • ⁠Pair of plates: two flat metal plates A and B in vacuum (a space empty of air), separation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ mm, small compared with the plate width. A 9 V battery is connected with its plus terminal to A and its minus terminal to B. The axis $x$ runs from A ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$) to B. Between the plates there is the uniform field $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ kV/m (Stages 5 and 8).

  • ⁠Two spheres: small spheres with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+10}$ nC and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-20}$ nC at the distance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ cm. Sphere A has the mass 1 g.

  • ⁠Circuit: the same battery across two resistors in series, 6 V across the first, 3 V across the second. A resistor is a component in which passing charge gives up energy as heat (Chapter 2).

  • ⁠Constants: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.602\cdot10^{-19}}$ C, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_\mathrm{e}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9.109\cdot10^{-31}}$ kg, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8.99\cdot10^{9}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}$; the stages explain them.

Stage 1: Quantities, force and work

Definition 1.1 (Physical quantity)

A physical quantity is a measurable property, the product of numerical value and unit:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{quantity}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{numerical value}\cdot\text{unit}.}$$
(1.1)
Definition 1.2 (Force and work)

The force on a body with mass $m$ and acceleration $a$, and the force between two masses $m$, $M$ at the distance $r$, are

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m\,a,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\gamma\,\frac{m\,M}{r^2}.}$$
(1.2)

The work done by a force along a path is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\vec F\cdot\Delta\vec s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{F\,\Delta s\cos\alpha,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{P_1}^{P_2}\vec F\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec s.}$$
(1.3)
Theorem 1.3 (Constant acceleration and kinetic energy)

With the initial values $s_0$ and $v_0$ at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, for constant $a$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v_0+a\,t,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_0+v_0\,t+\tfrac12a\,t^2.}$$
(1.4)

A body with the velocity $v$ carries the kinetic energy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm{kin}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12m\,v^2}$.

Stage 2: Charge

Definition 2.1 (Charge)

A body with $N_\mathrm{p}$ protons and $N_\mathrm{e}$ electrons carries the charge

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(N_\mathrm{p}-N_\mathrm{e})\,e,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.602\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[Q]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{A\,s}.}$$
(2.1)

The elementary charge $e$ is the smallest charge occurring freely. An electron carries $-e$, a proton $+e$. In a closed system, the sum of all charges stays constant (conservation of charge).

Stage 3: The force between charges

Theorem 3.1 (Coulomb's law)

Two point-like charges at rest at the distance $r$ exert forces of magnitude

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{|Q_\mathrm{A}\,Q_\mathrm{B}|}{r^2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8.99\cdot10^{9}\,\frac{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}{\mathrm{C^2}}}$$
(3.1)

on each other. As a vector, the force on A, with $\vec r$ pointing from B to A, is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec F_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{Q_\mathrm{A}\,Q_\mathrm{B}}{r^2}\cdot\frac{\vec r}{r}.}$$
(3.2)

Stage 4: The field strength

Definition 4.1 (Electric field strength)

If a small test charge $Q$ is brought to a location and the force $\vec F$ on it is measured, the electric field strength there is

$$\vec E:=\lim_{Q\to0}\frac{\vec F}{Q},$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{hence}\quad\vec F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\cdot\vec E.}$$
(4.1)

The direction of $\vec E$ is the direction of the force on a positive test charge. The electric field assigns such a vector to every location.

Theorem 4.2 (Field of point charges)

A point charge $Q_0$ produces at the distance $r$ the field

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{Q_0}{r^2}\cdot\frac{\vec r}{r},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{|Q_0|}{r^2},}$$
(4.2)

where $\vec r$ points from the charge to the location. Several charges together produce the sum of their fields: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_i\vec E_i}$.

Stage 5: Field lines and conductors

Theorem 5.1 (Field lines of charges at rest)

A field line is a curve whose tangent at each point has the direction of $\vec E$; the density of the lines shows the strength of the field. The following hold:

  1. Field lines begin on positive charges (sources) and end on negative ones (sinks), never in empty space. For a single charge, the other end runs off to infinity.

  2. Field lines do not cross.

  3. They are perpendicular to the surface of a conductor.

  4. Inside a conductor, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. A charge-free cavity enclosed by the conductor is field-free (shielding).

Stage 6: Work in the field

Theorem 6.1 (Work in the electric field)

If a charge $Q$ goes from $P_1$ to $P_2$, the field does on it the work

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{P_1}^{P_2}\vec F\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\int_{P_1}^{P_2}\vec E\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec s,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{in a uniform field}\quad W_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\,E\,s\cos\alpha.}$$
(6.1)

In the field of charges at rest, $W_{12}$ depends only on $P_1$ and $P_2$, not on the path. Equivalently, the integral over any closed path is zero; the field is curl-free:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\oint_L\vec E\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.}$$
(6.2)

Stage 7: The potential

Definition 7.1 (Potential)

In an electrostatic field with the reference point $P_0$, the potential of a point $P$ is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi(P)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{P}^{P_0}\vec E\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\int_{P_0}^{P}\vec E\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec s,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[\varphi]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm J}{\mathrm C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm V.}$$
(7.1)

A charge $Q$ there has the potential energy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm{pot}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\,\varphi(P)}$.

Theorem 7.2 (Work as a potential difference, two profiles)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\,\bigl(\varphi(P_1)-\varphi(P_2)\bigr).}$$
(7.2)

In the uniform field in the $x$-direction and in the field of a point charge $Q_0$ (reference point at infinity),

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi(0)-E\,x,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi(r)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{Q_0}{r}.}$$
(7.3)

Stage 8: Voltage and ground

Definition 8.1 (Electric voltage and ground)

The voltage between $P_1$ and $P_2$ is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi(P_1)-\varphi(P_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{P_1}^{P_2}\vec E\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{W_{12}}{Q},}$$
(8.1)

with the unit $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[U]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{[W]\cdot[Q]^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{N\,m\,A^{-1}\,s^{-1}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,A^{-1}\,s^{-3}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm V}$. The point of a circuit that is assigned $\varphi:=0$ V is called ground or reference potential, written GND. If it is connected to the soil, it is called earth ground. Starting from there,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_2+U_{12}.}$$
(8.2)
Theorem 8.2 (Uniform field, freedom of the reference point)

Between two parallel plates at the distance $d$ with the voltage $U$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U/d}$. If the reference point is shifted, all potentials change by the same constant $c$, and all voltages stay the same:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U}{d},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi'(P)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi(P)+c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\varphi'_1-\varphi'_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_{12}.}$$
(8.3)

Stage 9: What voltage does to a charge

Theorem 9.1 (Charge in the field)

In an electrostatic field, the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy stays constant:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12m\,v^2+Q\,\varphi}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{constant}.}$$
(9.1)

A charge that passes through the voltage $U$ from rest reaches, in a uniform field of length $d$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{\frac{2\,|Q\,U|}{m}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{|Q|\,E}{m},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2\,d}{v}.}$$
(9.2)

Stage 10: Limit of the picture: magnetism and induction

Theorem 10.1 (Law of induction)

If the magnetic flux $\Phi$ through a coil with $N$ turns changes, the following voltage arises between its ends:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{ind}(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-N\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d} t}.}$$
(10.1)

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. Charge is a property of electrons and protons, the charged building blocks of atoms. Like charges repel, opposite charges attract, and the sum of all charges is conserved.

  2. Every charge produces a field around itself. The field $\vec E$ determines the force per charge at every location: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\,\vec E}$.

  3. In a static field, the work done on a charge depends only on start and end, not on the path. That is why every point has a potential $\varphi$, and the voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_1-\varphi_2}$ is the energy per charge that the field gives up on the way from point 1 to point 2.

Chapter 2: Current, Resistance and Kirchhoff's Laws

Overview: Current, Resistance and Kirchhoff's Laws

What this is about
The reference model for this chapter

  • ⁠Chain: the 9 V battery from Chapter 1 across two resistors in series, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$. A current of 3 mA flows, 6 V are across $R_1$ and 3 V across $R_2$.

  • ⁠Wire: copper, length $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ m, cross section $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}}$, resistivity (material constant, Stage 3) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\rho}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.7\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm{\Omega\,m}}$, density of free electrons $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8.5\cdot10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}}$ (one per atom). It carries the 3 mA of the chain.

  • ⁠Network: a 9 V source and five resistors between four junctions, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ (all in $\mathrm{k\Omega}$), Fig. 7.

  • ⁠Constants: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.602\cdot10^{-19}}$ C, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_\mathrm e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9.109\cdot10^{-31}}$ kg.

Stage 1: Current is moving charge

Definition 1.1 (Current and current density)

The charge density $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Delta Q/\Delta V}$ is the charge per volume. The electric current (current for short) is the charge that passes through an area per time. The current density is the current per area:

$$\begin{aligned} i&=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}=\frac{\mathrm{d} Q}{\mathrm{d} t},\qquad I=q\,v\,A,\qquad [I]=\mathrm{C/s}=\mathrm A,\\ j&=\frac IA=q\,v,\qquad\vec j=q\,\vec v. \end{aligned}$$
(1.1)(1.2)

Stage 2: Slow electrons, immediate current

Theorem 2.1 (Drift velocity)

If each flight between two collisions takes the time $T$, then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\vec v_\mathrm{max}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{e\,|\vec E|}{m_\mathrm e}\,T,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec v_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mu\,\vec E,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mu}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{e\,T}{2\,m_\mathrm e}.}$$
(2.1)

With Stage 1, the current gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{j/(n\,e)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I/(n\,e\,A)}$.

Stage 3: Ohm's law from field and collisions

Theorem 3.1 (Ohm's law)

For a conductor with the electron density $n$,

$$\begin{aligned} \vec j&=\sigma\,\vec E,\qquad\sigma=n\,e\,\mu,\qquad\rho=\frac1\sigma,\\ U&=R\,I,\qquad R=\frac{L}{\sigma\,A}=\rho\,\frac LA,\qquad G=\frac1R=\frac IU. \end{aligned}$$
(3.1)(3.2)

Stage 4: Real resistors: values and temperature

Definition 4.1 (E series)

An E series E$N$ contains $N$ values per decade; according to DIN IEC 60063 there are E3, E6, E12, E24, E48, E96 and E192:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\sqrt[N]{10}\,\bigr)^{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt[N]{10^{i}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,1,\dots,N-1.}$$
(4.1)
Theorem 4.2 (Temperature dependence)

Near a reference temperature $\vartheta_0$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R(\vartheta)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R(\vartheta_0)\,\bigl(1+\alpha_{\vartheta_0}\,(\vartheta-\vartheta_0)\bigr).}$$
(4.2)

Stage 5: Two-terminal elements and reference arrows

Definition 5.1 (Two-terminal element, black box, network)

A two-terminal element is a component that can be fully described by the current and voltage between its two terminals. In the steady state (time-independent), that is its characteristic curve $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(U)}$. A black box is a system whose outward behavior is known but whose construction is unknown or unimportant. A network is an interconnection of two-terminal elements. If the characteristic curve is a straight line through the origin,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac IU}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{const}\quad\text{or}\quad R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac UI}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{const}.}$$
(5.1)
Definition 5.2 (Sign conventions)

The voltage arrow points from 1 to 2 and means $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_1-\varphi_2}$ (Chapter 1). The current arrow counts positive charge in the direction of the arrow. In the passive sign convention (PSC, load convention), the voltage and current arrows at a two-terminal element point in the same direction; in the active sign convention (ASC, generator convention), they point in opposite directions. For a resistor,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I\quad\text{(PSC)},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R\,I\quad\text{(ASC)}.}$$
(5.2)

Stage 6: Nodes, branches, meshes

Definition 6.1 (Basic topological terms)

Five terms describe the structure of a network:

  • A node is a point at which the current branches. $k$ is their number.

  • A branch connects two nodes via two-terminal elements, and a current flows in it. $z$ is their number.

  • The graph is the representation made of nodes and branches. It is connected: from every node, a path leads to every other. Each branch gets the direction of its current arrow.

  • A mesh is a closed path in which each node is connected to exactly two neighboring branches. No branch and no node is traversed more than once.

  • A tree is a connected subgraph that contains all nodes and forms no mesh. The remaining branches are called link branches.

Theorem 6.2 (Number of independent meshes)

Meshes are called linearly independent if they can be ordered so that each contains at least one branch missing from the previous ones. A graph with $k$ nodes and $z$ branches has exactly

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z-k+1}$$
(6.1)

independent meshes. They form a complete system: no further mesh adds anything new. Every tree defines such a system: each link branch closes exactly one mesh via the tree branches.

Stage 7: The node rule is charge conservation

Theorem 7.1 (Node rule, Kirchhoff's first law)

The sum of all currents flowing into a node is zero at every instant. Equivalently, the sum of the incoming currents equals the sum of the outgoing currents:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}I_\nu}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\quad\text{(all arrows toward the node)},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum I_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum I_\mathrm{out}.}$$
(7.1)

For a network with $k$ nodes, $k-1$ of these equations are independent.

Stage 8: The mesh rule is energy conservation

Theorem 8.1 (Mesh rule, Kirchhoff's second law)

The sum of all branch voltages along a mesh is zero in either direction of traversal and at every instant:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}U_\nu}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,}$$
$$U_\nu\text{ counted positive along the traversal, negative against it.}$$
(8.1)

For a network, $z-k+1$ of these equations are independent; all others are then satisfied.

Stage 9: Series connection and voltage divider

Theorem 9.1 (Series connection and voltage divider)

For $N$ resistors in a series connection,

$$\begin{aligned} I&=I_1=I_2=\dots=I_N,\qquad U=U_1+U_2+\dots+U_N,\qquad U_i=R_i\,I,\\ R_\mathrm{tot}&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_i,\qquad\frac{U_\nu}{U}=\frac{R_\nu}{\sum_{i=1}^NR_i}, \end{aligned}$$
(9.1)(9.2)

so for two resistors the voltage divider rule

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{U_2}{U}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{U_1}{U_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_1}{R_2}.}$$
(9.3)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(Z+\frac12\Bigr)\frac{U_\mathrm{ref}}{8}.}$$
(9.4)

Stage 10: Parallel connection, current divider and loading

Theorem 10.1 (Parallel connection and current divider)

For $N$ resistors in a parallel connection,

$$\begin{aligned} U&=U_1=\dots=U_N,\qquad I=I_1+\dots+I_N,\qquad I_i=G_i\,U=\frac{U}{R_i},\\ G_\mathrm{tot}&=\frac{1}{R_\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_i=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{R_i},\qquad R_\mathrm{tot}=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^N\frac1{R_i}\Bigr)^{-1}. \end{aligned}$$
(10.1)(10.2)

For two resistors one writes $R_1\parallel R_2$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1\parallel R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_1\,R_2}{R_1+R_2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{tot}\,\frac{G_1}{G_1+G_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{tot}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2},\quad I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{tot}\,\frac{G_2}{G_1+G_2}.}$$
(10.3)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{U_2}{U}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_2\parallel R_\mathrm L}{R_1+R_2\parallel R_\mathrm L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_2\,R_\mathrm L}{R_1\,(R_2+R_\mathrm L)+R_2\,R_\mathrm L},\quad I_\mathrm L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_2}{R_\mathrm L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U\,R_2}{R_1\,(R_2+R_\mathrm L)+R_2\,R_\mathrm L}.}$$
(10.4)

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. The field in the conductor accelerates the free electrons, and collisions take the gained velocity away again. What remains is a small drift velocity (average velocity along the conductor) that is proportional to the field. That is why the current density, the current per cross-sectional area, is proportional to the field, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I}$ follows for the component.

  2. The node rule is charge conservation, the mesh rule is energy conservation.

  3. Together, the three laws give every network exactly as many equations as it has unknowns. In series the voltage divides (voltage divider: two resistors whose ratio sets a partial voltage), in parallel the current divides.

Stage 1: Why current flows into an insulator

Definition 1.1 (Capacitor)

A capacitor consists of two electrodes separated by a dielectric. Its charge $Q$ is the charge of the positive electrode; the other carries $-Q$. Current through the capacitor means: a current flows in at one terminal, and at the same moment the same current flows out at the other.

Stage 2: Capacitance

Definition 2.1 (Capacitance)

The capacitance of a capacitor is the ratio of charge to voltage:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{Q}{U},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{C\,U,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[C]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{A\,s}}{\mathrm V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm F\ \text{(farad)}.}$$
(2.1)

Stage 3: Current and voltage at the capacitor

Theorem 3.1 (Current and voltage at the capacitor)

With the current and voltage reference arrows in the same direction (Chapter 2), the following holds for time-dependent quantities:

(3.1)(3.2)

Theorem 4.1 (Parallel-plate capacitor)

For two flat plates of area $A$ at separation $d$, with a dielectric filling the space,

(4.1)

Stage 5: Types of construction and self-discharge

Stage 6: The stored energy

Theorem 6.1 (Energy in the capacitor)

A capacitor with the charge $Q$ and the voltage $U$ stores

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_0^Q\frac qC\,\mathrm{d} q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{Q^2}{2C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,Q\,U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,C\,U^2.}$$
(6.1)

It resides in the field between the plates. For the parallel-plate capacitor, with the volume $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A\,d}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\varepsilon\,E^2\cdot A\,d,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac WV}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\varepsilon\,E^2.}$$
(6.2)

Here $w$ is the energy density, the energy per volume.

Stage 7: Parallel and series connection

Theorem 7.1 (Total capacitance)

For $n$ capacitors connected in parallel (left) and $n$ initially uncharged capacitors connected in series (right),

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_\mathrm{G}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^nC_i,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{1}{C_\mathrm{G}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{C_i}.}$$
(7.1)

For two capacitors in series, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_\mathrm{G}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}}$, and the voltage divides according to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{G}\,\dfrac{C_2}{C_1+C_2}}$.

Stage 8: Charge conservation when switching

Theorem 8.1 (Charge conservation on an island)

If every path from a node to ground leads through a capacitance, the sum of the charges on the connected plates stays constant:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_\mathrm{tot}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_iC_iU_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{const}.}$$
(8.1)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\frac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}\,(U_1-U_2)^2.}$$
(8.3)

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,C\,\frac{\mathrm{d} u_C}{\mathrm{d} t}+u_C.}$$
(9.1)

(9.2)
Theorem 9.1 (Charging)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_C(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $t\ge0$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_C(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_0\bigl(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\bigr),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_0}{R}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_R(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,C.}$$
(9.3)

Stage 10: Discharging and time constant

Theorem 10.1 (Discharging)

Without a battery, Kirchhoff's voltage law reads $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_R+u_C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, that is, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{RC\,\mathrm{d} u_C/\mathrm{d} t+u_C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_C(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_0}$ and $t\ge0$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_C(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{U_0}{R}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_R(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-U_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}.}$$
(10.1)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_C(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\infty+\bigl(u_C(0)-u_\infty\bigr)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}.}$$
(10.2)

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. A capacitor does not let charge through, it displaces it: electrons leave one plate and arrive on the other, and the same current flows at both terminals at the same moment.

  2. The separated charge $Q$ produces the field between the plates. This field determines the voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q/C}$, and it holds the field energy $\frac12CU^2$, stored in the space between the plates.

  3. Charge needs current and time, so the voltage does not jump. At the first moment an empty capacitor acts like a short circuit (current at 0 V), a full one like an open circuit (voltage without current). In between runs an exponential curve with the time constant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{RC}$, the time that sets the pace of charging.

Chapter 4: Measuring Current and Voltage

Overview: Measuring Current and Voltage

What this is about
The reference model for this chapter

  • ⁠Bridge: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.8}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ k$\Omega$ at 9 V; $R_4$ is wanted. For small changes, four resistors of 350 $\Omega$ each at 9 V.

Stage 1: The meter is a component

Definition 1.1 (Measuring range and internal resistance)

The measuring range extends up to the full-scale value, the largest value that can be displayed (for a pointer, full-scale deflection). The internal resistance $R_\mathrm i$ is the resistance between the terminals.

  • An ammeter is connected in series. Ideally $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, in practice about $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm i}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\Omega}$.

  • A voltmeter is connected in parallel. Ideally $R_\mathrm i\to\infty$, in practice about $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm i}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\mathrm{M\Omega}}$.

Stage 2: Extending the measuring range

Theorem 2.1 (Shunt and series resistor)

Let an instrument have the internal resistance $R_\mathrm{old}$ and the full-scale value $I_\mathrm{old}$ or $U_\mathrm{old}$, respectively. For the new full-scale value $I_\mathrm{new}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{new}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\alpha\,U_\mathrm{old}}$, it needs

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{Shunt}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{old}\,\frac{I_\mathrm{old}}{I_\mathrm{new}-I_\mathrm{old}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{old}\,(\alpha-1).}$$
(2.1)

The new internal resistance is $R_\mathrm{old}\parallel R_\mathrm{Shunt}$ or $R_\mathrm{old}+R_\mathrm V$, respectively.

Stage 3: The error in measuring current

Theorem 3.1 (Error of the ammeter)

Without a meter, $I_R$ flows; with a meter, $I_R'$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac UR,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_R'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U}{R+R_\mathrm i}<I_R,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{I_R}{I_R'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R+R_\mathrm i}{R}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+\frac{R_\mathrm i}{R}.}$$
(3.1)

The relative error is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(I_R'-I_R)/I_R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm i/(R+R_\mathrm i)}$. For $R_\mathrm i\ll R$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm i/R}$.

Stage 4: The error in measuring voltage

Theorem 4.1 (Error of the voltmeter)

On a current source $I_\mathrm q$ with load $R_L$, without a meter $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm qR_L}$, and with a meter

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_L'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm q\,(R_L\parallel R_\mathrm i)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_L\cdot\frac{1}{1+R_L/R_\mathrm i}.}$$
(4.1)

The relative error is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_L/(R_L+R_\mathrm i)}$, so for $R_L\ll R_\mathrm i$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_L/R_\mathrm i}$. On the voltage divider the same holds with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_1\parallel R_2}$.

Stage 5: Current and voltage at once

Definition 5.1 (Correct-current and correct-voltage measurement)

$U$ and $I$ are the readings; $U_R$ and $I_R$ are the values at the resistor.

Correct current: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_R}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_R+U_\mathrm{iA}\neq U_R}$. It follows that

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac UI}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R+R_\mathrm{iA},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+\frac{R_\mathrm{iA}}{R}.}$$
(5.1)

Correct voltage: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_R}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_R+I_\mathrm{iV}\neq I_R}$. It follows that

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac UI}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\parallel R_\mathrm{iV},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{R}{R+R_\mathrm{iV}}.}$$
(5.2)

Stage 6: The Wheatstone bridge

Theorem 6.1 (Balance condition)

If no current flows through the instrument,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_3\,\frac{R_2}{R_1}.}$$
(6.1)

Stage 7: The unbalanced bridge

Theorem 7.1 (Bridge voltage)

Between the midpoints there is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_4-U_2}$ with the voltages $U_2$ across $R_2$ and $U_4$ across $R_4$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q\left(\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}-\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\right)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q\,\frac{R_1R_4-R_2R_3}{(R_1+R_2)(R_3+R_4)}.}$$
(7.1)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R+\Delta R}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm q}{4}\cdot\frac{\Delta R/R}{1+\Delta R/(2R)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm q}{4}\cdot\frac{\Delta R}{R}}$$
$$\text{for }\Delta R\ll R.$$
(7.2)

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. An ammeter (current meter) sits in series in the circuit and adds its internal resistance to it. A voltmeter (voltage meter) sits in parallel and opens a second current path. Both show the value of the changed circuit.

  2. The relative error is about $R_\mathrm i/R$ for the ammeter and about $R/R_\mathrm i$ for the voltmeter. If both measure at once, only one reading is right: correct-current measurement (the current is right) or correct-voltage measurement (the voltage is right).

  3. The Wheatstone bridge (two voltage dividers on one source with an instrument between them) compares resistances. At balance (adjusted to a reading of zero), no current flows through the instrument. That is why its internal resistance, its scale and the source voltage drop out of the result $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_3R_2/R_1}$.

Stage 2: Using symmetry

Theorem 2.1 (Two symmetry rules)

For a network of resistors with the terminals A and B:

    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,R_\mathrm{A,axis}.}$$

Stage 3: Star and delta

Terminals 1, 2, 3, star point 0. From delta to star:

Stage 5: Real sources and the operating point

Definition 5.1 (Real sources)

An equivalent circuit is a circuit of ideal elements that behaves like the real component at the terminals. The real voltage source is an ideal voltage source in series with $R_\mathrm{q}$, the real current source an ideal current source in parallel with $G_\mathrm{q}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}-R_\mathrm{q}\,I_\mathrm{A},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{q}-G_\mathrm{q}\,U_\mathrm{AB}.}$$
(5.1)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}}{R_\mathrm{q}+R_\mathrm{L}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_\mathrm{L}}{R_\mathrm{q}+R_\mathrm{L}}\,U_\mathrm{q}.}$$
(5.2)

Theorem 6.1 (Equivalence of real sources)

A real voltage source and a real current source are equivalent if

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{q}}{G_\mathrm{q}}}$$
$$\text{and}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{G_\mathrm{q}}.}$$
(6.1)

Stage 7: Interconnecting sources

Theorem 7.1 (Interconnecting real sources)

Two sources in series or in parallel, respectively, act like one:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{eq}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q1}+U_\mathrm{q2},\quad R_\mathrm{eq}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{q1}+R_\mathrm{q2}}$$
$$\text{or}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{eq}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{q1}+I_\mathrm{q2},\quad G_\mathrm{eq}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G_\mathrm{q1}+G_\mathrm{q2}.}$$
(7.1)

For the parallel connection, in voltage-source form this means

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{eq}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{G_\mathrm{q1}U_\mathrm{q1}+G_\mathrm{q2}U_\mathrm{q2}}{G_\mathrm{q1}+G_\mathrm{q2}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{eq}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{q1}\parallel R_\mathrm{q2}.}$$
(7.2)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{circ}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q1}-U_\mathrm{q2}}{R_\mathrm{q1}+R_\mathrm{q2}}.}$$
(7.3)

Stage 8: Superposition

Theorem 8.1 (Superposition)

In a linear network with $n$ independent voltage sources and $m$ independent current sources, every branch voltage and every branch current satisfies

$$\begin{aligned} U_\mathrm{AB}&=a_1U_\mathrm{q1}+\dots+a_nU_{\mathrm{q}n}+Z_1I_\mathrm{q1}+\dots+Z_mI_{\mathrm{q}m},\\ I_\mathrm{CD}&=Y_1U_\mathrm{q1}+\dots+Y_nU_{\mathrm{q}n}+b_1I_\mathrm{q1}+\dots+b_mI_{\mathrm{q}m}. \end{aligned}$$
(8.1)(8.2)

The coefficients depend only on the resistors, not on the sources.

Stage 9: Thevenin and Norton

Theorem 9.1 (Equivalent sources: Thevenin and Norton)

Every linear one-port behaves at its terminals like an equivalent voltage source and equally like an equivalent current source:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{i}-R_\mathrm{i}\,I_\mathrm{A},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{i}-G_\mathrm{i}\,U_\mathrm{AB},}$$
(9.1)

with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{OC},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{SC},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{G_\mathrm{i}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{OC}}{I_\mathrm{SC}}.}$$
(9.2)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{U_\mathrm{x}-U_\mathrm{y}}{I_\mathrm{x}-I_\mathrm{y}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{x}+R_\mathrm{i}I_\mathrm{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{x}I_\mathrm{y}-U_\mathrm{y}I_\mathrm{x}}{I_\mathrm{y}-I_\mathrm{x}}.}$$
(9.3)

Stage 10: The R-2R converter

Theorem 10.1 (R-2R ladder)

For the converter in Fig. 12 with the output open,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{out}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{ref}\left(\frac{d_2}{2}+\frac{d_1}{4}+\frac{d_0}{8}\right),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R.}$$
(10.1)

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. In a linear network the effects of the sources add up, because all the equations are linear. This is superposition, the overlaying of effects.

  2. Therefore the voltage at two terminals A and B is a straight line in the terminal current: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{OC}-R_\mathrm{i}\,I_\mathrm{A}}$. The sources set the open-circuit voltage $U_\mathrm{OC}$ (the voltage with the terminals open), the resistors set the internal resistance $R_\mathrm{i}$ (the slope).

  3. A straight line has two numbers. So the network behaves like an equivalent source, a source with an internal resistance. The open circuit and the short circuit (terminals connected) supply the numbers: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{OC}/I_\mathrm{SC}}$.

Stage 1: From four-pole to two-port

Definition 1.1 (Four-pole, port, two-port)

At a four-pole with the terminals 1, 1$'$, 2, 2$'$, let all terminal currents be counted toward the network. It always holds that

A terminal pair is a port if the port condition holds. A four-pole with two ports is a two-port:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_{1'}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_1,}$$

Stage 3: Impedance form and open-circuit test

Definition 3.1 (Impedance form, Z form)

The impedance form, also resistance form, takes the currents as causes:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{11}I_1+Z_{12}I_2,\quad U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{21}I_1+Z_{22}I_2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{in short}\quad\mathbf U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf Z\cdot\mathbf I,\quad\mathbf Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}&Z_{12}\\mathbb{Z}_{21}&Z_{22}\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(3.1)

The Z parameters follow from two open-circuit tests:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z_{11}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{U_1}{I_1}\right|_{I_2=0},\quad Z_{21}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{U_2}{I_1}\right|_{I_2=0},\quad Z_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{U_1}{I_2}\right|_{I_1=0},\quad Z_{22}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{U_2}{I_2}\right|_{I_1=0}.}$$
(3.2)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z_{11}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{22}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(R_\mathrm{x}+R_\mathrm{y})\parallel(R_\mathrm{x}+R_\mathrm{y})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_\mathrm{x}+R_\mathrm{y}}{2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z_{21}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_\mathrm{y}-R_\mathrm{x}}{2}.}$$
(3.3)

Stage 4: The two-port in operation

Theorem 4.1 (Input resistance and output side)

With the load $Z_\mathrm{L}$ at port 2, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-Z_\mathrm{L}I_2}$ and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{Z_{21}}{Z_{22}+Z_\mathrm{L}}\,I_1,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_1}{I_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{11}-\frac{Z_{12}Z_{21}}{Z_{22}+Z_\mathrm{L}}.}$$
(4.1)

The limiting cases are the open-circuit and the short-circuit input resistance:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z_{1,\mathrm{OC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{11},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z_{1,\mathrm{SC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{11}-\frac{Z_{12}Z_{21}}{Z_{22}}.}$$
(4.2)

If a source $U_\mathrm{q}$, $R_\mathrm{q}$ feeds port 1, port 2 acts as an equivalent voltage source (Chapter 5) with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{Z_{21}}{Z_{11}+R_\mathrm{q}}\,U_\mathrm{q},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{22}-\frac{Z_{12}Z_{21}}{Z_{11}+R_\mathrm{q}}.}$$
(4.3)

Stage 5: Admittance form and short-circuit test

Definition 5.1 (Admittance form, Y form)

The admittance form, also conductance form, takes the voltages as causes:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11}U_1+Y_{12}U_2,\quad I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{21}U_1+Y_{22}U_2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{in short}\quad\mathbf I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf Y\cdot\mathbf U,\quad\mathbf Y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}Y_{11}&Y_{12}\\Y_{21}&Y_{22}\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(5.1)

The Y parameters follow from two short-circuit tests:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{11}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{I_1}{U_1}\right|_{U_2=0},\quad Y_{21}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{I_2}{U_1}\right|_{U_2=0},\quad Y_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{I_1}{U_2}\right|_{U_1=0},\quad Y_{22}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left.\frac{I_2}{U_2}\right|_{U_1=0}.}$$
(5.2)

For a load with the conductance $Y_\mathrm{L}$ at port 2, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-Y_\mathrm{L}U_2}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{Y_{21}}{Y_{22}+Y_\mathrm{L}}\,U_1,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_1}{U_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11}-\frac{Y_{12}Y_{21}}{Y_{22}+Y_\mathrm{L}},}$$
(5.3)

with the limiting cases

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{1,\mathrm{SC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{1,\mathrm{OC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11}-\frac{Y_{12}Y_{21}}{Y_{22}}.}$$
(5.4)

Stage 10: Properties and overview of all forms

Definition 10.1 (Reciprocal, unilateral, active)

A two-port is called reciprocal (also reversible, transfer-symmetric) if the same cause shows the same effect in both directions. It is called unilateral if the input does not depend on the output:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{reciprocal:}\quad&Z_{12}=Z_{21},\quad Y_{12}=Y_{21},\quad H_{12}=-H_{21},\quad\det\mathbf A=1,\\ \text{unilateral:}\quad&Z_{12}=0,\quad Y_{12}=0,\quad H_{12}=0,\quad\det\mathbf A=0. \end{aligned}$$
(10.1)(10.2)

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_1I_1}$ and the delivered power $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-U_2I_2}$, the power absorbed by the two-port is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_1-P_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_1I_1+U_2I_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{11}U_1^2+(Y_{12}+Y_{21})U_1U_2+Y_{22}U_2^2.}$$
(10.3)

The two-port is active if $P<0$ is possible in some operating state, and passive if $P\ge0$ always holds.

Stage 1: Energy per charge times charge per time

Definition 1.1 (Energy and power)

If the charge $\mathrm{d} Q$ passes through the voltage $u$, it gives off the energy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{d} W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u\,\mathrm{d} Q}$. With the current $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{d} Q/\mathrm{d} t}$, it follows that

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{d} W}{\mathrm{d} t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u(t)\,i(t),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int u\,\mathrm{d} Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{t_1}^{t_2}u(t)\,i(t)\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$
(1.1)

For DC quantities, that is, when $U$ and $I$ do not depend on time,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U\,I\,\Delta t,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{W}{\Delta t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U\,I.}$$
(1.2)

Stage 2: Why the resistor gets warm

Theorem 2.1 (Joule heat and power rating)

In a resistor, all the electrical power turns into heat:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U^2}{R}.}$$
(2.1)

A component tolerates this only up to its power rating $P_\mathrm{max}$:

$$P\le P_\mathrm{max}.$$
(2.2)

Stage 3: Average power with a sinusoidal voltage

Theorem 3.1 (Average power at a resistor)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat u\cos(\omega t)}$ across $R$, the current $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat\imath\cos(\omega t)}$ flows with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat\imath}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat u/R}$. The time average of the power is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1T\int_0^T u(t)\,i(t)\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\frac{\hat u^2}{R}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\hat\imath^2R.}$$
(3.1)

Stage 4: Line losses

Theorem 4.1 (Line losses)

If the current $I$ flows to the load over a line with $R_\mathrm{cable}$, then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{cable}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{cable}\,I,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{cable}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{cable}\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{cable}\,I^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{useful}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(U_\mathrm{q}-U_\mathrm{cable})\,I}$$
(4.1)

and therefore

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{P_\mathrm{useful}}{P_\mathrm{q}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(U_\mathrm{q}-U_\mathrm{cable})\,I}{U_\mathrm{q}\,I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\frac{U_\mathrm{cable}}{U_\mathrm{q}}.}$$
(4.2)

For two voltages with the same load power $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{L1}I_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{L2}I_2}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{I_1}{I_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{L2}}{U_\mathrm{L1}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{P_\mathrm{cable,1}}{P_\mathrm{cable,2}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left(\frac{U_\mathrm{L2}}{U_\mathrm{L1}}\right)^{\!2}.}$$
(4.3)

Stage 5: Where the power of a source goes

Definition 5.1 (Power balance and efficiency)

In the circuit with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}/(R_\mathrm{i}+R_\mathrm{L})}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}\,I,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{Ri}\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I^2R_\mathrm{i},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{L}\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I^2R_\mathrm{L},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_\mathrm{v}+P_\mathrm{L}.}$$
(5.1)

The efficiency is the power used relative to the total power expended by the source:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{P_\mathrm{L}}{P_\mathrm{q}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I\,U_\mathrm{L}}{I\,U_\mathrm{q}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{L}}{U_\mathrm{q}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_\mathrm{L}}{R_\mathrm{L}+R_\mathrm{i}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_\mathrm{L}/R_\mathrm{i}}{1+R_\mathrm{L}/R_\mathrm{i}}.}$$
(5.2)

Stage 6: Power matching

Theorem 6.1 (Power matching)

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}/(R_\mathrm{i}+R_\mathrm{L})}$, the power at the load is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}(R_\mathrm{L})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left(\frac{U_\mathrm{q}}{R_\mathrm{i}+R_\mathrm{L}}\right)^{\!2}R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}^2\,\frac{R_\mathrm{L}}{\bigl(R_\mathrm{i}(1+R_\mathrm{L}/R_\mathrm{i})\bigr)^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_\mathrm{SC}\,\frac{\alpha}{(1+\alpha)^2},}$$
(6.1)

with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{L}/R_\mathrm{i}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{SC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}^2/R_\mathrm{i}}$. It is greatest for

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{i},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L,max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}^2}{4\,R_\mathrm{i}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{P_\mathrm{SC}}{4},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{SC}}{2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}}{2}.}$$
(6.2)

Stage 7: Why 50 %, and which goal applies

Theorem 7.1 (Power and efficiency)

Dividing $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}-R_\mathrm{i}I}$ by $U_\mathrm{q}$ gives, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I/I_\mathrm{SC}}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-x,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{P_\mathrm{L}}{P_\mathrm{SC}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x\,\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x(1-x).}$$
(7.1)

At power matching, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}\,\frac{R}{2(R_\mathrm{q}+R)},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{L}^2}{R_\mathrm{L}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}^2\,R}{4\,R_\mathrm{q}(R_\mathrm{q}+R)}.}$$
(7.2)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}^2}{R_\mathrm{tot}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}^2\,(2R_\mathrm{q}+R)}{2R_\mathrm{q}(R_\mathrm{q}+R)},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{P_\mathrm{L}}{P_\mathrm{q}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\frac{R}{2R_\mathrm{q}+R}<\frac12\quad\text{for }R<\infty.}$$
(7.3)

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. Voltage is energy per charge, current is charge per time. Their product is energy per time: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u\,i}$.

  2. In a resistor, the field accelerates the electrons, and every collision hands the energy gained to the atomic lattice. That is why all the power there turns into heat, the Joule heat, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I^2}$.

  3. A source with internal resistance $R_\mathrm{i}$ delivers the greatest power to the load $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{i}}$. This is called power matching. There, the energy of each charge divides equally between source and load, and the efficiency, the fraction of the power that is used, is 50 %. A fixed voltage is provided by voltage matching (load large compared with the source), a fixed current by current matching (load small compared with the source).

From the ground up: Three equations, three unknowns

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\p&q&r\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\,(e\,r-f\,q)-b\,(d\,r-f\,p)+c\,(d\,q-e\,p).}$$
(0.1)
Theorem 0.1 (Cramer's rule)

If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det\mathbf M\neq0}$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf M\,\mathbf x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf b}$ has exactly one solution. Its $i$-th component is

Stage 1: How many quantities are free?

Theorem 1.1 (Free quantities of a network)

The $z$ branch currents of a connected network must satisfy $k-1$ independent node equations, the $z$ branch voltages $z-k+1$ independent mesh equations. Therefore

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z-k+1\ \text{currents}}$$
$$\text{and}$$
$$k-1\ \text{voltages}$$
(1.1)

can be chosen freely. All other currents and voltages follow from Kirchhoff.

Stage 2: Mesh currents satisfy the node rule

Theorem 2.1 (Mesh currents)

If you place a mesh current $I_\mu$ with a freely chosen direction in each of $m$ independent meshes, the current of a branch is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{branch}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\sigma_\mu\,I_\mu,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma_\mu}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{cases}+1&\text{mesh }\mu\text{ runs in the arrow direction of the branch,}\\-1&\text{mesh }\mu\text{ runs against the arrow,}\\0&\text{mesh }\mu\text{ does not contain the branch.}\end{cases}}$$
(2.1)

The node rule is then satisfied for all values of the $I_\mu$. Conversely, every current distribution that satisfies the node rule can be represented in this way.

Stage 3: Mesh current analysis

Definition 3.1 (Mesh current analysis)

The method has four steps:

  1. Convert real current sources into real voltage sources: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm q\,R}$, the same resistor in series (Chapter 5).

  2. Choose $m$ independent meshes and enter one mesh current each with any direction. The direction of traversal of the mesh is this direction.

  3. Set up the mesh rule for each mesh or read the matrix off directly.

  4. Solve the system for the mesh currents and calculate branch currents and branch voltages from them.

Theorem 3.2 (Matrix form of mesh current analysis)

The mesh currents satisfy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf Z\,\mathbf I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf U_\mathrm q}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}&Z_{12}&\dots&Z_{1m}\\mathbb{Z}_{21}&Z_{22}&\dots&Z_{2m}\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\mathbb{Z}_{m1}&Z_{m2}&\dots&Z_{mm}\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}I_1\\I_2\\\vdots\\I_m\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ \begin{pmatrix}U_\mathrm{q1}\\U_\mathrm{q2}\\\vdots\\U_{\mathrm qm}\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(3.1)

  • $Z_{\mu\mu}$ is the sum of all resistances of mesh $\mu$.

  • $Z_{\mu\nu}$ is the coupling resistance, the sum of the resistances traversed by both meshes: positive if both currents flow there in the same direction, negative for opposite currents, zero without a shared branch. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z_{\mu\nu}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Z_{\nu\mu}}$ holds.

  • $U_{\mathrm q\mu}$ is the sum of the source voltages in the mesh, positive for each source that drives the mesh current. With voltage arrows from the plus to the minus pole (ASC, Chapter 2) this means: an arrow against the traversal counts as positive, an arrow along the traversal as negative.

Stage 4: Potentials satisfy the mesh rule

Definition 4.1 (Reference node and node voltage)

A freely chosen node 0 is the reference node; its potential is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. The node voltage of another node $\mu$ is its voltage against the reference node:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mu}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_\mu-\varphi_0.}$$
(4.1)

The voltage of a branch between the nodes $\mu$ and $\nu$ is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{\mu\nu}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mu-U_\nu}$; toward the reference node it is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{\mu0}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mu}$.

Theorem 4.2 (Node voltages)

For any values of the $k-1$ node voltages, every mesh rule is satisfied. Conversely, every voltage distribution that satisfies the mesh rule has exactly one set of node voltages.

Stage 5: Node voltage analysis

Definition 5.1 (Node voltage analysis)

The method has four steps:

  1. Convert real voltage sources into real current sources: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q/R}$, the same resistor in parallel (Chapter 5).

  2. Fix a reference node and enter the potentials of the other nodes as voltages against it. Write resistors as conductances.

  3. Set up the node rule for the $k-1$ remaining nodes or read the matrix off directly.

  4. Solve the system for the node voltages and calculate branch voltages and branch currents from them.

Theorem 5.2 (Matrix form of node voltage analysis)

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k-1}$, the node voltages satisfy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf Y\,\mathbf U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf I_\mathrm q}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}Y_{11}&Y_{12}&\dots&Y_{1n}\\Y_{21}&Y_{22}&\dots&Y_{2n}\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\Y_{n1}&Y_{n2}&\dots&Y_{nn}\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}U_1\\U_2\\\vdots\\U_n\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ \begin{pmatrix}I_\mathrm{q1}\\I_\mathrm{q2}\\\vdots\\I_{\mathrm qn}\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(5.1)

  • $Y_{\mu\mu}$ is the sum of all conductances connected to node $\mu$.

  • $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{\mu\nu}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Y_{\nu\mu}}$ is the negative coupling conductance, that is, minus the sum of the conductances lying directly between the nodes $\mu$ and $\nu$. Without a direct connection, the entry is zero.

  • $I_{\mathrm q\mu}$ is the sum of the source currents at the node, positive if they flow into the node.

Stage 6: Which method when?

Theorem 6.1 (Comparison of the number of equations)

Node voltage analysis needs fewer equations exactly when $k-1<z-k+1$, that is,

$$z>2k-2.$$
(6.1)

Stage 7: Stamps and netlist

Theorem 7.1 (Stamps of conductance and current source)

Each row is the node rule “outgoing currents = impressed currents”.

  • A conductance $G$ between $i$ and $k$ carries the current $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_Y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G\,(U_i-U_k)}$ from $i$ to $k$. It adds $+G$ to the positions $(i,i)$ and $(k,k)$ and $-G$ to $(i,k)$ and $(k,i)$.

  • A fixed current source that pushes $I_\mathrm q$ through itself from $k$ to $i$ adds $+I_\mathrm q$ in row $i$ and $-I_\mathrm q$ in row $k$ of the right-hand side. It enters nothing in $\mathbf Y$, because its current depends on no voltage.

Stage 8: Controlled sources and amplifiers

Theorem 8.1 (Stamp of the voltage-controlled current source)

A source with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g\,(U_p-U_q)}$ from $i$ to $k$ adds $+g$ to $(i,p)$ and $(k,q)$ and $-g$ to $(i,q)$ and $(k,p)$.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i&0&-G_\mathrm i\\g&G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L&-g-G_\mathrm o\\-g-G_\mathrm i&-G_\mathrm o&G_\mathrm S+g+G_\mathrm i+G_\mathrm o\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}U_1\\U_2\\U_3\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}I_\mathrm q\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(8.3)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{U_2}{I_\mathrm q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{G_\mathrm iG_\mathrm o-g\,G_\mathrm S}{(G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L)\,[(G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i)\,G_\mathrm S+G_\mathrm qG_\mathrm i]+G_\mathrm L\,[(G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i)\,G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm q\,g]}.}$$
(8.4)

Stage 9: Modified nodal analysis

Theorem 9.1 (Modified nodal analysis, MNA)

A network with $n$ nodes besides the reference node and one ideal voltage source between $i$ and $k$ satisfies

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf Y&\mathbf C\\\mathbf B&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf U\\I_V\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf I_\mathrm q\\U_\mathrm q\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(9.1)

$\mathbf C$ is the new column with $+1$ in row $i$ and $-1$ in row $k$. $\mathbf B$ is the new row with $+1$ in column $i$ and $-1$ in column $k$. $I_V$ counts from $i$ through the source to $k$. The new row reads

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_i-U_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm q.}$$
(9.2)

For each further voltage source, one row and one column are added.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}G_\mathrm i&0&0\\g&G_\mathrm o&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_1\\U_2\\I_\mathrm{out}\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}I_\mathrm{in}\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{I_\mathrm{out}}{I_\mathrm{in}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{I_\mathrm{in}}\cdot\frac{g\,I_\mathrm{in}}{-G_\mathrm i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{g}{G_\mathrm i}.}$$
(9.3)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}G_\mathrm i&0&1\\g&G&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_\mathrm i\\U_\mathrm o\\I_V\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\U_\mathrm q\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm o}{U_\mathrm q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{U_\mathrm q}\cdot\frac{g\,U_\mathrm q}{-G}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac gG}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-g\,R,}$$
(9.4)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{voltage gain}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\text{transconductance}\cdot\text{load resistance}.}$$
(9.5)

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. A network with $z$ branches and $k$ nodes has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z-k+1}$ independent meshes and $k-1$ independent nodes. Of the $z$ currents only $m$ are free, of the $z$ voltages only $k-1$.

  2. Mesh current analysis calculates with the mesh current, an imagined circulating current for each mesh. It enters every node and leaves it again, so the node rule holds by itself. Node voltage analysis calculates with the node voltage, the voltage of each node against a freely chosen reference node. Every branch voltage is a difference, so the mesh rule holds by itself.

  3. The remaining equations can be read off the circuit as a matrix: in the resistance matrix, the diagonal holds the sum of the resistances of a mesh, and next to it stand the shared resistances of two meshes. In the conductance matrix, the diagonal holds the sum of the conductances at a node, and next to it stands the negative conductance between two nodes. Modified nodal analysis also takes the current of each voltage source as an unknown, and that is how SPICE calculates.

Chapter 9: Semiconductors and Diodes

Overview: Semiconductors and Diodes

What this is about
The reference model for this chapter

  • ⁠Diode: silicon, reverse saturation current $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-14}}$ A (the small current in the reverse direction), thermal voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{25.85}$ mV (thermal energy per charge) at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{300}$ K (27 °C). The switch model uses the forward voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.7}$ V (voltage across the conducting diode).

  • ⁠pn junction: $10^{16}$ foreign atoms per cm$^3$ on each side, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n_\mathrm i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}$ free electrons in pure silicon, relative permittivity $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon_\mathrm r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11.7}$.

  • ⁠Circuit: source $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ V, resistor $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$, diode in series.

  • ⁠Rectifier: sinusoidal voltage with a peak value of 12 V and 50 Hz, load 1 k$\Omega$.

  • ⁠Constants: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.602\cdot10^{-19}}$ C, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k_\mathrm B}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.3806\cdot10^{-23}}$ J/K.

From the ground up: Valve, AC voltage and thermal energy

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{fraction of particles with at least the energy }W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-W/(k_\mathrm BT)}.}$$
(0.1)

Stage 1: Why silicon hardly conducts

Definition 1.1 (Bands)

The valence band (VB) is the top band that is fully occupied at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. The conduction band (CB) lies above it and is empty at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. The band gap $W_\mathrm g$ is the smallest energy that lifts an electron from the VB into the CB. The Fermi energy $W_\mathrm F$ separates occupied from empty states at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. At $T>0$ it is the energy at which a state would be occupied half of the time; it may lie in the gap even though there is no state there.

Theorem 1.2 (Fraction of electrons in the conduction band)

In a pure semiconductor, $W_\mathrm F$ lies in the middle of the gap. For the states at the edge of the CB, this gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{occupation}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-W_\mathrm g/(2k_\mathrm BT)}.}$$
(1.1)

Stage 2: Doping

Definition 2.1 (n-type and p-type doping)

A donor (phosphorus) gives up an electron, an acceptor (boron) takes one up. $N_\mathrm D$ donors per cm$^3$ create an n-region, $N_\mathrm A$ acceptors per cm$^3$ a p-region:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{n-region: }n}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ N_\mathrm D,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{p-region: }p}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ N_\mathrm A,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{everywhere: }n\,p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n_\mathrm i^2.}$$
(2.1)

Stage 3: The space-charge region

Theorem 3.1 (Built-in voltage and width of the region)

For an abrupt junction, with the thermal voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k_\mathrm BT/e}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D0}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_T\ln\frac{N_\mathrm AN_\mathrm D}{n_\mathrm i^2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{w}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{e}\Bigl(\frac1{N_\mathrm A}+\frac1{N_\mathrm D}\Bigr)U_\mathrm{D0}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_\mathrm{max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2U_\mathrm{D0}}{w}.}$$
(3.1)

Stage 4: Why the current grows exponentially

Theorem 4.1 (Shockley equation)

For the ideal diode with $U$ from anode to cathode and $I$ from anode to cathode,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm S\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{U/U_T}-1\bigr),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{k_\mathrm BT}{e}.}$$
(4.1)

Stage 5: Characteristic curve, breakdown and special diodes

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_\mathrm{j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\varepsilon A}{w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{C_0}{\sqrt{1+U_\mathrm R/U_\mathrm{D0}}}.}$$
(5.1)
Theorem 5.1 (Illuminated diode)

The characteristic curve shifts downward by the photocurrent $I_\mathrm{Ph}$. The equivalent circuit is a current source in parallel with the diode:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm S\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{U/U_T}-1\bigr)-I_\mathrm{Ph},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{open circuit }(I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0):\ U_\mathrm L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_T\ln\Bigl(\frac{I_\mathrm{Ph}}{I_\mathrm S}+1\Bigr).}$$
(5.2)

Stage 6: The operating point

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_{R1}}{R_1},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_R+U_{R1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I+U_{R1},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_0-U_{R1}}{R}.}$$
(6.1)
Theorem 6.2 (Operating point of the diode)

For the series circuit, both of these hold:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_0-U_\mathrm D}{R}}$$
$$\text{and}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm S\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm D/U_T}-1\bigr).}$$
(6.2)

The characteristic curve rises everywhere, the line falls everywhere, so there is exactly one intersection.

Stage 7: Linear equivalent circuits

Definition 7.1 (Two diode models)

Ideal diode: a short circuit when conducting, an open circuit when blocking.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm D>0\ \Rightarrow\ U_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm D<0\ \Rightarrow\ I_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.}$$
(7.1)

Diode with forward voltage: a voltage source $U_\mathrm F$ when conducting, an open circuit when blocking.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm D>0\ \Rightarrow\ U_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm F,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm D<U_\mathrm F\ \Rightarrow\ I_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.}$$
(7.2)

Stage 8: Small changes around the operating point

Theorem 8.1 (Differential resistance)

At the OP with the direct current $I$, for small changes $u_\mathrm D$ and $i$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{d} U_\mathrm D}{\mathrm{d} I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_T}{I+I_\mathrm S}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_T}{I},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{r\,i.}$$
(8.1)

Stage 9: Rectifiers and charge pump

Theorem 9.1 (Bridge rectifier)

In the model with forward voltage, since the current always passes through two diodes in series,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{cases}|u_\mathrm e|-2U_\mathrm F&|u_\mathrm e|>2U_\mathrm F\\0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}}$$
(9.1)

A blocked diode bears at most $\hat u-U_\mathrm F$.

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. At the pn junction, mobile charges move into the other region and leave fixed ions behind. The field of these ions builds up a barrier in the space-charge region that stops the equalization.

  2. Heat lifts only the fraction $\mathrm{e}^{-W/(k_\mathrm BT)}$ of the charge carriers over a barrier of height $W$. Voltage in the forward direction lowers the barrier, so the current grows exponentially, by a factor of 10 per 60 mV: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm S(\mathrm{e}^{U/U_T}-1)}$. In the reverse direction only the tiny current $-I_\mathrm S$ remains.

  3. In a circuit, the operating point lies at the intersection of the characteristic curve and the load line (the straight line set by source and resistor). For hand calculations the diode becomes a switch with a fixed forward voltage, and for small changes around the operating point it becomes a resistor $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_T/I}$.

Chapter 10: The Bipolar Transistor

Overview: The Bipolar Transistor

What this is about
The reference model for this chapter

  • ⁠Transistor T: an npn small-signal transistor made of silicon with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{B}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{200}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{S}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.9\cdot10^{-13}}$ A (saturation reverse current, Chapter 9) and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{26}$ mV (thermal voltage at 300 K, rounded from 25.85 mV). For hand calculations, one approximates $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{BE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6}$ V when the transistor conducts and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE,sat}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.2}$ V when it is fully switched on. Limiting values: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE,max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C,max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{400}$ mW.

  • ⁠Circuit: supply $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12}$ V, collector resistor $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ k$\Omega$, base resistor $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{440}$ k$\Omega$, input voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ V. This gives the operating point $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ µ A, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ V.

  • ⁠Version with negative feedback: base divider $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{31}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$ k$\Omega$, emitter resistor $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ k$\Omega$ (Stage 7).

Stage 1: Three layers, two voltages

Definition 1.1 (Currents and voltages at the npn transistor)

$I_\mathrm{B}$ and $I_\mathrm{C}$ are counted into the component, $I_\mathrm{E}$ out of it. Voltages are potential differences, and the first index is the plus pole: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{BE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_\mathrm{B}-\varphi_\mathrm{E}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_\mathrm{C}-\varphi_\mathrm{E}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_\mathrm{C}-\varphi_\mathrm{B}}$. It always holds that

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{B}+I_\mathrm{C},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{CB}+U_\mathrm{BE}.}$$
(1.1)

Stage 2: Why current flows through the blocking diode

Theorem 2.1 (Currents in normal operation)

If the BE diode conducts with $U_\mathrm{BE}\gg U_T$ and the collector lies above the base, then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{BE}/U_T},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{C}}{B},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(1+\frac1B\Bigr)I_\mathrm{C}.}$$
(2.1)

Stage 3: One surface, many characteristic curves

Theorem 3.1 (The model surface)

For $U_\mathrm{BE}\gg U_T$ and $U_\mathrm{CE}\ge0$, the reference model has

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\underbrace{I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{BE}/U_T}}_{\text{injection}}\cdot\underbrace{\bigl(1-\mathrm{e}^{-U_\mathrm{CE}/U_T}\bigr)}_{\text{collection}}\cdot\underbrace{\Bigl(1+\frac{U_\mathrm{CE}-6\,\mathrm{V}}{80\,\mathrm{V}}\Bigr)}_{\text{small slope}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{S}}{B}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{BE}/U_T}.}$$
(3.1)

Stage 4: Three regions, three equivalent circuits

Definition 4.1 (Operating regions of the npn transistor)

With the threshold voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{BE,on}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6}$ V and the saturation voltage $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE,sat}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.2}$ V:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Cut-off region:}\quad & U_\mathrm{BE}<U_\mathrm{BE,on}\;\Rightarrow\; I_\mathrm{B}=I_\mathrm{C}=0,\\ \text{active region:}\quad & U_\mathrm{BE}=U_\mathrm{BE,on},\ I_\mathrm{B}>0,\quad I_\mathrm{C}=B\,I_\mathrm{B},\quad U_\mathrm{CE}\ge U_\mathrm{CE,sat},\\ \text{saturation region:}\quad & U_\mathrm{BE}=U_\mathrm{BE,on},\ I_\mathrm{B}>0,\quad U_\mathrm{CE}=U_\mathrm{CE,sat},\quad I_\mathrm{C}\le B\,I_\mathrm{B}. \end{aligned}$$
(4.1)(4.2)(4.3)
$$\begin{aligned} &\text{cut-off region: } U_\mathrm{BE}>-0.6\,\mathrm{V};\qquad \text{active: } I_\mathrm{C}=B\,I_\mathrm{B},\ U_\mathrm{CE}\le-0.2\,\mathrm{V};\\ &\text{saturation: } U_\mathrm{CE}=-0.2\,\mathrm{V},\ I_\mathrm{C}\le B\,I_\mathrm{B}. \end{aligned}$$
(4.4)

Stage 5: The operating point

Theorem 5.1 (Operating point of the circuit with base and collector resistor)

The input mesh is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{B}I_\mathrm{B}+U_\mathrm{BE}}$, the output mesh is the load line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{CC}-R_\mathrm{C}I_\mathrm{C}}$. For $U_\mathrm{in}\le U_\mathrm{BE,on}$ the transistor blocks and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{BE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{in}}$. For $U_\mathrm{in}>U_\mathrm{BE,on}$, in both conducting regions

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{in}-U_\mathrm{BE,on}}{R_\mathrm{B}}.}$$
(5.1)

Assuming active operation gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{B\,I_\mathrm{B},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{CC}-B\,I_\mathrm{B}R_\mathrm{C}.}$$
(5.2)

If the result is $U_\mathrm{CE}\ge U_\mathrm{CE,sat}$, the assumption is correct. Otherwise saturation applies, with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{CE,sat},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{CC}-U_\mathrm{CE,sat}}{R_\mathrm{C}}<B\,I_\mathrm{B}.}$$
(5.3)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE,OP}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{CE,sat}+\frac{\Delta U}{2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.1\,\mathrm{V},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{CC}-U_\mathrm{CE,OP}}{R_\mathrm{C}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.97\,\mathrm{mA}.}$$
(5.4)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{in}-U_\mathrm{BE,on}}{R_\mathrm{E}},\quad I_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{E}}{1+B},\quad I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{B\,I_\mathrm{B},\quad U_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{CC}-R_\mathrm{C}I_\mathrm{C}-R_\mathrm{E}I_\mathrm{E}.}$$
(5.5)

Stage 6: The transistor as a switch

Theorem 6.1 (Characteristic of the inverter)

With the linear model,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{out}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ \begin{cases} U_\mathrm{CC} & U_\mathrm{in}<U_\mathrm{BE},\\[2pt] U_\mathrm{CC}-B\,R_\mathrm{C}\dfrac{U_\mathrm{in}-U_\mathrm{BE}}{R_\mathrm{B}} & U_\mathrm{BE}\le U_\mathrm{in}\le U_\mathrm{in,sat},\\[6pt] U_\mathrm{CE,sat} & U_\mathrm{in}>U_\mathrm{in,sat}, \end{cases}}$$
(6.1)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{in,sat}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(U_\mathrm{CC}-U_\mathrm{CE,sat})\,R_\mathrm{B}}{B\,R_\mathrm{C}}+U_\mathrm{BE}.}$$
(6.2)

Stage 7: Negative feedback holds the operating point in place

Theorem 7.1 (Operating point with negative feedback)

Current feedback with the divider $R_1$, $R_2$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_1\parallel R_2}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{CC}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2},\quad I_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}-U_\mathrm{BE}}{(1+B)R_\mathrm{E}+R_\mathrm{i}},\quad I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}-U_\mathrm{BE}}{R_\mathrm{E}\bigl(1+\frac1B\bigr)+\frac{R_\mathrm{i}}{B}}.}$$
(7.1)

For $R_\mathrm{E}\gg R_\mathrm{i}/B$ and $B\gg1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(U_\mathrm{q}-U_\mathrm{BE})/R_\mathrm{E}}$; if in addition $U_\mathrm{q}\gg U_\mathrm{BE}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ U_\mathrm{CC}R_2/\bigl(R_\mathrm{E}(R_1+R_2)\bigr)}$.

Voltage feedback with $R_\mathrm{B}$ from the collector to the base:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{CC}-U_\mathrm{BE}}{R_\mathrm{C}\bigl(1+\frac1B\bigr)+\frac{R_\mathrm{B}}{B}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{CC}-U_\mathrm{BE}}{R_\mathrm{C}+\frac{R_\mathrm{B}}{B}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{for } R_\mathrm{C}\gg\frac{R_\mathrm{B}}{B}:\ I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{CC}-U_\mathrm{BE}}{R_\mathrm{C}}.}$$
(7.2)

Stage 8: Two circuits that make use of $U_\mathrm{BE}$

Theorem 8.1 (Current mirror)

With equal transistors and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{B1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{B2}}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{ref}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{CC}-U_\mathrm{BE}}{R_\mathrm{ref}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{C1}+I_\mathrm{B1}+I_\mathrm{B2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(B+2)I_\mathrm{B2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{B\,I_\mathrm{B2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{ref}}{1+\frac2B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ I_\mathrm{ref}.}$$
(8.1)
Theorem 8.2 (Voltage regulator with Zener diode)

The mesh across Zener diode, BE path and load gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{Z}-U_\mathrm{BE}.}$$
(8.2)

Stage 9: Tangent instead of curve

Definition 9.1 (Small-signal quantities at the operating point)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial I_\mathrm{C}}{\partial U_\mathrm{BE}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{C}}{U_T},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{BE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial U_\mathrm{BE}}{\partial I_\mathrm{B}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_T}{I_\mathrm{B}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{B}{S},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{r_\mathrm{CE}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial I_\mathrm{C}}{\partial U_\mathrm{CE}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{C}}{U_\mathrm{Early}+U_\mathrm{CE}}.}$$
(9.1)

For the changes this gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i_\mathrm{b}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{u_\mathrm{be}}{r_\mathrm{BE}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i_\mathrm{c}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S\,u_\mathrm{be}+\frac{u_\mathrm{ce}}{r_\mathrm{CE}}.}$$
(9.2)

Stage 10: Three basic circuits

Theorem 10.1 (Voltage gain of the basic circuits)

For small signals in the active region, $r_\mathrm{CE}$ neglected, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\mathrm{out}/u_\mathrm{in}}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{emitter}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-S\,R_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}}{U_T},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{collector}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{S\,R_\mathrm{E}}{1+S\,R_\mathrm{E}}<1,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{base}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+S\,R_\mathrm{C}.}$$
(10.1)

With an emitter resistor, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{C}/(R_\mathrm{E}+1/S)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{C}/R_\mathrm{E}}$; with $r_\mathrm{CE}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{emitter}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-S\,(R_\mathrm{C}\parallel r_\mathrm{CE})}$.

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. The emitter gives off electrons, the thin base lets them through, the collector collects them. The voltage $U_\mathrm{BE}$ decides how many electrons set off: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{BE}/U_T}}$. The field of the blocking collector diode sweeps them out, and only a small remainder flows through the base, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{C}/B}$. The factor $B$ is called the current gain.

  2. Three terminals mean two independent voltages. That is why $I_\mathrm{C}$ is a surface over $U_\mathrm{BE}$ and $U_\mathrm{CE}$, and each characteristic curve is a slice of it. On this surface the external circuit selects the operating point, the DC state of the transistor. An emitter resistor holds it in place through negative feedback: the effect weakens its own cause.

  3. For small signals, the tangent replaces the curve. Its slope, the transconductance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{C}/U_T}$, turns the transistor into a controlled current source. The voltage gain of the common-emitter circuit, the standard circuit with the emitter at ground, is the DC voltage across the collector resistor divided by $U_T$.

Overview: The Operational Amplifier

What this is about

Stage 1: Noise

Definition 1.1 (Noise)

The rms value of the noise voltage and the density of the thermal noise of a resistor are

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{r,eff}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{\int_0^\infty|u_\mathrm{r}(f)|^2\,\mathrm{d} f},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|u_\mathrm{r,R}(f)|^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\,k_\mathrm{B}\,T\,R.}$$
(1.1)

The signal-to-noise ratio is the ratio of the powers, written in decibels (dB):

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{SNR}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{P_\mathrm{s}}{P_\mathrm{r}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\frac{u_\mathrm{s,eff}}{u_\mathrm{r,eff}}\Bigr)^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{SNR}_\mathrm{dB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\lg\frac{P_\mathrm{s}}{P_\mathrm{r}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20\lg\frac{u_\mathrm{s,eff}}{u_\mathrm{r,eff}}.}$$
(1.2)

Stage 2: The differential amplifier

Theorem 2.1 (Differential amplifier)

T1 has its base at the input $\varphi_-$, T2 at the input $\varphi_+$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_+-\varphi_-}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{a1}-U_\mathrm{a2}}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_0}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-U_\mathrm{D}/U_T}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_0}{1+\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{D}/U_T}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{C}\,(I_\mathrm{C2}-I_\mathrm{C1}).}$$
(2.1)

For $|U_\mathrm{D}|\ll U_T$ this is a straight line:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A_\mathrm{D}\,U_\mathrm{D},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S\,R_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_0\,R_\mathrm{C}}{2\,U_T}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}}{U_T}.}$$
(2.2)

Stage 3: The op amp without feedback

Definition 3.1 (Model of the op amp)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_+-\varphi_-,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{cases}U_\mathrm{A,max}&\text{for }A\,U_\mathrm{D}\ge U_\mathrm{A,max},\\A\,U_\mathrm{D}&\text{in between},\\U_\mathrm{A,min}&\text{for }A\,U_\mathrm{D}\le U_\mathrm{A,min}.\end{cases}}$$
(3.1)

The ideal op amp has $r_\mathrm{e}\to\infty$ (no input current), $A\to\infty$ at all frequencies, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{a}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ (the output current adjusts itself so that all node and mesh equations are satisfied) and no noise.

Stage 4: Negative feedback

Theorem 4.1 (Amplifier with negative feedback)

If the fraction $\beta$ of the output is fed to the minus input and $U_\mathrm{E}$ is at the plus input, then in the linear range

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{E}}{1+A\beta},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{A}}{U_\mathrm{E}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{A}{1+A\beta}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1\beta\cdot\frac{1}{1+\dfrac1{A\beta}}.}$$
(4.1)

For $A\beta\gg1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/\beta}$ with the relative deviation $-1/(A\beta)$. A change in $A$ acts weakened by the factor $1+A\beta$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\Delta G}{G}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{1+A\beta}\cdot\frac{\Delta A}{A}.}$$
(4.2)

With the divider, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_1/(R_1+R_2)}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+R_2/R_1}$.

Stage 5: The rule and the virtual ground

Theorem 5.1 (The rule for the op amp with negative feedback)

As long as $U_\mathrm{A,min}<U_\mathrm{A}<U_\mathrm{A,max}$,

  1. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varphi_+}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_-}$: The output adjusts itself so that the difference vanishes.

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_+}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_-}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: No current flows into the inputs.

  3. The output supplies any current needed. No node equation is written for the output node.

Set up the node rule at the minus input, solve for $U_\mathrm{A}$, and check the limits last.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{U_\mathrm{E}}{R_1}+\frac{U_\mathrm{A}}{R_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0\quad\Rightarrow\quad U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{R_2}{R_1}\,U_\mathrm{E},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{E}}{I_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_1.}$$
(5.1)

Stage 6: Several inputs, several stages

Theorem 6.1 (Summing amplifier, difference amplifier, chain)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{F}\sum_i\frac{U_{\mathrm{E}i}}{R_{\mathrm{E}i}},}$$
(6.1)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1}\cdot\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}\,U_\mathrm{E2}-\frac{R_2}{R_1}\,U_\mathrm{E1}.}$$
(6.2)

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2}$, what remains is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_2}{R_1}\,(U_\mathrm{E2}-U_\mathrm{E1})}$. Stages in a chain multiply: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G_1\,G_2}$.

Stage 7: Calculating with capacitor and diode

Theorem 7.1 (Operations)

With the plus input at ground and an ideal op amp,

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Integrator: }&u_\mathrm{A}(t)=u_\mathrm{A}(0)-\frac{1}{R\,C}\int_0^t u_\mathrm{E}(t')\,\mathrm{d} t', \\ \text{Differentiator: }&u_\mathrm{A}(t)=-R\,C\,\frac{\mathrm{d} u_\mathrm{E}}{\mathrm{d} t}, \\ \text{Logarithmic amplifier: }&U_\mathrm{A}=-U_T\,\ln\frac{U_\mathrm{E}}{R\,I_\mathrm{S}}, \\ \text{Exponential amplifier: }&U_\mathrm{A}=-R\,I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{U_\mathrm{E}/U_T}. \end{aligned}$$
(7.1)(7.2)(7.3)(7.4)

Stage 8: Current as the signal

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R\,I_\mathrm{E},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\Delta U_-}{\Delta I_\mathrm{E}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R}{1+A}.}$$
(8.1)
Theorem 8.1 (NIC)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_1}{I_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_2.}$$
(8.2)

The rule holds only as long as the resistance $R_\mathrm{i}$ that acts at terminal 1 with respect to ground is smaller than $R_2$.

Stage 9: Positive feedback and the Schmitt trigger

Theorem 9.1 (Schmitt trigger)

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_1/(R_1+R_2)}$ the output flips

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{downward at }U_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\beta\,U_\mathrm{A,max},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{upward at }U_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\beta\,U_\mathrm{A,min}.}$$
(9.1)

The width of the hysteresis is $\beta\,(U_\mathrm{A,max}-U_\mathrm{A,min})$. Between the thresholds both states are possible.

Stage 10: Where the rules end

Definition 10.1 (Common-mode rejection)

The common-mode rejection CMRR (common mode rejection ratio) compares the gain of the difference with that of the common mode:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{CMRR}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{A}{A_\mathrm{cm}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{CMRR}_\mathrm{dB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20\lg\frac{A}{A_\mathrm{cm}}.}$$
(10.1)

Ideally $\mathrm{CMRR}\to\infty$; usual values are 60 dB ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{cm}/A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-3}}$) to 120 dB ($10^{-6}$).

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|G(f)|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1/\beta}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_G)^2}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\beta\,f_\mathrm{T},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G\,f_G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_\mathrm{T}.}$$
(10.2)
The calculation scheme

  1. ⁠No feedback (comparator): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{A,max}}$ or $U_\mathrm{A,min}$, $U_\mathrm{D}\ne0$.

  2. ⁠Check the sign: Raise the output in your mind. If $U_\mathrm{D}$ falls, it is negative feedback; if it rises, positive feedback.

  3. ⁠Negative feedback: Assume $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and calculate $U_\mathrm{A}$ with the node rule and Ohm's law. If it lies within the limits, the calculation is valid. Otherwise the op amp is overdriven: $U_\mathrm{A}$ is at its limit, $U_\mathrm{D}\ne0$, and you calculate the network with this fixed $U_\mathrm{A}$.

  4. ⁠Positive feedback: As a rule, $U_\mathrm{A}$ at the limit, $U_\mathrm{D}\ne0$. The thresholds follow from the sign change of $U_\mathrm{D}$.

  5. ⁠Accuracy: $1/(A\beta)$ at the signal frequency, $U_\mathrm{off}/\beta$ and $I_\mathrm{B}R_2$ at the output.

Key points

The answer in three sentences

  1. Inside sits a differential amplifier, a circuit that responds only to the difference between two voltages: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{A\,U_\mathrm{D}}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_+-\varphi_-}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100000}$ and at most 11 V at the output, this leaves a window of $\pm110$ µ V for $U_\mathrm{D}$.

  2. Negative feedback routes the fraction $\beta$ of the output to the minus input. The output runs until the loop comes to rest, and that happens when $U_\mathrm{D}$ is just enough for the output: 5 V need 50 µ V. The gain becomes $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/\beta}$, and the loop gain $A\beta$ (the factor for one trip around the loop) only sets the small deviation.

  3. This yields a rule: equal potentials at the inputs, no current into them, node rule at the minus input. It explains the virtual ground (a node at 0 V with no connection to ground) and all the basic circuits. It ends where the output hits its limit, where the feedback changes sign, and where $A\beta$ becomes small.
