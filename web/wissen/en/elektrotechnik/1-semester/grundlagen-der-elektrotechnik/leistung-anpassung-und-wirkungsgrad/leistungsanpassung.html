What this stage is about:

The table shows a maximum of $P_\mathrm{L}$. This stage finds it and explains why it lies exactly at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{i}}$.

The idea. Two influences work against each other. With a small $R_\mathrm{L}$, many coulombs arrive, but almost all their energy stays in $R_\mathrm{i}$. With a large $R_\mathrm{L}$, each coulomb gives off almost everything in the load, but few arrive. The straight line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}-R_\mathrm{i}I}$ from Chapter 5 makes this visible: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{L}I}$ is the area of the rectangle under it (Fig. 7). It is largest when its corner lies in the middle of the line, at half the current and half the voltage. Then the same voltage drops across $R_\mathrm{i}$ and $R_\mathrm{L}$ at the same current, so the two resistors are equal.

Theorem 6.1 (Power matching)

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}/(R_\mathrm{i}+R_\mathrm{L})}$, the power at the load is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}(R_\mathrm{L})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\left(\frac{U_\mathrm{q}}{R_\mathrm{i}+R_\mathrm{L}}\right)^{\!2}R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}^2\,\frac{R_\mathrm{L}}{\bigl(R_\mathrm{i}(1+R_\mathrm{L}/R_\mathrm{i})\bigr)^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_\mathrm{SC}\,\frac{\alpha}{(1+\alpha)^2},}$$
(6.1)

with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{L}/R_\mathrm{i}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{SC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}^2/R_\mathrm{i}}$. It is greatest for

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{i},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L,max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}^2}{4\,R_\mathrm{i}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{P_\mathrm{SC}}{4},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm{SC}}{2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_\mathrm{q}}{2}.}$$
(6.2)

⁠How to read it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{SC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}I_\mathrm{SC}}$ is the short-circuit power, the power of the source at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. It ends up entirely in $R_\mathrm{i}$, and no load receives more than a quarter of it. Validity: The source is fixed; only $R_\mathrm{L}$ is chosen. With an equivalent source, $U_\mathrm{OC}$ takes the place of $U_\mathrm{q}$.

Derivation: The maximum, in three ways

By the quotient rule, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\bigl(f/g\bigr)'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(f'g-f\,g')/g^2}$, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\alpha}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1+\alpha)^2}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} P_\mathrm{L}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_\mathrm{SC}\,\frac{(1+\alpha)^2-2\alpha(1+\alpha)}{(1+\alpha)^4}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_\mathrm{SC}\,\frac{1-\alpha}{(1+\alpha)^3}.}$$

The derivative is zero at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, positive before it and negative after it. That is the maximum.

Without a derivative: With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I/I_\mathrm{SC}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_\mathrm{SC}\,x(1-x)}$. This parabola has the zeros 0 and 1, so its maximum $\frac14$ lies at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$.

As a mechanism: A current increase $\mathrm{d} I$ brings $U_\mathrm{q}\mathrm{d} I$ out of the source, and the losses grow by $2R_\mathrm{i}I\,\mathrm{d} I$. At $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{SC}/2}$, the loss eats up the gain: 9 V against $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot2\,\Omega\cdot2.25\,\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$ V.

Example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\Omega}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.25}$ A, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.5}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10.125}$ W, which is a quarter of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{SC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40.5}$ W. The loads 0.5 $\Omega$ and 8 $\Omega$ both give 6.48 W. In general, $\alpha$ and $1/\alpha$ supply the same power, because they swap $x$ and $1-x$.

⁠Limiting cases: For $\alpha\to0$, the current is fixed and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ P_\mathrm{SC}\,\alpha}$. For $\alpha\to\infty$, the voltage is fixed and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ P_\mathrm{SC}/\alpha}$.

QuestionI have a fixed load. Should I choose the source so that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{L}}$?

No. The theorem applies to a fixed source and a selectable load. If the load is fixed and $R_\mathrm{i}$ can be chosen, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ is best, because $U_\mathrm{q}^2R_\mathrm{L}/(R_\mathrm{i}+R_\mathrm{L})^2$ falls with $R_\mathrm{i}$. The 16 $\Omega$ load then gets 4 W with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\Omega}$, but 5.06 W with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Exercise 6 ★★ Loudspeaker on the amplifier

An amplifier output acts like $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{i}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\,\Omega}$. (a) Which loudspeaker extracts the greatest power, and how large is it? (b) What does 8 $\Omega$ give? Compare power and efficiency. (c) Which second load supplies the same power as 8 $\Omega$? (d) ★★★ Prove with (6.1) that $\alpha$ and $1/\alpha$ always give the same power.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\,\Omega}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L,max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{144\,\mathrm{V^2}/16\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$ W at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{50}$ %. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ A, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8}$ W, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac23}$. Only 11 % of the power is lost, and the losses fall from 9 W to 4 W. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ goes with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\Omega}$: 8 W, but only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13}$.

(d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\dfrac{1/\alpha}{(1+1/\alpha)^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{1/\alpha}{(\alpha+1)^2/\alpha^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{\alpha}{(1+\alpha)^2}}$.
