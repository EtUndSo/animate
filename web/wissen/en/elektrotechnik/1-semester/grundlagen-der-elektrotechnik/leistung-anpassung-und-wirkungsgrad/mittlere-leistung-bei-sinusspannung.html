Stage 3: Average power with a sinusoidal voltage

DEENES中文
What this stage is about:

With an AC voltage, the power changes constantly. This stage finds the one number that determines the heating.

The idea. At a resistor, $i$ is proportional to $u$. Both change sign at the same time, so their product is never negative. If the charge runs backward, the voltage is also reversed, and it gives off energy again. Both half-waves heat (Fig. 4). The power pulsates between zero and the peak value, twice as fast as the voltage. Its mean value lies at half height.

Theorem 3.1 (Average power at a resistor)

For $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{u(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat u\cos(\omega t)}$ across $R$, the current $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{i(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat\imath\cos(\omega t)}$ flows with $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\hat\imath}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat u/R}$. The time average of the power is

$$\textcolor{#3f1a78}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1T\int_0^T u(t)\,i(t)\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\frac{\hat u^2}{R}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\hat\imath^2R.}$$
(3.1)

⁠How to read it: $\hat u$ and $\hat\imath$ are the amplitudes, the peak values. $T$ is the period, $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\pi/T}$ the angular frequency. On the mains, $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{T}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ ms and $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{314}$ 1/s. Validity: The average runs over whole periods. The formula holds only if $i$ is proportional to $u$, that is, for resistors.

Derivation: Where the factor $\frac12$ comes from

Substituting gives $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u\,i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u^2/R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\hat u^2/R)\cos^2(\omega t)}$. With $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\cos^2x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12(1+\cos2x)}$, this becomes

$$\textcolor{#3f1a78}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{T\,R}\int_0^T\hat u^2\cos^2(\omega t)\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{T\,R}\int_0^T\hat u^2\,\frac12\bigl(1+\cos(2\omega t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d} t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\,\frac{\hat u^2}{R}.}$$

The cosine part goes through two full oscillations in $T$; its integral is zero. With $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\hat u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,\hat\imath}$, the second form follows.

Example. For $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\hat u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ V and $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\,\Omega}$, $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\hat\imath}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ A, the peak value of the power is 20 W and $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$ W. At 50 Hz, 0.2 J flow into the lattice per period. Limiting case: For $\omega\to0$, $u$ becomes a DC voltage and $p$ stays at $\hat u^2/R$. The factor $\frac12$ disappears, because averaging over whole periods is then no longer possible.

Going deeper, not needed for the rest: RMS value

A DC voltage $U$ produces the same heat in $R$ if $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{U^2/R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\hat u^2/R}$, that is, $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat u/\sqrt2}$. This value is called the RMS value. Here it is 7.07 V. The 230 V of the wall outlet are an RMS value; the amplitude is 325 V.

QuestionWith alternating current, the electrons only swing back and forth. How can that deliver energy?

Because the energy does not travel with the electrons. Even at 1 A/mm$^2$ and 50 Hz, they cover only about 0.5 µ m per half-period. The field in the resistor accelerates them in both directions, and every collision gives energy to the lattice. That requires no forward progress, only voltage and current.

Exercise 3 ★★ The fan heater

A fan heater absorbs on average 2 kW from a sinusoidal voltage with $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\hat u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{325}$ V and 50 Hz. (a) What are $R$, $\hat\imath$ and the peak value of the power? (b) How many times per second is $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$? (c) A component blocks every second half-wave. Predict the average power if $R$ stays the same.

Solution

(a) From (3.1): $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat u^2/(2P)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{26.4\,\Omega}$, $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\hat\imath}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\hat u/R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12.3}$ A, peak value $\textcolor{#3f1a78}{\hat u\,\hat\imath}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ kW. (b) Twice per period, so 100 times per second. (c) Every second half-wave is missing, and with it half of the energy: 1 kW.
