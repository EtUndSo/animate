What this stage is about:

Voltage says how much energy a charge gives off, current says how much charge passes. This stage combines the two into energy per time.

The idea. Between the terminals of a component there is a voltage $U$. According to Chapter 1, every coulomb that gets from the higher to the lower potential gives off the energy $U$ in joules. The current $I$ counts how many coulombs pass through per second. So $U\cdot I$ joules are converted per second (Fig. 1). Both factors are needed: voltage without current converts nothing, because no charge is moving. Current without voltage converts nothing, because no charge gives anything off.

Definition 1.1 (Energy and power)

If the charge $\mathrm{d} Q$ passes through the voltage $u$, it gives off the energy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{d} W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u\,\mathrm{d} Q}$. With the current $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{d} Q/\mathrm{d} t}$, it follows that

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{d} W}{\mathrm{d} t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u(t)\,i(t),}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int u\,\mathrm{d} Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{t_1}^{t_2}u(t)\,i(t)\,\mathrm{d} t.}$$
(1.1)

For DC quantities, that is, when $U$ and $I$ do not depend on time,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U\,I\,\Delta t,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{W}{\Delta t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U\,I.}$$
(1.2)

⁠How to read it: $u$ in V, $i$ in A, $p$ in watts: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[P]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{V\,A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{J/s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm W}$. An indicator LED converts 20 mW, an electric kettle 2 kW, a power plant unit 1 GW. The expression $W/\Delta t$ is in general the mean value of the power over the time $\Delta t$, and $p(t)$ is its instantaneous value. Devices often run continuously, which is why they are characterized by their power and not by their energy. For energy, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{W\,s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{V\,A\,s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{J}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{N\,m}}$. Electrical engineering mostly calculates in watt-seconds or kilowatt-hours, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{kWh}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.6\,\mathrm{MJ}}$. Joules are common for combustion processes, newton-meters for mechanical work.

⁠Signs: In the passive sign convention (PSC, Chapter 2), $p>0$ means that the component absorbs energy, and $p<0$ that it gives off energy. In the active sign convention (ASC), it is the other way around. Validity: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u\,i}$ holds for every one-port if $u$ and $i$ are counted at the same pair of terminals. Nothing is assumed about the interior of the component.

Derivation: Why only voltage and current count

In the time $\mathrm{d} t$, the charge $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{d} Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{i\,\mathrm{d} t}$ enters at terminal 1 and the same charge leaves at terminal 2 (node rule, Chapter 2). The interior remains unchanged. For the balance, it is as if $\mathrm{d} Q$ had gone directly from $\varphi_1$ to $\varphi_2$. According to Chapter 1, the energy given off is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{d} W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\varphi_1-\varphi_2)\,\mathrm{d} Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u\,i\,\mathrm{d} t}$. Dividing by $\mathrm{d} t$ gives (1.1). Electrons move the opposite way and carry negative charge. The two signs together give the same product.

Example

At $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16\,\Omega}$ there are 8 V, and 0.5 A flow (Chapter 5). Each coulomb gives off 8 J, and 0.5 C arrive per second: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ W. In one hour that is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\,\mathrm{W}\cdot3600\,\mathrm{s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{14.4\,\mathrm{kJ}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\,\mathrm{Wh}}$. Counted individually, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I/e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.12\cdot10^{18}}$ electrons pass through per second, and each gives off $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{e\cdot8\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.28\cdot10^{-18}}$ J. The product is again 4 W.

⁠Limiting cases: At open terminals there are 9 V and 0 A, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. An ideal wire has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ at any current.

QuestionIs current used up in the component?

No. The same current flows before and after the component; the charge comes back out completely. What is used up is energy: the charge continues at a lower potential. That is why the bill for “electricity consumption” shows kilowatt-hours.

Exercise 1 ★ Energy and sign

(a) A rechargeable battery with 3.7 V stores the charge 2 Ah (2 A for one hour). How much energy is that in Wh and in kJ? How long does it supply a device with 0.74 W? (b) An electric kettle with 2 kW runs for 3 minutes. How many kWh is that, and what do they cost at 30 ct/kWh? (c) At a one-port, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3}$ A are measured in the passive sign convention. Does it absorb power or deliver it?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\mathrm{A}\cdot3600\,\mathrm{s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{7200}$ C, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.7\,\mathrm{V}\cdot7200\,\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{26.64}$ kJ $=7.4$ Wh. At 0.74 W, that lasts 10 h. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\mathrm{kW}\cdot0.05\,\mathrm{h}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.1}$ kWh, which costs 3 ct. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12\,\mathrm{V}\cdot(-3\,\mathrm{A})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-36}$ W. The one-port delivers 36 W.
