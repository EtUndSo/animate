The answer in three sentences

  1. Voltage is energy per charge, current is charge per time. Their product is energy per time: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u\,i}$.

  2. In a resistor, the field accelerates the electrons, and every collision hands the energy gained to the atomic lattice. That is why all the power there turns into heat, the Joule heat, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I^2}$.

  3. A source with internal resistance $R_\mathrm{i}$ delivers the greatest power to the load $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{i}}$. This is called power matching. There, the energy of each charge divides equally between source and load, and the efficiency, the fraction of the power that is used, is 50 %. A fixed voltage is provided by voltage matching (load large compared with the source), a fixed current by current matching (load small compared with the source).

Key points

  • Power is energy per coulomb times coulombs per second: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u\,i}$. Energy is its time integral.

  • In a resistor, collisions give everything to the lattice: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U^2/R}$, limited by $P_\mathrm{max}$.

  • At a resistor with a sinusoidal voltage, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\hat u^2/R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\hat\imath^2R}$.

  • The same power at $n$ times the voltage lowers the line loss to $1/n^2$.

  • Balance: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{P_\mathrm{v}+P_\mathrm{L}}$. Efficiency: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{L}/U_\mathrm{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{L}/(R_\mathrm{L}+R_\mathrm{i})}$.

  • Greatest power at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{i}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P_\mathrm{L,max}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}^2/(4R_\mathrm{i})}$, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\eta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12}$, because current and efficiency run linearly against each other.

  • Power engineering wants $R_\mathrm{L}\gg R_\mathrm{i}$, signal transmission $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{L}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{i}}$. Equivalent sources give the maximum correctly, but not the efficiency.

One principle behind all the results. Every power is the product of two answers: How much energy does one coulomb give off at this point, and how many coulombs pass per second?
