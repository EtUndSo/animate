From the ground up: What electrical engineering is and where charge comes from

The discipline of electrical engineering is the engineering science that moves electric charges in a controlled way in order to transmit energy and process information. A power plant, a motor, a wireless network and a processor all rest on the same process: forces set charges in motion. Physics describes nature with measurable quantities and looks for the laws connecting them. The engineering sciences apply these laws, for example in components, circuits (connections of components) and power plants.

All materials consist of atoms. An atom has a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons, around which electrons move (Fig. 1). Protons carry a positive charge, electrons a negative charge of exactly the same size, neutrons none. An atom contains as many protons as electrons; seen from outside, their charges cancel, and it is neutral. If it loses an electron, a positive charge is left over. An atom with a surplus charge is called an ion. Protons sit firmly in the nucleus and are 1836 times heavier than electrons, whereas outer electrons can be detached. That is why electrons are what moves in nearly all processes of electrical engineering.

Five questions guide you through the chapter: What is charge (Stage 2)? How does the force between charges arise (Stage 3)? Why is the concept of the field needed for it (Stages 4 and 5)? Why is voltage a potential difference (Stages 6 to 8)? What does this mean concretely for a charge in a field (Stage 9)? Stage 1 provides the tools.
