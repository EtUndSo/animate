Stage 1: Quantities, force and work

  • Deutsch
  • English
  • Español
  • Français
  • Türkçe
  • 中文
What this stage is about:

Voltage will turn out to be work per charge. That requires quantities with units, force, work and kinetic energy.

The idea. Someone coming in from the snow calls a room warm; someone coming out of the sauna calls it cool. Such judgments are qualitative. A thermometer shows both of them 21 °C; that is quantitative: a number on a scale, independent of who is measuring. Physics states its laws in terms of such quantities, for example Ohm's law $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\cdot I}$ (Part 2). The most important mechanical quantity in this chapter is work: a force acting along a path transfers energy, and only its component along the path counts. Lifting a backpack means working against gravity; carrying it horizontally does not (Fig. 2).

Definition 1.1 (Physical quantity)

A physical quantity is a measurable property, the product of numerical value and unit:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{quantity}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{numerical value}\cdot\text{unit}.}$$
(1.1)

⁠How to read it: In $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$ V, $U$ is the symbol (italic), 9 the numerical value, V the unit (upright). Square brackets ask for the unit: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[U]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm V}$. The numerical value depends on the unit, the quantity does not: 72 km/h and 20 m/s are the same speed.

Units. The SI system of units has seven base quantities, which cannot be expressed in terms of one another: length (meter, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s), electric current (ampere, A), absolute temperature (kelvin, K), luminous intensity (candela, cd) and amount of substance (mole, mol). Their definitions continue to be refined along with measurement technology. The units of all other quantities are products of powers of the base units. An electric current is moving charge; one ampere transports one coulomb per second. Power is energy per time. For this chapter the following is enough:

Calculating with units.

  1. ⁠Adding is allowed only for quantities with the same unit; prefixes are matched first: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\,\mathrm{kV}+40\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.04\,\mathrm{kV}}$, not 42 kV. Different units in a sum nearly always reveal a calculation error.

  2. ⁠Multiplying is always allowed. Numerical values, units and prefixes are multiplied separately, and in fractions units cancel like numbers: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{6\,\mathrm{N\,m}}{3\,\mathrm{m}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\mathrm{N}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.5\,\mathrm{A}\cdot9\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.5\,\mathrm{W}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{k\cdot M}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm G}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mu\text{}\cdot\mathrm M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. In the reference model, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{9\,\mathrm{V}/3\,\mathrm{mm}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3000\,\mathrm{V/m}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\,\mathrm{kV/m}}$.

  3. ⁠Converting sometimes needs a factor: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{Wh}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3600\,\mathrm{W\,s}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{km/h}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{3.6}\,\mathrm{m/s}}$. Because prefix and unit form one symbol, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{km^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1000\,\mathrm{m})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^6\,\mathrm{m^2}}$, not 1000 m$^2$.

  4. ⁠The quantity determines the unit. Depending on the field, energy is written in W s, N m or J. But equal base units do not prove equal quantities: hertz (oscillations per second) and becquerel (decays per second) are both $1/\mathrm s$, and N m is also torque (force times lever arm).

Force and work. A force deforms a body that is held in place or accelerates a movable one. Energy is the capacity to do work. Force and displacement are vectors: quantities with magnitude and direction, written with an arrow, $\vec F$. Velocity is the change of position per time, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{d} s/\mathrm{d} t}$, acceleration the change of velocity per time, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{d} v/\mathrm{d} t}$.

Definition 1.2 (Force and work)

The force on a body with mass $m$ and acceleration $a$, and the force between two masses $m$, $M$ at the distance $r$, are

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m\,a,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\gamma\,\frac{m\,M}{r^2}.}$$
(1.2)

The work done by a force along a path is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\vec F\cdot\Delta\vec s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{F\,\Delta s\cos\alpha,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\int_{P_1}^{P_2}\vec F\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec s.}$$
(1.3)
Theorem 1.3 (Constant acceleration and kinetic energy)

With the initial values $s_0$ and $v_0$ at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, for constant $a$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v_0+a\,t,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{s_0+v_0\,t+\tfrac12a\,t^2.}$$
(1.4)

A body with the velocity $v$ carries the kinetic energy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm{kin}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12m\,v^2}$.

⁠How to read it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[F]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm N}$; at the surface of the Earth, about 1 N acts on 100 g. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\gamma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.674\cdot10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}}$ is the gravitational constant. $\alpha$ is the angle between the force and the path segment. The product $\vec F\cdot\Delta\vec s$ is called the scalar product; its result is a number. The integral is called a line integral: the path is divided into short straight pieces and their contributions are summed. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[W]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm J}$; one joule lifts 102 g by one meter. For $\alpha<90^\circ$ the force gives energy to the body, for $\alpha>90^\circ$ it takes energy from it. Validity: (1.4) holds only for constant acceleration; Stage 9 needs exactly this case.

Derivation: Kinetic energy

From (1.4) with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v/a}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12a\,t^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v^2/(2a)}$. Over this distance, a constant force $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m\,a}$ does the work $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F\,s}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m\,a\,v^2/(2a)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12m\,v^2}$.

Example. The weight $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F_\mathrm{G}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m\,g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{19.62\,\mathrm{N}}$ acts on the backpack. To lift it steadily, the hand applies the same force upward. Over the stairs, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{19.62\,\mathrm{N}\cdot6\,\mathrm{m}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{117.7\,\mathrm{J}}$; over the ramp, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{19.62\,\mathrm{N}\cdot10\,\mathrm{m}\cdot0.6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{117.7\,\mathrm{J}}$. The hand does $+117.7$ J of work, the weight $-117.7$ J. The energy is then stored in the height, as potential energy: if the backpack falls, it becomes kinetic energy. This pair, work of the field force and work from outside, returns in Stage 6. Limiting cases: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{F\,\Delta s}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{90^\circ}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta W}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

QuestionCarrying something horizontally still makes your arm tired. Is no work being done there?

Not on the backpack: it gains neither height nor speed. The muscle consumes energy all the same, because its fibers keep tensing anew. That energy turns into heat in the body and never reaches the backpack.

Exercise 1 ★ Units and prefixes

Calculate and give the result in a suitable unit: (a) $3\,\mathrm{kN}+450\,\mathrm{N}$, (b) $2\,\mathrm{mA}\cdot4\,\mathrm{ks}$, (c) $6\,\mu\text{C}/3\,\mathrm{ms}$, (d) 1 kWh in joules, (e) 1.5 mm$^2$ in m$^2$, (f) the unit V/m in base units.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3000\,\mathrm{N}+450\,\mathrm{N}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.45\,\mathrm{kN}}$. (b) Milli and kilo cancel, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{A\cdot s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm C}$: 8 C. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mu\text{}/\mathrm m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm m}$ (milli) and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{C/s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm A}$: 2 mA. (d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1000\,\mathrm{W}\cdot3600\,\mathrm{s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.6\,\mathrm{MJ}}$. (e) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{mm^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}}$, so $1.5\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. (f) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm V}{\mathrm m}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{kg\,m^2}}{\mathrm{A\,s^3}\cdot\mathrm m}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{kg\,m}}{\mathrm{A\,s^3}}}$.
