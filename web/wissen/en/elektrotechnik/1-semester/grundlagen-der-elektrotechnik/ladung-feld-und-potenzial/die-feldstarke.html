Stage 4: The field strength

What this stage is about:

Coulomb's law gives the force but does not say how it gets across empty space. The concept of the field answers this and separates cause from effect.

The idea. Instead of “B pulls A across 3 cm”, the process is split in two. First: charge B changes the space around it; it produces a field. Second: charge A feels the field at its own location and experiences a force there. Why charges produce fields cannot be traced back to anything simpler; it is a fundamental law. But the picture solves three problems. Cause and effect are separated: The field depends only on the charges producing it and holds for any charge brought into it. Many charges become manageable: Their fields are added once, and after that you calculate with one vector per location. The effect takes time: If B is moved, the force on A changes only when the change in the field arrives there at the speed of light. Over 3 cm this takes 100 ps, from the Sun to the Earth 8.3 minutes. In the meantime, the change resides in the field, so the field is more than a computational aid.

Analogy (a picture for orientation, not mathematics)

The Earth is surrounded by a gravitational field: at every location, the force $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{m\,\vec g}$ acts on every mass. The field $\vec g$ is force per mass; the electric field is force per charge. The picture stops working in two places: masses always attract, and a gravitational field cannot be shielded.

Definition 4.1 (Electric field strength)

If a small test charge $Q$ is brought to a location and the force $\vec F$ on it is measured, the electric field strength there is

$$\vec E:=\lim_{Q\to0}\frac{\vec F}{Q},$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\text{hence}\quad\vec F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\cdot\vec E.}$$
(4.1)

The direction of $\vec E$ is the direction of the force on a positive test charge. The electric field assigns such a vector to every location.

⁠How to read it: The unit is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[E]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{N/C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{m\,kg\,A^{-1}\,s^{-3}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{V/m}}$, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{J/C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{N\,m/C}}$. On a negative charge, the force acts opposite to $\vec E$. Typical values: in a current-carrying conductor 0.1 V/m, in the atmosphere in clear weather 100 to 200 V/m, at the surface of a broadcast antenna 1 to 1000 V/m, at a high-voltage line $10^6$ V/m, in a capacitor (a component made of two plates, Chapter 3) $10^6$ to $10^8$ V/m. At $3\cdot10^6$ V/m air breaks down and a spark forms. Validity: The limit $Q\to0$ is needed because a large test charge displaces the charges producing the field, such as the free electrons of a metal plate. But what is to be measured is the field without the test charge.

Theorem 4.2 (Field of point charges)

A point charge $Q_0$ produces at the distance $r$ the field

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{Q_0}{r^2}\cdot\frac{\vec r}{r},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,\frac{|Q_0|}{r^2},}$$
(4.2)

where $\vec r$ points from the charge to the location. Several charges together produce the sum of their fields: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_i\vec E_i}$.

Derivation: Field of a point charge

By (3.2), a test charge $Q$ experiences the force $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\,Q\,Q_0/r^2\cdot\vec r/r}$. Dividing by $Q$ removes $Q$; the result depends only on $Q_0$ and the location. The sum rule follows from the superposition of forces.

Example

Sphere B ($-20$ nC) produces at the location of sphere A the field $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\cdot20\,\mathrm{nC}/(3\,\mathrm{cm})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.0\cdot10^5}$ V/m, which points toward B. The force on A is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\mathrm{nC}\cdot2.0\cdot10^5\,\mathrm{V/m}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.0}$ mN as in Stage 3. Conversely, A produces only $1.0\cdot10^5$ V/m at the location of B, but meets twice the charge there: again 2.0 mN.

⁠In short: the force on a charge is its charge times the field at its location.

⁠Limiting cases: For $r\to\infty$, $E\to0$. For $r\to0$, $E$ would grow without bound; this shows the limit of the point-charge model.

QuestionDoes a charge also feel its own field?

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\vec E}$ contains only the field of the other charges. A charge at rest exerts no force on itself.

Exercise 4 ★★ Two charges, one field-free point

On the $x$-axis, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+4}$ nC is at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+1}$ nC at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ cm.

  1. Calculate the magnitude and direction of the field at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ cm. What force does an electron experience there?

  2. Where between the charges is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$?

  3. Limiting case: Where does this point move for $Q_2\to0$? Does this fit the field of a single charge?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\cdot4\,\mathrm{nC}/(3\,\mathrm{cm})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{40.0}$ kV/m to the right, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k\cdot1\,\mathrm{nC}/(3\,\mathrm{cm})^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9.99}$ kV/m to the left, together $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{30.0}$ kV/m to the right. Electron: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{e\,E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.8\cdot10^{-15}}$ N to the left.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ requires $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q_1/x^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q_2/(a-x)^2}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ cm. From this $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2/x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/(a-x)}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac23a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ cm. (c) In general, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a/(1+\sqrt{Q_2/Q_1})}$. For $Q_2\to0$, $x\to a$: the point moves to the location of $Q_2$ and vanishes: a single charge has no field-free point.
