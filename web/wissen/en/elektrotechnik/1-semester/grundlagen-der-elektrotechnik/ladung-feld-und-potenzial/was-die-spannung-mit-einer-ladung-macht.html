Stage 9: What voltage does to a charge

What this stage is about:

This stage brings field, potential and voltage together on a single electron: What force acts, what energy does it gain, how fast does it get?

The idea. An electron detaches from plate B and starts from rest. The field points from A to B. Because of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q\vec E}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-e}$, the force acts opposite to the field, toward plate A. It is equally large everywhere, so the acceleration is constant and (1.4) holds. On the way, potential energy turns into kinetic energy. The electron moves toward the high potential, yet its energy $Q\varphi$ falls, because $Q$ is negative.

Theorem 9.1 (Charge in the field)

In an electrostatic field, the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy stays constant:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12m\,v^2+Q\,\varphi}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{constant}.}$$
(9.1)

A charge that passes through the voltage $U$ from rest reaches, in a uniform field of length $d$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{\frac{2\,|Q\,U|}{m}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{|Q|\,E}{m},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2\,d}{v}.}$$
(9.2)
Derivation: Energy theorem and flight time

The work of the field becomes kinetic energy entirely: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac12mv_2^2-\frac12mv_1^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{W_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q(\varphi_1-\varphi_2)}$. Rearranging gives (9.1). With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|Q\,U|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12m\,v^2}$. At constant acceleration the average speed is $v/2$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d/(v/2)}$.

⁠How to read it: $m$ is the mass in kg, $\varphi$ the potential at the location. The path does not appear in $v$; the voltage alone determines the energy. Validity and neglected effects: The equations hold in vacuum. In air, an electron collides with a molecule after only fractions of a micrometer. The weight $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m_\mathrm{e}g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8.9\cdot10^{-30}}$ N is dropped; it is $5.4\cdot10^{13}$ times smaller than the field force. In addition, $v\ll c$ must hold. Here $v$ is only 0.6 % of the speed of light $c$, and the correction to the kinetic energy is 0.003 %.

Example

For the electron in the pair of plates,

$$\begin{aligned} F&=e\,E=4.81\cdot10^{-16}\,\mathrm{N},\qquad a=\frac{F}{m_\mathrm{e}}=5.28\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\qquad W=e\,U=1.44\cdot10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J},\\ v&=\sqrt{\frac{2\cdot1.44\cdot10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J}}{9.109\cdot10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}}}=1.78\cdot10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s},\qquad t=\frac{2\cdot3\,\mathrm{mm}}{v}=3.37\,\mathrm{ns}. \end{aligned}$$

A proton moves from A to B. It gains the same energy but is 1836 times heavier: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.15\cdot10^4}$ m/s, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{1836}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{42.9}$ times slower, flight time 144 ns.

⁠In short: if a charge in vacuum passes through a voltage, its kinetic energy changes by charge times voltage, regardless of path and distance.

⁠Limiting cases: For $U\to0$, $v\to0$. Double the plate distance at the same voltage halves $E$ and $a$ but does not change $v$: the electron accelerates more weakly but for longer, and needs 6.74 ns. Four times the voltage gives twice the speed.

QuestionDoes the electron really move against the field? Surely a field drives charges in its own direction.

It drives positive charges in its direction; that is how the direction of $\vec E$ is defined. Every charge moves of its own accord to where its potential energy $Q\varphi$ becomes smaller. For the electron, that is the higher potential.

Going deeper, not needed for the rest: The electronvolt

The energy an elementary charge gains on passing through 1 V is called the electronvolt: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{eV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.602\cdot10^{-19}}$ J. The electron in the example gains 9 eV.

Exercise 10 ★★★ Electron against the field

An electron leaves plate A of the reference model with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.0\cdot10^6}$ m/s toward plate B.

  1. Does it reach plate B? Compare its kinetic energy with the energy needed.

  2. At which location $x$ does it turn around? Calculate once with the energy theorem and once with (1.4).

  3. When is it back at plate A, and with what speed?

  4. What initial speed would it need to just reach B?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{W_\mathrm{kin}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12m_\mathrm{e}v_0^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.55\cdot10^{-19}}$ J. Reaching B would take $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{e\cdot9\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.44\cdot10^{-18}}$ J. It does not reach B. (b) Energy theorem: it travels until $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta\varphi}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{W_\mathrm{kin}/e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.84}$ V are used up. At 3 V/mm, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.948}$ mm. With (1.4): deceleration $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5.28\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}$, turnaround at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v_0/a}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v_0^2/(2a)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.948}$ mm.

(c) There and back: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2v_0/a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.79}$ ns. The potential is the same again, so by (9.1) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.0\cdot10^6}$ m/s, in the opposite direction. (d) Exactly the energy from the example: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.78\cdot10^6}$ m/s.
