Stage 10: Three basic circuits

What this stage is about:

Input and output need two terminals each, and the transistor has three in total. So one terminal becomes common for the signal. This stage works out the three possibilities.

The idea. The common terminal gives the circuit its name (Fig. 12). In the common-emitter circuit, the signal comes in at the base and leaves from the collector. $u_\mathrm{in}$ lies fully across the BE path and produces $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i_\mathrm{c}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{S\,u_\mathrm{in}}$. This current flows through $R_\mathrm{C}$ and pulls the collector potential down: the output is inverted. In the common-collector circuit, the signal comes in at the base and leaves from the emitter. The emitter follows the base: if it stood still, $U_\mathrm{BE}$ would change, and the resulting current would at once raise it via $R_\mathrm{E}$. That is why it is also called the emitter follower. In the common-base circuit, the base is held fixed and the signal comes in at the emitter. If the emitter falls, $U_\mathrm{BE}$ rises, and more current pulls the collector down: input and output move in the same sense. The magnitude corresponds to the common-emitter circuit.

Theorem 10.1 (Voltage gain of the basic circuits)

For small signals in the active region, $r_\mathrm{CE}$ neglected, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\mathrm{out}/u_\mathrm{in}}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{emitter}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-S\,R_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}}{U_T},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{collector}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{S\,R_\mathrm{E}}{1+S\,R_\mathrm{E}}<1,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{base}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+S\,R_\mathrm{C}.}$$
(10.1)

With an emitter resistor, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{C}/(R_\mathrm{E}+1/S)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{C}/R_\mathrm{E}}$; with $r_\mathrm{CE}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{emitter}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-S\,(R_\mathrm{C}\parallel r_\mathrm{CE})}$.

⁠How to read it: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{C}R_\mathrm{C}}$ is the DC voltage across the collector resistor, 6 V in the reference model. In short: the gain of the common-emitter circuit is the DC voltage across the collector resistor divided by $U_T$. Neither $B$ nor the transistor type appears in it. Validity: $|u_\mathrm{be}|\ll U_T$, $R_\mathrm{C}\ll r_\mathrm{CE}$, driven by a source with low internal resistance.

Derivation: Three meshes in the small signal

For the signal, $U_\mathrm{CC}$ is ground. Common emitter: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{be}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\mathrm{in}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{out}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{C}i_\mathrm{c}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{C}S\,u_\mathrm{in}}$, and with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{C}/U_T}$ follows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S\,R_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{C}R_\mathrm{C}/U_T}$. With an emitter resistor: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\mathrm{be}+R_\mathrm{E}S\,u_\mathrm{be}}$, the larger part of $u_\mathrm{in}$ lies across $R_\mathrm{E}$, and the rest, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{be}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\mathrm{in}/(1+S\,R_\mathrm{E})}$, does the controlling. Common collector: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{out}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\mathrm{E}S\,u_\mathrm{be}}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{be}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_\mathrm{in}-u_\mathrm{out}}$, solve. Common base: the base rests, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{be}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-u_\mathrm{in}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_\mathrm{out}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R_\mathrm{C}S\,u_\mathrm{be}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+S\,R_\mathrm{C}u_\mathrm{in}}$.

Example

Common-emitter circuit at point A: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-6\,\mathrm{V}/26\,\mathrm{mV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-231}$, more accurately $-215$ with $r_\mathrm{CE}$. 1 mV at the input becomes 0.23 V at the output. The point in the middle allows a voltage swing, an output amplitude, of almost 6 V, but at 25 mV at the input the tangent has long ceased to be valid. For the small-signal limit of 2.6 mV it is 0.6 V. For an amplifier, the operating point must lie in the area of nearly constant slope, and the swing should be as large as possible. The version with negative feedback has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3\,\text{k}\Omega/(1\,\text{k}\Omega+12.9\,\Omega)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2.96}$: only 1.3 % of $u_\mathrm{in}$ lies across the BE path, but in exchange the gain is determined almost entirely by two resistors. Emitter follower with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ k$\Omega$ at 2 mA: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S\,R_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{231}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.996}$. Its strength is not the voltage but the current: the current gain is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1+B}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{201}$. It serves as a buffer: it hardly loads the source and drives the load with low impedance.

⁠Limiting cases: $R_\mathrm{E}\to0$ in the form with negative feedback gives $-S\,R_\mathrm{C}$. $R_\mathrm{E}\to\infty$ for the emitter follower gives $A\to1$.

QuestionBut a real common-emitter circuit has a flatter characteristic than the common-base circuit. Why should the magnitude be the same?

It is the same with direct drive, equal $R_\mathrm{C}$ and equal current. If there is a series resistor before the base, or a source with internal resistance, it forms a voltage divider with $r_\mathrm{BE}$, and only part of $u_\mathrm{in}$ reaches the BE path. Then the characteristic of the common-emitter circuit is flatter. In the common-base circuit this divider does not arise, because the low-impedance source sits at the emitter.

Exercise 12 ★★★ Amplifier and follower

T operates at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ mA and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.3}$ V with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{CC}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$ V, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.7}$ k$\Omega$. (a) Predict how large $A$ of the common-emitter circuit is, using only the voltage across $R_\mathrm{C}$. (b) How large is the output amplitude at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat u_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ mV? How does $r_\mathrm{CE}$ change the result? (c) With an emitter resistor of 470 $\Omega$: How large is $A$? (d) As an emitter follower with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.7}$ k$\Omega$: How large is $A$? Is the output inverted?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{R_\mathrm{C}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.7}$ V, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4.7\,\mathrm{V}/26\,\mathrm{mV}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-181}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat u_\mathrm{out}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.36}$ V. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_\mathrm{CE}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{78}$ k$\Omega$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-S(R_\mathrm{C}\parallel r_\mathrm{CE})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-171}$, only 6 % less.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4.7\,\text{k}\Omega/(470\,\Omega+26\,\Omega)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-9.5}$. The approximation $-R_\mathrm{C}/R_\mathrm{E}$ gives $-10$.

(d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{S\,R_\mathrm{E}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{181}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.994}$. The output is not inverted; the emitter follows the base in phase.
