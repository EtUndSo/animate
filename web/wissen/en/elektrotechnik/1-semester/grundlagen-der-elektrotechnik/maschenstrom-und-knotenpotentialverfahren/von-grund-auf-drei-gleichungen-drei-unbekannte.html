From the ground up: Three equations, three unknowns

Both methods end in a linear system of equations $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf M\,\mathbf x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf b}$, that is, a matrix times a column vector (Chapter 6). $\mathbf x$ contains the unknowns, $\mathbf b$ the known right-hand side. Each row of the matrix is an equation, each column belongs to an unknown. For two unknowns, the determinant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf M}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\,d-b\,c}$ is familiar. For three unknowns, you go through the first row. Each number is multiplied by the determinant that remains after deleting its row and column, and the signs alternate ($+,-,+$):

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\p&q&r\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\,(e\,r-f\,q)-b\,(d\,r-f\,p)+c\,(d\,q-e\,p).}$$
(0.1)
Theorem 0.1 (Cramer's rule)

If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det\mathbf M\neq0}$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf M\,\mathbf x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf b}$ has exactly one solution. Its $i$-th component is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{D_i}{D}.}$$
(0.2)

$D_i$ is the determinant of the matrix in which column $i$ has been replaced by $\mathbf b$.

Derivation: Cramer with two unknowns

From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a\,x+b\,y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{e}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c\,x+d\,y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f}$ you get an equation free of $y$: $d$ times the first minus $b$ times the second equation gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(a\,d-b\,c)\,x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{e\,d-b\,f}$. On the left stands $D\,x$, on the right the determinant with $(e,f)$ in column 1. With more unknowns it works the same way.

Example: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2x+y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x+3y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. The rule is suitable for hand calculation up to about four unknowns, especially if the matrix contains many zeros. A matrix is called symmetric if it does not change when reflected in the main diagonal (from top left to bottom right). The conductance $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/R}$ (Chapter 2) has the unit siemens: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{mS}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/\mathrm{k\Omega}}$.
