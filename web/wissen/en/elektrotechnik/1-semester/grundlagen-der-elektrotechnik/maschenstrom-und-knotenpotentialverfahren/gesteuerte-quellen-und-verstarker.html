What this stage is about:

Amplifiers contain controlled sources (Chapter 6). This stage gives them a stamp and shows what that means for the matrix.

The idea. A voltage-controlled current source (Chapter 6) is a generalized conductance. With a conductance, its own voltage $U_{ik}$ controls the current from $i$ to $k$. With the source, a foreign voltage $U_{pq}$ controls it. The rows remain, because the current flows out of and into the same nodes. The columns move from $i$, $k$ to $p$, $q$, because they count the controlling potentials. The symmetry is lost in the process. That is physics: the input acts on the output, but not the other way around.

Theorem 8.1 (Stamp of the voltage-controlled current source)

A source with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g\,(U_p-U_q)}$ from $i$ to $k$ adds $+g$ to $(i,p)$ and $(k,q)$ and $-g$ to $(i,q)$ and $(k,p)$.

⁠How to read it: $g$ is the transconductance, current per control voltage in siemens, 50 mS in the model. Validity: The source must be linear. Transistors are linear only for small changes around an operating point (Chapter 10).

Examplewithout negative feedback

A current source $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ µ A with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_\mathrm q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5}$ mS feeds node 1. There, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_\mathrm i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.5}$ mS lies to ground. The source $g\,U_1$ draws current from node 2 into ground; $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_\mathrm o}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.05}$ mS and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_\mathrm L}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.2}$ mS are also connected there. The stamps give

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i&0\\g&G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_1\\U_2\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}I_\mathrm q\\0\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(8.1)

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_{21}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i)(G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L)\,I_\mathrm q}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-g\,I_\mathrm q}$, this becomes

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{I_\mathrm q}{G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{g\,I_\mathrm q}{(G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i)(G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L)},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{U_2}{U_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{g}{G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L}.}$$
(8.2)

In the model, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20}$ mV, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4}$ V and the voltage gain is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-200}$, as in Chapter 6. The minus arises because the controlled current flows out of node 2.

With negative feedback. The common lower point of input and output is called the foot point. If you separate it from ground and insert $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$ there, node 3 arises (Fig. 8). The output current now runs through $R_\mathrm S$ and raises $U_3$. This lowers the control voltage $U_1-U_3$, and the output current decreases. This action of the output back on its own input is called negative feedback.

The stamps of $G_\mathrm q$, $G_\mathrm i$, $G_\mathrm S$, $G_\mathrm o$, $G_\mathrm L$ and $g\,(U_1-U_3)$ from 2 to 3 give

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i&0&-G_\mathrm i\\g&G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L&-g-G_\mathrm o\\-g-G_\mathrm i&-G_\mathrm o&G_\mathrm S+g+G_\mathrm i+G_\mathrm o\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}U_1\\U_2\\U_3\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}I_\mathrm q\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(8.3)
Derivation: The determinants

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{B}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L}$, expansion along the first row gives

$$\begin{aligned} D&=A\,[B\,(G_\mathrm S+G_\mathrm i)+G_\mathrm L\,(g+G_\mathrm o)]-G_\mathrm i\,[B\,G_\mathrm i+G_\mathrm L\,g]\\ &=B\,[A\,G_\mathrm S+G_\mathrm qG_\mathrm i]+G_\mathrm L\,[A\,G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm q\,g]. \end{aligned}$$

$D_2$ is expanded along column 2, in which only $I_\mathrm q$ stands: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-I_\mathrm q\,[g\,(G_\mathrm S+g+G_\mathrm i+G_\mathrm o)-(g+G_\mathrm o)(g+G_\mathrm i)]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(G_\mathrm iG_\mathrm o-g\,G_\mathrm S)\,I_\mathrm q}$.

This gives the transimpedance, the output voltage per input current,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{U_2}{I_\mathrm q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{G_\mathrm iG_\mathrm o-g\,G_\mathrm S}{(G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L)\,[(G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i)\,G_\mathrm S+G_\mathrm qG_\mathrm i]+G_\mathrm L\,[(G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i)\,G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm q\,g]}.}$$
(8.4)

In the model with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_\mathrm S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ mS, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5.3225}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D_2/I_\mathrm q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-49.975}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2/I_\mathrm q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-9.39\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$. From 20 µ A come $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{39.0}$ mV, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-188}$ mV and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4.81}$. Limiting case: For $G_\mathrm S\to\infty$, only the terms with $G_\mathrm S$ remain, and (8.4) turns into $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-g/[(G_\mathrm q+G_\mathrm i)(G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L)]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-200\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$, the result from (8.2). The foot point is then at ground again.

Set RS to 1u: then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathtt{u2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathtt{au}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-200}$. Check your result from Exercise 10 with V(3).

QuestionWhy would you reduce the gain voluntarily?

Because it then hardly depends on the transconductance any more, and $g$ varies widely in transistors. If you double $g$ in the model, $A_u$ only goes from $-4.81$ to $-4.90$. Without $R_\mathrm S$, by contrast, $-200$ would become $-400$. The result lies close to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-R_\mathrm L/R_\mathrm S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-5}$, that is, it is set by two precise resistors. Chapter 11 uses this for the operational amplifier.

Exercise 10 ★★★ The input resistance rises

Consider the amplifier from Fig. 8 with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G_\mathrm S}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ mS.

  1. Show $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{[(G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L)(G_\mathrm S+G_\mathrm i)+G_\mathrm L\,(g+G_\mathrm o)]\,I_\mathrm q}$ and calculate $U_1/I_\mathrm q$.

  2. The input conductance without $G_\mathrm q$ is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm q/U_1-G_\mathrm q}$. How large is the input resistance $1/Y_\mathrm{in}$? Compare with 2 k$\Omega$ without $R_\mathrm S$.

  3. Explain the mechanism: How large is $U_3$, and what current therefore still flows through $G_\mathrm i$?

Solution

(a) Replacing column 1 by $(I_\mathrm q,0,0)$ leaves $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm q\,[(G_\mathrm o+G_\mathrm L)(G_\mathrm S+g+G_\mathrm i+G_\mathrm o)-(g+G_\mathrm o)\,G_\mathrm o]}$. Multiplied out, the bracket is the one given. Numbers: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.25\cdot1.5+0.2\cdot50.05}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10.385}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5.3225}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1/I_\mathrm q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.95\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Y_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/(1.951\,\mathrm{k\Omega})-0.5\,\mathrm{mS}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0125}$ mS, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{in}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{79.9\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$, forty times as much.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{38.05}$ mV lies only 0.98 mV below $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{39.02}$ mV. The potential at the other end of $R_\mathrm i$ almost follows along. That is why just under 1 mV is across $R_\mathrm i$, and instead of 10 µ A, 0.49 µ A flow. Little current at the same voltage means a large input resistance.
