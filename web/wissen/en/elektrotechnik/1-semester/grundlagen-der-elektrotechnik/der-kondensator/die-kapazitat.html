What this stage is about:

How much charge does a given voltage hold on the plates? The answer is a single number for each component.

The idea. If you double the charge on both plates, each charge contributes its field twice. By the superposition from Chapter 1, the field doubles at every location, and its shape stays the same (Fig. 2 left). The voltage is the integral of the field from plate to plate, so it doubles too. $Q$ and $U$ are therefore proportional. Their ratio does not depend on how much charge happens to be there, but only on shape, separation and material.

Definition 2.1 (Capacitance)

The capacitance of a capacitor is the ratio of charge to voltage:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{Q}{U},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{C\,U,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{[C]}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{A\,s}}{\mathrm V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm F\ \text{(farad)}.}$$
(2.1)

⁠How to read it: $Q$ is the charge of the positive plate in C (coulombs), $U$ the voltage between the plates in V, $C$ the capacitance in F. At 1 F, one volt holds one coulomb. That is a great deal. Usual values are in the fF, pF, nF and µF ranges; electrolytic capacitors reach into the mF range. Do not confuse the symbols: italic $C$ is the capacitance, upright C is the coulomb. Validity: (2.1) assumes that the material between the plates does not change with the field. Ceramics with very large $\varepsilon_\mathrm{r}$ (Stage 4) noticeably lose capacitance at high voltage.

Example

The pair of plates has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{29.5}$ pF (Stage 4). At 9 V it carries $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{29.5\,\mathrm{pF}\cdot9\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{266}$ pC, which is $1.66\cdot10^{9}$ electrons. A 1 mm thick copper plate of this area contains $8.5\cdot10^{23}$ free electrons. So only one in $5\cdot10^{14}$ moves, and these sit on the inner side of the plate. The metal stays practically unchanged. The capacitor of the RC circuit carries $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{900\,\mu\text{C}}$ at 9 V, 3.4 million times more.

⁠Limiting case: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: without voltage nothing is separated. For the same charge, the smaller capacitor shows the larger voltage.

QuestionCapacitance sounds like holding capacity. Does a capacitor ever get full?

Not in the sense of a bucket. More voltage always brings more charge, and $C$ stays the same. The limit is set by the dielectric: above a certain field strength it breaks down. In this chapter, full only means: the voltage has reached that of the source.

Exercise 2 ★ Charge and voltage

  1. A 470 nF capacitor is connected to 12 V. How large is its charge?

  2. At what voltage does it carry 1 µC?

  3. The voltage is halved. What happens to $Q$ and to $C$?

  4. Two capacitors of 10 µF and 1 µF each carry 50 µC. What voltages are across them?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{470\,\mathrm{nF}\cdot12\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5.64\,\mu\text{C}}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q/C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\mu\text{C}/470\,\mathrm{nF}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.13}$ V.

(c) $Q$ halves to 2.82 µC. $C$ stays the same, because it depends only on the component.

(d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{Q/C}$: 5 V across the 10 µF capacitor, 50 V across the 1 µF capacitor. The same charge produces the larger voltage on the smaller one.
