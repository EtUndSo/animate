What this is about#

Two metal plates with an insulator between them: this component is called a capacitor. If you connect it to a battery, current flows in the leads even though the plates do not touch. The current grows weaker and stops by itself. If you disconnect the battery, the voltage remains, and the component can drive current on its own. The question of this chapter is: How can current flow into an insulator, why does it end by itself, and where is the energy afterward?

Each plate is an electrode; the insulator between them is called the dielectric. The quantity that characterizes the component is the capacitance: it says how much charge sits on the plates per volt. The basic form is two flat plates, the parallel-plate capacitor.

The field is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ kV/m.

  • ⁠RC circuit: An RC circuit is the series connection of a resistor and a capacitor across a source. Here a 9 V battery ($\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$ V) charges a capacitor $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100\,\mu\text{F}}$ through $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$. This gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tau}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{RC}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ s. The internal resistance of the battery, around 2 $\Omega$, is neglected: it is 5000 times smaller than $R$.

  • ⁠Two capacitors: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100\,\mu\text{F}}$ (the same one as in the RC circuit) and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{C_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{50\,\mu\text{F}}$.
