What this stage is about:

When there are several sources at different places in the network, groups alone do not help. The superposition method splits the problem into one calculation per source.

The idea. A network is determined by three kinds of equations: node rule, mesh rule, Ohm's law. None of them contains a product or a square of currents and voltages, as long as $R$ is fixed. Such equations are linear. If such a system has the solution $x'$ for one source and the solution $x''$ for another, then $x'+x''$ is the solution when both sources act at the same time. Substitution shows this: every equation adds term by term. So each source drives its own current through the network as if the other were not there, and the currents add in every branch.

Theorem 8.1 (Superposition)

In a linear network with $n$ independent voltage sources and $m$ independent current sources, every branch voltage and every branch current satisfies

$$\begin{aligned} U_\mathrm{AB}&=a_1U_\mathrm{q1}+\dots+a_nU_{\mathrm{q}n}+Z_1I_\mathrm{q1}+\dots+Z_mI_{\mathrm{q}m},\\ I_\mathrm{CD}&=Y_1U_\mathrm{q1}+\dots+Y_nU_{\mathrm{q}n}+b_1I_\mathrm{q1}+\dots+b_mI_{\mathrm{q}m}. \end{aligned}$$
(8.1)(8.2)

The coefficients depend only on the resistors, not on the sources.

⁠How to read it: A source is independent if its value depends on no quantity in the network. $a_k$ is the voltage gain from source $k$ to the voltage $U_\mathrm{AB}$, without a unit: this is how many volts $U_\mathrm{AB}$ changes per volt of the source. It is also called the sensitivity. $Z_k$ in $\Omega$ is the transfer resistance (the transimpedance) from a current source to this voltage. Correspondingly, $Y_k$ in S and the current gain $b_k$ describe the current between two nodes C and D. $U_\mathrm{AB}$ and $I_\mathrm{CD}$ stand for any voltage and any current in the network. Validity: only for linear networks and only for voltages and currents.

The procedure.

  1. Turn off all sources except one. An ideal voltage source with the value zero forces equal potentials at its terminals, which is what a wire does: it becomes a short circuit. An ideal current source with the value zero forces the current zero, which is what a gap does: it becomes open terminals. Internal resistances of real sources stay in the network.

  2. Calculate the partial effect of this one source. Usually only a voltage or current divider remains.

  3. Repeat for every source and add all the partial effects with the correct signs.

Example

We want $U_\mathrm{AB}$ in N with the terminals open (Fig. 9). Partial circuit 1: voltage divider, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\,U_\mathrm{q1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13\cdot18\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ V. Partial circuit 2: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U''}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(R_1\parallel R_2)\,I_\mathrm{q2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\Omega\cdot1.5\,\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ V. Sum: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$ V. The coefficients are therefore $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13}$ for $U_\mathrm{q1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\Omega}$ for $I_\mathrm{q2}$. Check with the node rule at A: through $R_1$ come $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(18-9)\,\mathrm{V}/6\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5}$ A, plus 1.5 A from the current source, and through $R_2$ go off $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{9\,\mathrm{V}/3\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ A. The branch currents superpose too: in $R_1$ flow $2$ A from partial circuit 1 and $-0.5$ A from partial circuit 2.

⁠Limiting case: If you double both sources, $U_\mathrm{AB}$ doubles to 18 V, because the coefficients stay the same.

Derivation: The coefficients of N

With the terminals open, the node rule at A reads $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(U_\mathrm{q1}-U_\mathrm{AB})/R_1+I_\mathrm{q2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{AB}/R_2}$. Solved for the voltage we want:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\,U_\mathrm{q1}+\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}\,I_\mathrm{q2}.}$$

This is the form (8.1): each term belongs to one source, and the coefficients contain only resistors.

The limit: power. The power in a resistor is quadratic in the voltage, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{UI}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U^2/R}$ (Chapter 7). In $R_2$, adding would give $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(6\,\mathrm{V})^2/3\,\Omega+(3\,\mathrm{V})^2/3\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{15}$ W. The truth is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(9\,\mathrm{V})^2/3\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{27}$ W. The cross term $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2U'U''/R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12}$ W is missing. Powers must not be superposed; voltages and currents may. An exception comes later: for alternating quantities of different frequencies, the cross term vanishes in the time average. Nor does superposition hold once a diode sits in the network (Chapter 9).

QuestionWhy must I not take a turned-off voltage source out of the network?

Because zero volts does not turn into “nothing”. The source remains part of the line; it just no longer holds a voltage. If you take it out, you interrupt the current path through it. That would be the case of the current source, not of the voltage source.

Exercise 9 ★★ Two sources, one load

From node C, three branches lead to the common ground: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\,\Omega}$ directly, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\Omega}$ in series with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12}$ V, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\,\Omega}$ in series with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ V (both plus terminals point toward C). (a) Calculate the voltage $U_3$ across $R_3$ by superposition and generally as a formula. Check with the node rule. (b) Compare the true power in $R_3$ with the sum of the partial powers. (c) What is the sensitivity of $U_3$ to $U_\mathrm{t}$? What does this give for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8}$ V, without recalculating everything?

Solution

(a) $U_\mathrm{t}$ turned off: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{t}\parallel R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\,\Omega}$, divider $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_3'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2}{2+2}\cdot12\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ V. $U_\mathrm{s}$ turned off: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{s}\parallel R_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac43\,\Omega}$, divider $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_3''}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{4/3}{4+4/3}\cdot6\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5}$ V. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{7.5}$ V. In general:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_\mathrm{t}R_3}{R_\mathrm{s}R_\mathrm{t}+R_3(R_\mathrm{s}+R_\mathrm{t})}\,U_\mathrm{s}+\frac{R_\mathrm{s}R_3}{R_\mathrm{s}R_\mathrm{t}+R_3(R_\mathrm{s}+R_\mathrm{t})}\,U_\mathrm{t}.}$$

Node rule: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{12-7.5}{2}\,\mathrm{A}+\frac{6-7.5}{4}\,\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.25\,\mathrm{A}-0.375\,\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.875}$ A, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{7.5\,\mathrm{V}/4\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.875}$ A. The 6 V source absorbs current.

(b) Partial powers: 9 W and 0.5625 W, sum 9.56 W. True: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(7.5\,\mathrm{V})^2/4\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{14.06}$ W.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_\mathrm{t}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1.5\,\mathrm{V}}{6\,\mathrm{V}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac14}$. For 8 V: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6\,\mathrm{V}+\frac14\cdot8\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8}$ V.
