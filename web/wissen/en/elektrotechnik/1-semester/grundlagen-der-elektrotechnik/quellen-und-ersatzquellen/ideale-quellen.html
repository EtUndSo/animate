Stage 4: Ideal sources

DEENES中文
What this stage is about:

What has been missing so far is whatever drives the current. This stage introduces the two basic building blocks that bring energy into a network.

The idea. A source holds a single quantity fixed. The connected network determines the other. An ideal voltage source holds the potential difference between its terminals. The load decides what current flows. An ideal current source holds the current in its branch. The load likewise decides what voltage arises. Both are models. They cannot be built, but a regulated power supply or a transistor circuit (Part 10) comes close to them within a limited range.

Definition 4.1 (Ideal sources)

For the ideal voltage source and the ideal current source,

$$\textcolor{#123d29}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}\quad\text{for every current},}$$
$$\textcolor{#123d29}{I_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{q}\quad\text{for every voltage}.}$$
(4.1)

⁠How to read it: $U_\mathrm{q}$ is the source voltage in V, from millivolts to kilovolts, here 9 V. $I_\mathrm{q}$ is the source current in A, from microamperes to amperes. In Fig. 5 the slope of the characteristic curve is the internal resistance with a negative sign: zero for the voltage source, infinite for the current source. In calculations, a voltage source replaces the difference of two node potentials by a known value, and a current source fixes the current of a branch.

⁠Validity: Two connections contradict the model. A short-circuited voltage source would have to satisfy both $\textcolor{#123d29}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U_\mathrm{q}}$ and $\textcolor{#123d29}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. An open current source would have to satisfy both $\textcolor{#123d29}{I_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I_\mathrm{q}}$ and $\textcolor{#123d29}{I_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, because a current needs a closed path.

Example. The 9 V source delivers 0.5625 A into 16 $\Omega$, 9 A into 1 $\Omega$, and it would be 9000 A into 1 m$\Omega$. A 0.5 A source produces 8 V across 16 $\Omega$, 500 V across 1 k$\Omega$, and it would be 500 kV across 1 M$\Omega$. Limiting case: The more extreme the load, the further the model departs from any real device. That is the reason for the next stage.

QuestionCan the current also flow backward through a source?

Yes. An ideal source fixes only its one quantity. If the current flows into the plus terminal of a voltage source, then $I_\mathrm{A}<0$, and the source absorbs power. That is how a rechargeable battery is charged.

Exercise 5 ★ What changes?

An ideal current source of 2 mA feeds 1 k$\Omega$. (a) What is the voltage, and what is it with 4.7 k$\Omega$? (b) A second resistor of 1 k$\Omega$ is added in series with the source. What changes at the first one, what at the source? (c) In parallel with an ideal 5 V source feeding 1 k$\Omega$, 10 $\Omega$ is added. What changes at the load, what at the source?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#123d29}{2\,\mathrm{mA}\cdot1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ V, with 4.7 k$\Omega$ it is 9.4 V. The current stays.

(b) Nothing changes at the first resistor: the current is fixed, the voltage stays 2 V. The source must produce 4 V. In series with an ideal current source, a resistor is invisible from outside.

(c) Nothing changes at the load; it stays at 5 V and 5 mA. The source delivers 505 mA instead of 5 mA. In parallel with an ideal voltage source, a resistor is invisible from outside.
