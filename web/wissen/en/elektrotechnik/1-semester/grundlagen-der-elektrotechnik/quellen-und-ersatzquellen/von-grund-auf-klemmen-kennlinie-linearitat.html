From the ground up: Terminals, characteristic curve, linearity

A terminal is a connection point to the outside. A one-port is a network with exactly two terminals that form a pair: the current flowing out at A comes back in at B, because charge is not lost (node rule, Chapter 2). A one-port therefore has only one current and one voltage. It is a two-terminal element from Chapter 2, even if it contains many components inside. The characteristic curve is the relationship between these two quantities. In this chapter it always appears in the same diagram: terminal current horizontal, terminal voltage vertical.

The reference arrows apply throughout the chapter: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_\mathrm{A}-\varphi_\mathrm{B}}$ counts from A to B, $I_\mathrm{A}$ counts out of terminal A. For the connected load both arrows point in the same direction (passive sign convention, Chapter 2), for the one-port itself in opposite directions (active sign convention). If both are positive, the one-port delivers power. Open circuit means: the terminals are open, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I_\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Short circuit means: the terminals are connected by a wire, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{AB}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

A component is linear if its current is proportional to its voltage. A resistor is linear as long as $R$ does not depend on the current: its characteristic curve is a straight line through the origin. An incandescent lamp or a diode (Chapter 9) is not. The symbol $\parallel$ stands for the resistance of a parallel connection, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1\parallel R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}}$ (Chapter 2). It binds more tightly than the plus sign, as multiplication does.
