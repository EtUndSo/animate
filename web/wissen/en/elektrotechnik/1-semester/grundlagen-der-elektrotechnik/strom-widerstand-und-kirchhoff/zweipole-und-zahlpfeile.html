Stage 5: Two-terminal elements and reference arrows

What this stage is about:

From now on, what counts is no longer what happens inside the component, but what can be measured at its terminals. That requires a term for the component and an agreement about signs.

The idea. For calculating, it is enough to know how a component responds to voltage with current. The inside may be unknown. Signs are fixed beforehand with arrows, because the true directions are often not known before the calculation. The result then says whether reality follows the arrow.

Definition 5.1 (Two-terminal element, black box, network)

A two-terminal element is a component that can be fully described by the current and voltage between its two terminals. In the steady state (time-independent), that is its characteristic curve $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(U)}$. A black box is a system whose outward behavior is known but whose construction is unknown or unimportant. A network is an interconnection of two-terminal elements. If the characteristic curve is a straight line through the origin,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{G}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac IU}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{const}\quad\text{or}\quad R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac UI}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\text{const}.}$$
(5.1)

⁠How to read it: Such a linear two-terminal element is the ohmic resistor (Fig. 4 right, each straight line one value). Batteries and voltage sources also belong to the building blocks of linear networks, but their characteristic curve does not pass through the origin (Chapter 5). For a nonlinear two-terminal element such as the incandescent lamp (Fig. 5 right), $U/I$ depends on the operating point, and (5.1) does not hold there.

Definition 5.2 (Sign conventions)

The voltage arrow points from 1 to 2 and means $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_{12}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_1-\varphi_2}$ (Chapter 1). The current arrow counts positive charge in the direction of the arrow. In the passive sign convention (PSC, load convention), the voltage and current arrows at a two-terminal element point in the same direction; in the active sign convention (ASC, generator convention), they point in opposite directions. For a resistor,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I\quad\text{(PSC)},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-R\,I\quad\text{(ASC)}.}$$
(5.2)

⁠How to read it: Both conventions are only agreements. The direction of both arrows can be chosen freely; that changes signs, never the physics. The usual choice is the PSC at a load and the ASC at a source, because then all numbers are positive in normal operation (Fig. 6 left). Why this fits: In a load, positive charge runs from high to low potential and gives up energy; current and voltage have the same direction. In a source, it is lifted from low to high potential; current and voltage have opposite directions. In the PSC, $U\,I>0$ therefore means that energy is absorbed; in the ASC, it means that energy is delivered.

The conventional current direction is the direction in which positive charge would move: outside the source from plus to minus. It corresponds to the current arrow at a load in the PSC. The electrons move the opposite way.

Example

9 V across $3\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ give $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ mA. If the voltage arrow points from bottom to top, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\varphi_\text{bottom}-\varphi_\text{top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-9}$ V, and (5.2) reads $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-9\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\cdot3\,\mathrm{mA}}$. Check: Reversing both arrows turns $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I}$ into $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,(-I)}$, which is the same equation again.

QuestionElectrons move from minus to plus. Why is it calculated the other way round?

The direction was fixed before electrons were known, and it does no harm: in $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q\,\vec v}$, both factors change sign for electrons. Electrons moving left give the same current density as positive charge moving right. Voltage drop, heat and magnetic field depend only on $\vec j$.

Exercise 5 ★ Interpreting signs

  1. A black box in the PSC shows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}$ mA. Does it absorb or deliver energy, and how much per second?

  2. A car battery in the ASC shows $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{14}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-10}$ A. What is happening to it?

  3. In a wire, electrons move to the right; the magnitude of the current is 2 mA. The current arrow points to the right. How large is $I$?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-10}$ mW in the PSC: the box delivers 10 mW; it works as a source. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-140}$ W in the ASC: it does not deliver, but absorbs 140 W. It is being charged. (c) The current in the sense of positive charge runs to the left, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}$ mA.
