What this stage is about:

Chapter 1 considered charges at rest or flying individually. In a wire, countless charges move together. This stage gives this motion two measures.

The idea. Stand at one spot of the conductor and measure how much charge passes per second. That depends on three things: how densely the charge sits, how fast it moves and how large the cross section is. In the time $\Delta t$, exactly the charge that was at most the distance $v\,\Delta t$ away beforehand passes through the area (Fig. 1).

Definition 1.1 (Current and current density)

The charge density $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Delta Q/\Delta V}$ is the charge per volume. The electric current (current for short) is the charge that passes through an area per time. The current density is the current per area:

$$\begin{aligned} i&=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}=\frac{\mathrm{d} Q}{\mathrm{d} t},\qquad I=q\,v\,A,\qquad [I]=\mathrm{C/s}=\mathrm A,\\ j&=\frac IA=q\,v,\qquad\vec j=q\,\vec v. \end{aligned}$$
(1.1)(1.2)

⁠How to read it: $i$ is the time-dependent current, $I$ the constant one, in amperes: 3 mA in the reference model, 20 mA in a light-emitting diode, 16 A in a household line. $q$ has the unit $\mathrm{C/m^3}$. For the free electrons in copper, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-n\,e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1.36\cdot10^{10}\,\mathrm{C/m^3}}$. $j$ has the unit $\mathrm{A/m^2}$; household lines carry around $10\,\mathrm{A/mm^2}$. The vector $\vec j$ points in the direction of $\vec v$ for $q>0$ and opposite to it for $q<0$. Validity: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q\,v\,A}$ assumes that $q$ and $v$ are the same across $A$ and that $v$ is perpendicular to $A$.

Derivation: Current from density and velocity

Let the area be $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Delta x\,\Delta y}$. The shaded section has the volume $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v\,\Delta t}$. It contains the charge $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q\,\Delta V}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q\,A\,v\,\Delta t}$, which passes through $A$ in the time $\Delta t$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta Q/\Delta t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q\,v\,A}$.

When does current flow continuously? Two conditions must be met. First, the circuit needs a source, such as a battery or a power supply. It supplies energy, not charge: the charge carriers are already in the conductor; the source only drives them around the circuit. Second, the circuit must be closed, that is, a path that returns to itself. Otherwise charge piles up at a gap, its field cancels the driving field, and the current ends. A current that is constant in time in a closed circuit is called steady.

Example

In the reference model, 3 mC per second pass through the wire, which is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3\,\mathrm{mC}/e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.87\cdot10^{16}}$ electrons. The current density is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\,\mathrm{mA}/0.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6\,\mathrm{mA/mm^2}}$. One meter of wire contains the free charge $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n\,e\,A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6800}$ C. So the entire charge that a rectangular battery moves in its lifetime (around 1800 C) already sits in 26 cm of this wire. Check: For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, no matter how much charge sits in the wire. Twice the area at the same $v$ gives twice the current, because that is two wires side by side.

QuestionAre the electrons used up in the load?

No. Charge is conserved; as many electrons per second come out of a component as go in. What is used up is energy: each coulomb gives its energy per charge, the voltage, to the component and moves on at a lower potential.

Exercise 1 ★ Current, charge, current density

  1. A light-emitting diode carries 20 mA for 5 min. How much charge passes through it, and how many electrons is that?

  2. A cable with a cross section of $1.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ carries 16 A. How large is $j$ in $\mathrm{A/mm^2}$?

  3. In which direction does $\vec j$ point in a wire in which electrons move to the right?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{I\,t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{20\,\mathrm{mA}\cdot300\,\mathrm{s}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ C, which is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{6\,\mathrm{C}/e}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.7\cdot10^{19}}$ electrons. (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16\,\mathrm{A}/1.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10.7\,\mathrm{A/mm^2}}$. (c) To the left: for electrons, $q<0$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q\,\vec v}$ points against $\vec v$.
