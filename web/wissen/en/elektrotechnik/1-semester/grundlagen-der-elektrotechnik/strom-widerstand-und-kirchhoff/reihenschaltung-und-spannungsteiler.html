Stage 9: Series connection and voltage divider

What this stage is about:

Many networks are composed of two basic patterns. This stage combines resistors in series, shows how the voltage divides, and builds a converter from this.

The idea. Nothing branches off between the resistors; the node rule forces the same current in all of them. The midpoint settles at the potential at which that holds. The same current needs more drive in a larger resistor: the field does more work per charge there, and more voltage drops across it.

Theorem 9.1 (Series connection and voltage divider)

For $N$ resistors in a series connection,

$$\begin{aligned} I&=I_1=I_2=\dots=I_N,\qquad U=U_1+U_2+\dots+U_N,\qquad U_i=R_i\,I,\\ R_\mathrm{tot}&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_i,\qquad\frac{U_\nu}{U}=\frac{R_\nu}{\sum_{i=1}^NR_i}, \end{aligned}$$
(9.1)(9.2)

so for two resistors the voltage divider rule

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{U_2}{U}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{U_1}{U_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{R_1}{R_2}.}$$
(9.3)

⁠How to read it: $R_\mathrm{tot}$ is the total resistance, the one resistor that replaces the chain. The voltages are in the same ratio as the resistances. Validity: The divider rule holds only when unloaded: no current may be drawn at the tap, otherwise $I_1\neq I_2$ (Stage 10).

Derivation: Total resistance and divider rule

The mesh rule gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum U_i}$, Ohm's law gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_i\,I}$ with the same $I$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(R_1+\dots+R_N)\,I}$. In the quotient of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\nu}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_\nu\,I}$ and $U$, $I$ cancels.

Example

The chain has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{tot}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ mA, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$ V and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ V (Fig. 10 left). From (9.3): $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9\,\mathrm{V}\cdot1/3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ V.

⁠Limiting cases: $R_2\to0$ gives $U_2\to0$: a short circuit carries no voltage. $R_2\to\infty$ gives $U_2\to U$: an open point carries the entire voltage. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2}$ halves $U$.

Application: digital-to-analog converter. A digital-to-analog converter (D/A converter) turns a number into a voltage. Three bits (binary digits $b_0$, $b_1$, $b_2$) give the numbers $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ to 7. A chain of $R/2$, seven $R$ and $R/2$ has the total resistance $8R$ and divides $U_\mathrm{ref}$ into eight steps. The taps carry

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(Z+\frac12\Bigr)\frac{U_\mathrm{ref}}{8}.}$$
(9.4)

A tree of switches, one level per bit, lets exactly one tap through. A buffer, an amplifier with the factor 1, outputs the voltage without drawing current from the chain, so that the divider stays unloaded. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{ref}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.2}$ V the step is 0.4 V, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ gives 2.2 V.

Application: flash analog-to-digital converter. The same chain also works in reverse. A comparator, a device that reports which of two voltages is higher, compares the input voltage with every tap, all at the same time. A decoder, a logic circuit, turns the reports into the three-bit number. For the numbers 0 to 7, seven thresholds are enough, here 0.2 to 2.6 V. An eighth threshold can report an overflow. At 1.3 V, three thresholds lie below; the converter outputs $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, which is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3\cdot0.4\,\mathrm{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.2}$ V, the nearest multiple of the step.

QuestionDoesn't the first resistor slow the current down, so that less arrives at the second?

No, current is not used up (Stage 1). The electrons do not pile up in front of $R_2$: otherwise the midpoint would charge up until both currents are equal. $R_2$ acts back on the whole circuit, because the one common current is set by $R_1+R_2$.

Exercise 9 ★ Designing a divider

  1. 3.3 V are to be derived from 12 V, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.3\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$. Calculate $R_1$. Which E24 values (8.2 and 9.1 k$\Omega$) lie next to it, and what voltage do they give?

  2. A chain of $1$, $2$, $3$ and $6\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ is connected to 24 V. Give the current and the partial voltages.

  3. A 3-bit converter has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_\mathrm{ref}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ V. What voltage does it output at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{Z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R_2\,(12-3.3)/3.3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8.7\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$. With $8.2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ the result is 3.44 V ($+4.3\,\%$), with $9.1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ it is 3.19 V ($-3.2\,\%$). (b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R_\mathrm{tot}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ mA, partial voltages 2, 4, 6 and 12 V. (c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U_6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.5\cdot5\,\mathrm{V}/8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.06}$ V.
