Stage 3: Ohm's law from field and collisions

What this stage is about:

Stage 2 has shown that the drift is proportional to the field. In a few steps, this stage turns that into the most important law of circuit engineering.

The idea. More voltage means a stronger field in the conductor, a stronger field means faster drift, faster drift means more current. Each step is a proportionality, and so is the whole way from $U$ to $I$. The factor contains the material and the shape (Fig. 4).

Theorem 3.1 (Ohm's law)

For a conductor with the electron density $n$,

$$\begin{aligned} \vec j&=\sigma\,\vec E,\qquad\sigma=n\,e\,\mu,\qquad\rho=\frac1\sigma,\\ U&=R\,I,\qquad R=\frac{L}{\sigma\,A}=\rho\,\frac LA,\qquad G=\frac1R=\frac IU. \end{aligned}$$
(3.1)(3.2)

⁠How to read it: $\sigma$ is the conductivity in S/m, $\rho$ the resistivity in $\Omega\,\mathrm m$. It is often given in $\Omega\,\mathrm{mm^2/m}$; copper then has 0.017. $R$ is the resistance in $\Omega$ (ohms), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\Omega}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ V/A: m$\Omega$ in a wire, k$\Omega$ in circuits. Its circuit symbol is a rectangle (Fig. 6). $G$ is the conductance in S (siemens), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1\,\mathrm{S}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\,\Omega^{-1}}$. The units bear the names of Georg Simon Ohm and Werner von Siemens. Validity: The conductor must be linear ($\vec j$ proportional to $\vec E$) and homogeneous (the same material everywhere). The temperature must be constant (Stage 4), and $L\gg\sqrt A$ must hold so that the field at the ends plays no role. Linearity holds very precisely in metals: the flight time $T$ does not depend on $E$, because the drift is more than $10^{9}$ times smaller than the random motion.

Derivation: From field to component

Current density of the electrons, Stage 1, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{q}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-n\,e}$ and (2.1):

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\vec j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{q\,\vec v_\mathrm D}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-n\,e)(-\mu\,\vec E)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n\,e\,\mu\,\vec E}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma\,\vec E.}$$

The field is uniform, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{E\,L}$ (Chapter 1). Thus

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{j\,A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma\,E\,A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\sigma\,A}{L}\,U\quad\Longrightarrow\quad U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{L}{\sigma\,A}\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I.}$$

Example

The wire has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\rho\,L/A}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{34\,\mathrm{m\Omega}}$. At 3 mA, 0.10 mV drops across it, which is $1.1\cdot10^{-5}$ of the 9 V. This justifies why circuit diagrams draw wires as ideal, without voltage along the wire. The same calculation shows why resistors are not made of copper: $2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ would need 1.2 km of copper at a cross section of $0.01\,\mathrm{mm^2}$, but only 40 m of constantan.

⁠Limiting cases: Twice the length at the same voltage halves the field and thus the current. Twice the cross section acts like two wires side by side; the current doubles. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{U}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Where the energy goes. At each collision, the electron gives the kinetic energy gained in the field to the lattice. The lattice vibrates more strongly, and that is heat. Each coulomb gives up the energy $U$ in joules, and $I$ coulombs pass per second. The power, the energy per time, is therefore $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{P}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U\,I}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{R\,I^2}$. The chain converts $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{9\,\mathrm{V}\cdot3\,\mathrm{mA}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{27}$ mW. Chapter 7 treats power in more detail.

QuestionYou can always calculate $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{U/I}$. So what does Ohm's law still say?

The quotient $U/I$ exists for every component. The law says more: it is constant, independent of $U$. That is a statement about the material and follows from the fixed flight time. It does not hold for an incandescent lamp (Stage 4).

Exercise 3 ★★ Resistance from geometry

A supply line consists of an outgoing and a return conductor, each 20 m of copper with a cross section of $1.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}$.

  1. Calculate its resistance and the voltage that drops across it at 16 A.

  2. What cross section would aluminum need for the same resistance?

  3. Prediction: A wire is drawn to twice its length while its volume stays the same. By what factor does $R$ change?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\rho\cdot40\,\mathrm{m}/1.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.453\,\Omega}$. The drop is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.453\,\Omega\cdot16\,\mathrm{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{7.3}$ V, 3.2 % of 230 V. (b) The same $R$ means the same $\rho/A$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{A_\mathrm{Al}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}\cdot2.8/1.7}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}}$. (c) Volume $L\,A$ constant: $L\to2L$ forces $A\to A/2$. So $R\to4R$.
