What this stage is about:

In every step, bisection asks only for the sign of $f$ and thereby gains one bit. This section shows a method that also uses the slope of $f$ and thereby roughly doubles the number of significant digits in every step. It is Heron's method from Chapter 1, now with the reason why it is so fast and when it fails.

What you need for thisderivative and tangent line

The derivative $f'(g)$ of a function at the point $g$ is the slope of its graph there. The tangent line at the point $g$ is the straight line through the point $(g, f(g))$ with this slope:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(g)+f'(g)\,(x-g).}$$

Near $g$, the graph hardly differs from its tangent line. For the square root, you need only one derivative from school: for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-a}$ with a fixed number $a$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x}$.

Example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-2}$ at the point $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, so the tangent line is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2+4(x-2)}$. It crosses the $x$-axis where $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2-2/4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5}$.

⁠In short: The tangent line is the straight line that hugs the graph at one point; its slope is the derivative.

Newton's method#

The zero of a curve is hard to find, whereas the zero of a straight line is easy to find. Newton's method therefore replaces the curve by its tangent line in every step.

Following the tangent. You stand on the graph above an estimate $g$, draw the tangent line and follow it down to the $x$-axis. The crossing point is the new estimate. If the estimate is already close to the zero, the tangent line hugs the curve closely all the way there, and you land very close to the true zero (Fig. 9).

Definition 8.1 (Newton's method)

Newton's method for a zero of a differentiable function $f$ starts with a starting value $g_0$ and from it computes one after another

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g_n-\frac{f(g_n)}{f'(g_n)},}$$

as long as $|f(g_n)|$ is greater than a tolerance $\varepsilon$. $g_{n+1}$ is the zero of the tangent line at the point $g_n$; for this, $f'(g_n)\ne0$ must hold.

The formula follows directly from the tangent line: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(g_n)+f'(g_n)(x-g_n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g_n-f(g_n)/f'(g_n)}$.

Example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-2}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5}$ as in subsection 8.1. In the next step, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(1.5)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.25}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(1.5)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.5-0.25/3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.41\overline6}$.

⁠In short: In every step, Newton's method jumps to the zero of the tangent line.

The square root by Newton's method#

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(g)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g^2-a}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(g)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2g}$, and the Newton step is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g_n-\frac{g_n^2-a}{2g_n}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{2g_n^2-g_n^2+a}{2g_n}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\Bigl(g_n+\frac{a}{g_n}\Bigr).}$$

This is exactly Heron's method from Chapter 1, Sections 1.1 and 4.5: the old recipe for the square root is Newton's method for $x^2-a$. As a script with a loop that stops as soon as $|g^2-a|\le10^{-12}$:

wurzel_newton.pyPython
# Square root with Newton's method: g -> g - f(g)/f'(g) with f(g) = g*g - a and f'(g) = 2*g
a = float(input("a = "))
eps = 1e-12                               # tolerance for |g*g - a|
g = max(1.0, a)                           # starting value
schritte = 0
while abs(g * g - a) > eps:
    g = g - (g * g - a) / (2 * g)
    schritte = schritte + 1
    print(f"Schritt {schritte}: g = {g}")
print("Näherung:", g, "nach", schritte, "Schritten")

⁠In short: For the square root, the Newton step is Heron's recipe $\frac12(g+a/g)$, and it needs five steps for $\sqrt2$.

BackgroundWhy Heron's recipe from Chapter 1 landed one step off

In Chapter 1, Section 4.5, the recipe in the form (g + 2/g) / 2 ended at 1.414213562373095, one smallest step away from 2 ** 0.5. The script here calculates with the form g - (g*g - 2)/(2*g) and stops after step 5 at 1.4142135623730951. Both forms are mathematically equal, but they round differently, just as ten times + 0.1 and * 10 do in Chapter 2, Section 5.1. Very close to the target, something else comes into play with the second form: in g*g - 2, two almost equal numbers are subtracted from each other, and the result consists almost entirely of rounding errors, as with cancellation in Chapter 2, Section 5.4. If you let this form keep running, $g$ would therefore jump back and forth between 1.4142135623730951 and 1.414213562373095 without end; when the script stopped, it happened to catch the better of the two. The Heron form, by contrast, stays at 1.414213562373095. For $\sqrt3$, both forms hit the best value with the script's stopping rule. In a test with 100 000 numbers between 0.01 and 1000, both forms were never more than one step away from the best float after many steps; the Newton form oscillated between two neighbors in a good quarter of the cases. A method that itself calculates with rounded numbers can hardly promise more, and tolerances such as $10^{-12}$ are much coarser than one step anyway.

The speed of convergence#

That the number of correct digits roughly doubles in every step is no coincidence of the number 2. For the square root, you can calculate it exactly.

Theorem 8.2 (Error of the Newton step for the square root)

Let $a>0$, $g_n>0$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12(g_n+a/g_n)}$. The errors $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g_n-\sqrt a}$ satisfy

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{d_n^2}{2g_n}.}$$

Proof: Direct calculation

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{g_n^2+a}{2g_n}-\sqrt a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{g_n^2-2\sqrt a\,g_n+a}{2g_n}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{(g_n-\sqrt a)^2}{2g_n}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac{d_n^2}{2g_n}}$.

Two consequences. First, $d_{n+1}\ge0$: after the first step, every estimate lies above $\sqrt a$ or on it. Second, the error is squared in every step, apart from the factor $1/(2g_n)$, which is about $1/(2\sqrt a)$ near the solution. For the relative error $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\delta_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_n/\sqrt a}$, this together with $g_n\ge\sqrt a$ gives the estimate $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\delta_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\delta_n^2\sqrt a/(2g_n)\le\delta_n^2/2}$. If $\delta_n$ is about $10^{-p}$, so that $g_n$ has about $p$ significant decimal digits, then $\delta_{n+1}$ is at most about $10^{-2p}/2$: $p$ significant digits become about $2p$. This property is called quadratic convergence.

BackgroundWhy Newton doubles the digits

The theorem does not hold only for the square root. Taylor's formula with remainder, applied to a twice differentiable function $f$ at the point $g$, gives for a zero $x^*$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(x^*)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(g)+f'(g)\,(x^*-g)+\tfrac12f''(\xi)\,(x^*-g)^2}$$

with a number $\xi$ between $g$ and $x^*$. The tangent line leaves out the last term; so its error is quadratic in the distance. Dividing by $f'(g)$ and substituting the Newton step $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g-f(g)/f'(g)}$ leaves

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g'-x^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{f''(\xi)}{2f'(g)}\,(g-x^*)^2 .}$$

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2-a}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f''}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(g)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2g}$, and you get exactly Theorem 8.2. The mechanism: the method replaces the function by a straight line, and a smooth function differs from its tangent line only by an error that grows with the square of the distance. Bisection uses only the sign of $f$, one bit per step. Newton uses the value and the slope, that is, much more information about the location of the zero, and therefore gains more. This holds as long as $f'(x^*)\ne0$ and you start close enough to the zero; the next subsection shows what happens otherwise.

The limits of Newton's method#

With a sign change, bisection always finds a zero. Newton's method is much faster, but it has no such guarantee.

The slope is zero. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(g)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, the tangent line is horizontal and does not cross the axis. For the square root, this happens at the starting value $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$:

A cycle. For some functions and starting values, the estimates jump back and forth without approaching the zero. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^3-2x+2}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3x^2-2}$ and the starting value 0, the method returns to the start after two steps. The only real zero lies at about $-1.77$, far away from both values:

# Newton for f(x) = x**3 - 2*x + 2 with starting value 0: six steps
x = 0.0
for schritt in range(1, 7):
    x = x - (x ** 3 - 2 * x + 2) / (3 * x ** 2 - 2)

Far away, it is slow. Quadratic convergence holds only near the zero. For $\sqrt{25}$ with the starting value 25, the error shrinks only by a small factor in the first steps, from 20 to 8 and then to about 2.5, before the digits begin to double:

For $g_n\gg\sqrt a$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac12(g_n+a/g_n)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ g_n/2}$: far above the square root, every step only halves the estimate and therefore also roughly the error, which there is almost as large as the estimate itself.

⁠In short: Newton needs a nonzero derivative, a starting value close to the solution and a suitable tolerance; then it is very fast, otherwise it can cycle, crawl or run forever.

The ready-made way: math.sqrt#

For the square root itself, in practice you do not write a loop but call math.sqrt (Chapter 2, Section 8.2). The following script puts the ways by hand and the ready-made way side by side, with the same tolerance:

wurzel_vergleich.pyPython
# Square root of 2 by hand (bisection, Newton) and ready-made (math.sqrt), same tolerance 1e-12
import math
a = 2.0
eps = 1e-12

lo = 0.0                                  # bisection
hi = max(1.0, a)
schritte_b = 0
while hi - lo > eps:
    mitte = (lo + hi) / 2
    if mitte * mitte < a:
        lo = mitte
    else:
        hi = mitte
    schritte_b = schritte_b + 1
bisektion = (lo + hi) / 2

g = max(1.0, a)                           # Newton
schritte_n = 0
while abs(g * g - a) > eps:
    g = g - (g * g - a) / (2 * g)
    schritte_n = schritte_n + 1

fertig = math.sqrt(a)                     # ready-made way
print(f"Bisektion:  {bisektion}  nach {schritte_b} Schritten, Abstand zu math.sqrt {abs(bisektion - fertig):.1e}")
print(f"Newton:     {g}  nach {schritte_n} Schritten, Abstand zu math.sqrt {abs(g - fertig):.1e}")
print(f"math.sqrt:  {fertig}")
print("Newton == math.sqrt:", g == fertig)

What math.sqrt does in addition: it is fast and accurate for every number, without your having to choose a starting value, a tolerance or an emergency brake, and for negative numbers it reports an error instead of letting a loop run forever.

⁠In short: math.sqrt delivers in one call the result that the methods by hand reach only approximately and with a carefully chosen tolerance.

BackgroundWhat math.sqrt does in the processor

The module math passes most functions on to the math library of the C language. For the square root, common PC processors (x86-64) have an instruction of their own, and the C library uses it. The IEEE 754 standard requires the same for the square root as for the four basic arithmetic operations: the result is the exact value, rounded to the nearest float (Chapter 2, Section 5.1). For $\sqrt2$, you can check this: 1.4142135623730951 lies $9.7\cdot10^{-17}$ above $\sqrt2$, and its neighbor 1.414213562373095 lies $1.25\cdot10^{-16}$ below it. How the instruction calculates internally is up to the processor. Depending on its design, it determines the square root digit by digit, similar to a calculation with pen and paper, or with a Newton-like iteration, in both cases with enough extra digits to be able to round correctly.

Exercises for Section 8#

Exercise 31 ★ Newton by hand

Calculate three Newton steps for $\sqrt3$ with the starting value $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ by hand, as a fraction and as a decimal number. Calculate the errors $d_0$ to $d_3$ and check Theorem 8.2 for $d_1$ and $d_2$. How many steps would bisection on $[1,2]$ need for the same accuracy as $g_3$?

Solution

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12(g_n+3/g_n)}$:

$$\begin{aligned} g_1&=\tfrac12\bigl(2+\tfrac32\bigr)=\tfrac74=1.75,\\ g_2&=\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac74+\tfrac{12}7\bigr)=\tfrac{97}{56}\approx1.7321429,\\ g_3&=\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac{97}{56}+\tfrac{168}{97}\bigr)=\tfrac{18817}{10864}\approx1.7320508100 . \end{aligned}$$

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.7320508075\ldots}$, the errors are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.26795}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.017949}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9.205\cdot10^{-5}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.446\cdot10^{-9}}$. Theorem 8.2: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_0^2/(2g_0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.26795^2/4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.017949}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_1^2/(2g_1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.017949^2/3.5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9.205\cdot10^{-5}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_2}$. The correct digits grow from 0 through 2 and 4 to 8: 2, 1.7, 1.732, 1.7320508.

Bisection on $[1,2]$ yields a midpoint with an error of at most $\varepsilon/2$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.4\cdot10^{-9}}$, it needs $\varepsilon\le4.9\cdot10^{-9}$, that is, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lceil\log_2(1/(4.9\cdot10^{-9}))\rceil}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lceil27.6\rceil}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{28}$ steps instead of three.

Exercise 32 ★★ The cube root with Newton's method

The cube root $\sqrt[3]{10}$ is the zero of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(g)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g^3-10}$. Derive the Newton step, write a script with the starting value 10 and the stopping condition $|g^3-10|\le10^{-12}\cdot10$ that prints every step, and compare the result with 10 ** (1/3). From which step on do the digits double?

Solution

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(g)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g^3-10}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(g)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3g^2}$, the step is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g_n-\dfrac{g_n^3-10}{3g_n^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac13\Bigl(2g_n+\dfrac{10}{g_n^2}\Bigr)}$.

kubik_newton.pyPython
# Cube root of 10 with Newton: f(g) = g**3 - a, f'(g) = 3*g**2
a = 10.0
g = a                                     # starting value
schritte = 0
while abs(g ** 3 - a) > 1e-12 * a:
    g = g - (g ** 3 - a) / (3 * g ** 2)
    schritte = schritte + 1
    print(f"Schritt {schritte}: g = {g}")
print("Vergleich 10 ** (1/3):", a ** (1 / 3))

Exercise 33 ★★ Newton with a relative tolerance

Change wurzel_newton.py so that the condition reads abs(g * g - a) > 1e-12 * a and only the final result is printed. Test it with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{25}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{123456789}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0001}$. How many steps does it need in each case? Why does $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0001}$ need a few more steps despite being a small number?

Solution
newton_relativ.pyPython
# Newton for the square root with a relative tolerance: ends for large and small a, too
a = float(input("a = "))
g = max(1.0, a)
schritte = 0
while abs(g * g - a) > 1e-12 * a:
    g = g - (g * g - a) / (2 * g)
    schritte = schritte + 1
print("Näherung:", g, "nach", schritte, "Schritten")

Newton's method
Definition 8.1 (Newton's method)

Newton's method for a zero of a differentiable function $f$ starts with a starting value $g_0$ and from it computes one after another

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g_n-\frac{f(g_n)}{f'(g_n)},}$$

as long as $|f(g_n)|$ is greater than a tolerance $\varepsilon$. $g_{n+1}$ is the zero of the tangent line at the point $g_n$; for this, $f'(g_n)\ne0$ must hold.

Theorem 8.2 (Error of the Newton step for the square root)

Let $a>0$, $g_n>0$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12(g_n+a/g_n)}$. The errors $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g_n-\sqrt a}$ satisfy

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{d_n^2}{2g_n}.}$$

Summary

Terms
  • Newton's method – Newton's method for a zero of a differentiable function $f$ starts with a starting value $g_0$ and from it computes one after another …
  • quadratic convergence – If $\delta_n$ is about $10^{-p}$, so that $g_n$ has about $p$ significant decimal digits, then $\delta_{n+1}$ is at most about $10^{-2p}/2$: $p$ significant digits become about $2p$. This property is called quadratic convergence.
Formulas
  • Definition 8.1 (Newton's method)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g_n-\frac{f(g_n)}{f'(g_n)},}$$
  • Theorem 8.2 (Error of the Newton step for the square root)
    $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{d_n^2}{2g_n}.}$$
Key relationships
  • On “Newton's method”: In every step, Newton's method jumps to the zero of the tangent line.
  • On “The square root by Newton's method”: For the square root, the Newton step is Heron's recipe $\frac12(g+a/g)$, and it needs five steps for $\sqrt2$.
  • On “The speed of convergence”: With Newton's method, the error is squared in every step, and the number of significant digits doubles.
  • On “The limits of Newton's method”: Newton needs a nonzero derivative, a starting value close to the solution and a suitable tolerance; then it is very fast, otherwise it can cycle, crawl or run forever.
  • On “The ready-made way: math.sqrt”: math.sqrt delivers in one call the result that the methods by hand reach only approximately and with a carefully chosen tolerance.
