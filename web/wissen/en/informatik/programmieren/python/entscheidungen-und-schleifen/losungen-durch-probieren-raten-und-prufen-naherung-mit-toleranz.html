Solutions by trying: guess and check, approximation with a tolerance

What this stage is about:

With conditions and loops, a program can solve equations for which it knows no formula: it tries out candidates and checks each one. This section shows the simplest way to do this, using the example of the square root, where you can easily check every result, and shows why this way is far too slow for accurate results.

Guess and check#

The task is to find the square root of an integer, say of 49. Even if you know no formula, you can still check any number for whether it gives 49 when multiplied by itself.

A combination lock. With a three-digit combination lock, you can try all combinations from 000 to 999 in order. After 1000 tries at the latest, it is open, or you know that no combination fits.

Definition 6.1 (Guess and check)

Guess and check, also called exhaustive search, goes through the possible solutions in a fixed order and checks each one. It stops as soon as a candidate fits or it is certain that none of the remaining ones can fit.

Examplethe integer square root

The candidates are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,1,2,\ldots}$. As long as $g^2<a$, $g$ cannot be the square root, and the loop moves on to the next one. It ends at the first $g$ with $g^2\ge a$, and then there are two cases: either $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a}$, and then $g$ is the square root, or $g^2>a$, and then $a$ has no integer square root, because all smaller candidates had $g^2<a$, and all larger ones have $g^2>a$ all the more (in the outputs, “Wurzel” is German for “root” and “nach … Schritten” for “after … steps”).

wurzel_raten.pyPython
# Integer square root by guess and check: g = 0, 1, 2, ... until g*g is at least a
a = int(input("Ganze Zahl a ab 0: "))
g = 0
while g * g < a:
    g = g + 1
if g * g == a:
    print("Wurzel:", g, "nach", g, "Schritten")
else:
    print(a, "hat keine ganzzahlige Wurzel, geprüft bis", g)
LinuxTerminal
$ python3 wurzel_raten.py
Ganze Zahl a ab 0: 49
Wurzel: 7 nach 7 Schritten
$ python3 wurzel_raten.py
Ganze Zahl a ab 0: 50
50 hat keine ganzzahlige Wurzel, geprüft bis 8

The loop is certain to end, because $g$ grows by 1 in every iteration, and at some point $g^2\ge a$. How long that takes is the weakness of the method: the number of steps is the smallest integer $g$ with $g^2\ge a$, that is, about $\sqrt a$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{12}}$, that is a million steps; with every two more digits in $a$, it takes ten times as long.

⁠In short: Guess and check is certain to find a solution or to show that there is none, but it needs as many steps as there are candidates.

Approximation with a tolerance#

For $\sqrt2$, guessing fails, because $\sqrt2$ is not an integer, not even a fraction. There is also no float whose square is exactly 2; even the built-in value does not satisfy the equation:

Terminal
>>> import math
>>> math.sqrt(2) ** 2
2.0000000000000004
>>> math.sqrt(2) ** 2 == 2
False

A loop that waits for g * g == 2 would therefore never end.

A target. In target shooting, a shot counts as a hit if it lies in the inner ring, not only if it hits the exact center. In the same way, you settle for a number $g$ whose square lies close enough to $a$.

Definition 6.2 (Approximation with a tolerance)

A number $g$ is an approximation of $\sqrt a$ with the tolerance $\varepsilon>0$ if $|g^2-a|<\varepsilon$. The tolerance $\varepsilon$ determines how close “close enough” is; it is a freely chosen number and has nothing to do with the machine epsilon $\varepsilon_{\mathrm M}$.

The simplest method for finding such an approximation is called the stepping method here: you start at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and increase $g$ in fixed steps of step size $h$ until $|g^2-a|<\varepsilon$.

Example

$\sqrt{25}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.01}$ and step size $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0001}$. The loop has a second condition: it also stops when $g^2$ already lies above $a$, because then no further step can hit.

wurzel_schritte.pyPython
# Search for the square root in small steps until g*g is close enough to a
a = 25.0                                  # number whose square root is sought
eps = 0.01                                # tolerance for |g*g - a|
h = float(input("Schrittweite h = "))
g = 0.0
schritte = 0
while abs(g * g - a) >= eps and g * g <= a:
    g = g + h
    schritte = schritte + 1
if abs(g * g - a) < eps:
    print("Näherung:", g, "nach", schritte, "Schritten")
else:
    print("übersprungen: g =", g, "nach", schritte, "Schritten")
LinuxTerminal
$ python3 wurzel_schritte.py
Schrittweite h = 0.0001
Näherung: 4.999000000001688 nach 49990 Schritten

The loop stops at the first $g$ whose square lies above $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{25-0.01}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{24.99}$; that is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.999}$. The output shows 4.999000000001688 (“Näherung” is German for “approximation”), because $g$ arose from almost 50 000 additions, each of which was rounded, as with the floating-point trap in subsection 3.3.

⁠In short: With floating-point numbers, you do not look for an exact solution but for one that meets a tolerance.

Step size and tolerance#

The stepping method has two knobs to turn, $h$ and $\varepsilon$, and they are linked.

A narrow ditch. The values of $g$ that meet the tolerance form a narrow range around $\sqrt a$, like a ditch across a path. If you take large steps, you can step over the ditch without ever falling in.

How wide is the range? From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g^2-a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(g-\sqrt a)(g+\sqrt a)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g+\sqrt a}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\sqrt a}$ near the solution, it follows that $|g^2-a|<\varepsilon$ means roughly $|g-\sqrt a|<\varepsilon/(2\sqrt a)$. So the range is about $\varepsilon/\sqrt a$ wide, for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{25}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.01}$ from 4.999 to 5.001.

Examplea step that is too large

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.003}$, the candidates lie at …, 4.998, 5.001, …. The square of the first is 24.980, that of the second 25.010; both lie outside the tolerance (“übersprungen” is German for “skipped”):

LinuxTerminal
$ python3 wurzel_schritte.py
Schrittweite h = 0.003
übersprungen: g = 5.0010000000000785 nach 1667 Schritten

Without the second condition g * g <= a, the loop would run on forever here, further and further away from the solution.

The cost. So the step size may be at most as large as the range, $h\lesssim\varepsilon/\sqrt a$, and then the method needs about $\sqrt a/h\gtrsim a/\varepsilon$ steps. For $\sqrt{25}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.01}$, that is a few thousand to tens of thousands, which takes a fraction of a second. For $\sqrt2$ to twelve digits, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{-12}}$, it is about $2\cdot10^{12}$ steps. On a typical computer, a Python loop manages roughly ten million simple iterations per second; that gives $2\cdot10^5$ s, more than two days. Section 7 shows how to reach twelve digits in 41 steps.

⁠In short: The step size must be smaller than the range the tolerance allows, and that is why the number of steps grows with $a/\varepsilon$.

BackgroundWhy a tolerance is necessary, even for the best floating-point number

Between 1 and 2, adjacent float values lie at a spacing of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm M}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2^{-52}}$ (Chapter 2, Section 5.2). $\sqrt2$ lies between two of them, 1.414213562373095 and 1.4142135623730951. Their squares, rounded to the nearest float, are 1.9999999999999996 and 2.0000000000000004; neither of the two values is 2. For every other float, the square lies even further away. So the equation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ has no solution among the float values, and the smallest achievable deviation is $4.44\cdot10^{-16}$. Every loop that searches for a square root therefore needs a tolerance, and this tolerance must be larger than the deviation that can be achieved with float at all.

Exercises for Section 6#

Exercise 24 ★★ The integer cube root

Write a script kubikwurzel.py that uses guess and check to find the integer cube root of an integer $k$, that is, an integer $g$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g^3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k}$, or reports that there is none. Negative numbers should work, too. Hint: search for the root of $|k|$ and then give it the sign of $k$. Test it with 27, $-8$ and 10.

Solution
kubikwurzel.pyPython
# Integer cube root by guess and check, also for negative numbers
k = int(input("Ganze Zahl k: "))
g = 0
while g ** 3 < abs(k):
    g = g + 1
if g ** 3 == abs(k):
    if k < 0:
        g = -g
    print("Kubikwurzel:", g)
else:
    print(k, "hat keine ganzzahlige Kubikwurzel")

Exercise 25 ★★ The step size for $\sqrt{10}$

Change wurzel_schritte.py so that it searches for $\sqrt{10}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.01}$. Try the step sizes 0.001, 0.003, 0.004 and 0.005. Solution

The admissible $g$ lie between $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{9.99}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.16070}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{10.01}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.16386}$; the range is about 0.0032 wide, as the estimate $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\varepsilon/\sqrt{10}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0032}$ says. Step sizes up to this width are certain to hit it, larger ones only with luck.

Exercise 26 ★ How long guessing takes

How many steps does wurzel_raten.py need for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{14}}$ and for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{20}}$? Roughly how long does that take at ten million iterations per second? Measure it for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{14}}$ if you have the patience.

Solution

wurzel_raten.py needs about $\sqrt a$ steps: ten million for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{14}}$, ten billion for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{20}}$. At ten million iterations per second, that is one second and 1000 s, respectively, the latter about a quarter of an hour. On the computer used to test this chapter, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10^{14}}$ took just over a second (between 1.1 s and 1.6 s, depending on the system). Every two additional digits in $a$ multiply the time by ten.

Summary

Terms
  • Guess and check, exhaustive search – Guess and check, also called exhaustive search, goes through the possible solutions in a fixed order and checks each one.
  • approximation – A number $g$ is an approximation of $\sqrt a$ with the tolerance $\varepsilon>0$ if $|g^2-a|<\varepsilon$.
  • stepping method – The simplest method for finding such an approximation is called the stepping method here: you start at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and increase $g$ in fixed steps of step size $h$ until $|g^2-a|<\varepsilon$.
Key relationships
  • Guess and check is certain to find a solution or to show that there is none, but it needs as many steps as there are candidates.
  • With floating-point numbers, you do not look for an exact solution but for one that meets a tolerance.
  • The step size must be smaller than the range the tolerance allows, and that is why the number of steps grows with $a/\varepsilon$.
