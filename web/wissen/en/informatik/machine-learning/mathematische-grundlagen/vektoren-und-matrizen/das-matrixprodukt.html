What this stage is about:

Two linear maps applied one after the other again give a linear map, and therefore a matrix. This section determines it. The result is the matrix product, and its definition follows inevitably from this task.

The matrix product#

First a vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^k$ is mapped with a matrix $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$, then the result $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ with a matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ (Fig. 33). The composition $\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})$ is linear, because each of the two stages preserves sums and multiples. So by the theorem in Subsection 8.2 it has a matrix. Its column $l$ is the image of $\mathbf{e}_l$: the first stage turns this unit vector into the column $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}_l$ of $\mathbf{B}$, and the second stage multiplies this column by $\mathbf{A}$.

Definition 9.1 (Matrix product)

For $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$, the matrix product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$ is the matrix with the entries

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{il}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\,b_{jl},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,\dots,m,\quad l}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,\dots,k .}$$
(9.1)

Entry $(i,l)$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$ with column $l$ of $\mathbf{B}$. The product is defined only if $\mathbf{A}$ has as many columns as $\mathbf{B}$ has rows: the “inner” sizes must match, $(m\times n)\cdot(n\times k)$, and the result has the “outer” size $m\times k$.

Theorem 9.2 (The product is the composition)

For all $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^k$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\,\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\,(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})}$.

Derivation: Composition

By (7.1) and (9.1), component $i$ of the left-hand side is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sum_{l=1}^k(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{il}\,x_l}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{l=1}^k\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij}\,b_{jl}\,x_l}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij}\Bigl(\sum_{l=1}^kb_{jl}\,x_l\Bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij}\,(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})_j ,}$$

and this is component $i$ of $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})$. In the middle step, the two sums were swapped and $a_{ij}$ was moved in front of the inner sum.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.}$$

Each of the four entries is a sum according to (9.1) (Fig. 34).

Check with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,2)^{\top}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2,\,1)^{\top}}$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(4,\,1)^{\top}}$; and directly, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2+2,\ 1+0)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(4,\,1)^{\top}}$.

⁠In short: The matrix product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is the matrix of the map “first $\mathbf{B}$, then $\mathbf{A}$”; its entry $(i,l)$ is row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$ times column $l$ of $\mathbf{B}$.

BackgroundWhy the matrix product is defined exactly this way

At first glance, formula (9.1) looks arbitrary. It would seem more natural to multiply matrices entry by entry. But the matrix product is not a free invention; it is fixed by a requirement: it is supposed to be the matrix of the composition. By the theorem in Subsection 8.2, this matrix is unique, and its column $l$ must be the image of $\mathbf{e}_l$ under both maps, that is, $\mathbf{A}$ times column $l$ of $\mathbf{B}$. Writing this out component by component gives exactly (9.1). An entrywise product would have no such meaning: it does not describe the composition of two maps.

Many vectors at once#

By the derivation of the product, column $l$ of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is the product of $\mathbf{A}$ with column $l$ of $\mathbf{B}$. So you can read a matrix product as several matrix–vector products side by side:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}_1&\cdots&\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}_k\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}_1&\cdots&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}_k\end{pmatrix}.}$$
(9.2)

Likewise, row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$, multiplied by $\mathbf{B}$.

For data this means: if you want to apply several weighted sums to all examples, you write the weight vectors side by side as the columns of a matrix $\mathbf{W}$. The product $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$ then contains in column $l$ the predictions with the $l$th weight vector.

Worked example

Besides the purchase price with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{w}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,10,\,-4)^{\top}}$ (thousand euros), the monthly rent is to be estimated with the weights $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm M}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(12,\,30,\,-8)^{\top}}$ (euros). With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{W}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{w}&\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm M}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times2}}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}60&2&3\\40&1&1\\90&4&6\\70&3&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3&12\\10&30\\-4&-8\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}188&756\\126&502\\286&1152\\232&914\end{pmatrix}.}$$

Row $i$ contains both estimates for apartment $i$, column $1$ all purchase prices, column $2$ all rents.

⁠In short: The columns of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ are $\mathbf{A}$ times the columns of $\mathbf{B}$; $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$ applies several weighted sums to all examples at once.

Rules of calculation: the order matters#

For numbers, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\cdot2}$. For matrices this does not hold, and the reason is easy to see: rotating first and then stretching is something different from stretching first and then rotating (Fig. 35).

Theorem 9.3 (Rules of calculation for the matrix product)

For matrices of compatible sizes and scalars $c$:

  1. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C})}$ (associative);

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})\mathbf{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}}$ (distributive);

  3. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c\,(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(c\,\mathbf{A})\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(c\,\mathbf{B})}$.

In general, however, $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\ne\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$: the matrix product is not commutative.

Rule (i) follows from the meaning: both sides are the matrix of the map “first $\mathbf{C}$, then $\mathbf{B}$, then $\mathbf{A}$”, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}((\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C})\mathbf{x})}$ for every $\mathbf{x}$. Rules (ii) and (iii) are verified entry by entry with (9.1) and the rules for sums.

Worked example 1

With the rotation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf R_{90^\circ}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and the stretch $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{S}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{diag}(2,\,0.5)}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{S}\mathbf R}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\0.5&0\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf R\mathbf{S}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}0&-0.5\\2&0\end{pmatrix}.}$$

Rotating first, then stretching sends $\mathbf{e}_1$ to $(0,\,0.5)^{\top}$; stretching first, then rotating sends it to $(0,\,2)^{\top}$.

Worked example 2

From Subsection 9.1, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$. With different sizes it is even clearer: for $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times3}$ and $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times2}$, $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is a $2\times2$ matrix and $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$ a $3\times3$ matrix.

⁠In short: In a matrix product you may regroup the parentheses and multiply out, but you may not swap the order.

BackgroundWhy the placement of parentheses saves computing time

By rule (i), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})}$. The result is the same, the effort is not. For two $n\times n$ matrices, the product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ needs $n$ multiplications and $n-1$ additions for each of the $n^2$ entries, together about $2n^3$ basic arithmetic operations; after that, $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}$ costs about another $2n^2$. The route $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})$ consists of two matrix–vector products of about $2n^2$ each. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1000}$ that is roughly $2\cdot10^9$ versus $4\cdot10^6$ basic arithmetic operations, a difference by a factor of $500$. Anyone who applies a long chain of matrices to a vector therefore computes from right to left and never forms the product matrix.

Two linear stages are one#

If you connect several linear computing stages in series, no new kind of map arises. By Subsection 9.1 this is clear, but it deserves a theorem of its own, because it marks the limits of linear rules.

Theorem 9.4 (Linear maps connected in series)

If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(\mathbf{u})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}}$ are linear, then the composition $\mathbf{x}\mapsto g(f(\mathbf{x}))$ is again linear, namely with the single matrix $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$. The same holds for every finite chain of linear stages.

No matter how many linear stages you connect in series, the result can always be computed with a single matrix in one step. So whatever a chain of linear stages can do, a single matrix can already do. On a small scale, you know this from shopping: if you first convert a price from euros to dollars and then deduct a $10$ % discount, the two together are a single conversion factor.

Worked example

A first stage turns three numbers into two, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, a second turns two numbers back into three, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)}$. Together this is the single matrix

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}2&5&-1\\1&1&1\\0&3&-3\end{pmatrix}.}$$

Check with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1,\,1)^{\top}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,0)^{\top}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}(3,\,0)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(6,\,3,\,0)^{\top}}$; likewise, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2+5-1,\ 1+1+1,\ 0+3-3)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(6,\,3,\,0)^{\top}}$.

⁠In short: Linear stages in series together form a single linear map; its matrix is the product of the individual matrices.

The outer product#

The dot product $\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}$ is “row times column” and gives a number. If you reverse the order, “column times row”, then by (9.1) you get a whole matrix: the table of all products of one component of the first vector with one component of the second, like a multiplication table.

Definition 9.5 (Outer product)

For $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ and $\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is the matrix with the entries

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top})_{ij}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_i\,v_j .}$$
(9.3)

It is the matrix product of an $m\times1$ matrix with a $1\times n$ matrix. Each column is a multiple of $\mathbf{u}$, namely $v_j\,\mathbf{u}$, and each row is a multiple of $\mathbf{v}^{\top}$.

Worked example

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}4&5\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}4&5\\8&10\\12&15\end{pmatrix}.}$$

With the outer product, every matrix product can be written as a sum: “column $j$ of $\mathbf{A}$ times row $j$ of $\mathbf{B}$”, summed over $j$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf{a}_j\,\bigl(\text{row $j$ of }\mathbf{B}\bigr) .}$$
(9.4)

Entry $(i,l)$ of the right-hand side is $\sum_ja_{ij}\,b_{jl}$, exactly as in (9.1). For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ from Subsection 9.1, the sum of two outer products gives $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ again (Fig. 36).

⁠In short: The outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top}$ is the table of all products $u_iv_j$; every matrix product is a sum of such tables.

Exercise 21 ★ Products and sizes

  1. Compute $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{D}\mathbf{C}$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$.

  2. Let $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times3}$, $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times4}$ and $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^4$. Which of the expressions $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{A}$ are defined, and what is the size of the result?

  3. Compute the outer product $(1,\,-1)^{\top}(2,\,0,\,3)$ and check that every row is a multiple of $(2,\,0,\,3)$.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{C}\mathbf{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1\cdot0+2\cdot1&1\cdot1+2\cdot1\\3\cdot0+4\cdot1&3\cdot1+4\cdot1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\4&7\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{D}\mathbf{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0+3&0+4\\1+3&2+4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}3&4\\4&6\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$. The two are different.

(b) $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$: $(2\times3)\cdot(3\times4)$ fits, size $2\times4$. $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$: $(3\times4)\cdot(2\times3)$, inner sizes $4\ne2$, not defined. $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}$: $(2\times4)\cdot(4\times1)$ fits, a vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$. $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{A}$: $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}$ has size $3\times1$, and $(3\times1)\cdot(2\times3)$ does not fit, inner sizes $1\ne2$; not defined.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1,\,-1)^{\top}(2,\,0,\,3)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0&3\\-2&0&-3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$. The first row is $1\cdot(2,0,3)$, the second $-1\cdot(2,0,3)$.

Exercise 22 ★★ Two rotations

  1. Compute $\mathbf R_\alpha\mathbf R_\beta$ with (8.3). Which rotation is this? Which two formulas of trigonometry do you get along the way?

  2. Compute $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf R_{90^\circ}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf R_{90^\circ}\mathbf R_{90^\circ}}$ and $\mathbf R_{90^\circ}^4$ and interpret the results geometrically.

  3. Is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf R_\alpha\mathbf R_\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf R_\beta\mathbf R_\alpha}$? Why does this not contradict the rule that matrices do not commute?

Solution

(a) Multiplied out,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf R_\alpha\mathbf R_\beta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta&-(\cos\alpha\sin\beta+\sin\alpha\cos\beta)\\\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta&\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\end{pmatrix}.}$$

Geometrically, this is “first rotate by $\beta$, then by $\alpha$”, that is, the rotation by $\alpha+\beta$, whose matrix by (8.3) is $\mathbf R_{\alpha+\beta}$. Comparing the entries gives the addition formulas $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\cos(\alpha+\beta)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta}$. The matrix product delivers them without your having to know them by heart.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf R_{90^\circ}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mathbf{I}}$: twice $90^\circ$ is a half turn, and every point goes to its mirror image through the origin. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf R_{90^\circ}^4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-\mathbf{I})(-\mathbf{I})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$: four quarter turns are a full turn.

(c) Yes: both products are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf R_{\alpha+\beta}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf R_{\beta+\alpha}}$, because rotations of the plane about the same point can be carried out in any order. “Not commutative” only means that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}}$ does not always hold. For certain pairs, for example two rotations of the plane or two diagonal matrices, it can hold nevertheless.

Exercise 23 ★★ The right parentheses

Let $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{100\times1000}$, $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{1000\times1000}$ and $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{1000}$. Estimate the number of basic arithmetic operations for $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}$ and for $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})$. Count about $2mnk$ for a product of an $m\times n$ matrix with an $n\times k$ matrix.

Solution

$(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}$: the product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ of a $100\times1000$ matrix with a $1000\times1000$ matrix costs about $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot100\cdot1000\cdot1000}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot10^8}$, and then $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}$ costs about another $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot100\cdot1000}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot10^5}$; about $200.2$ million in total. $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})$: $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}$ costs about $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\cdot1000\cdot1000}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot10^6}$, and then $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})$ about $2\cdot10^5$; about $2.2$ million in total. The second way is about $91$ times faster, with the same result.

Theorem 9.3 (Rules of calculation for the matrix product)

For matrices of compatible sizes and scalars $c$:

  1. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C})}$ (associative);

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})\mathbf{C}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}}$ (distributive);

  3. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c\,(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(c\,\mathbf{A})\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(c\,\mathbf{B})}$.

In general, however, $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\ne\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$: the matrix product is not commutative.

Two linear stages are one
Theorem 9.4 (Linear maps connected in series)

If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(\mathbf{u})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}}$ are linear, then the composition $\mathbf{x}\mapsto g(f(\mathbf{x}))$ is again linear, namely with the single matrix $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$. The same holds for every finite chain of linear stages.

The outer product
Definition 9.5 (Outer product)

For $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ and $\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is the matrix with the entries

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top})_{ij}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_i\,v_j .}$$
(9.3)
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf{a}_j\,\bigl(\text{row $j$ of }\mathbf{B}\bigr) .}$$
(9.4)

Key relationships
  • On “The matrix product”: The matrix product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is the matrix of the map “first $\mathbf{B}$, then $\mathbf{A}$”; its entry $(i,l)$ is row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$ times column $l$ of $\mathbf{B}$.
  • On “Many vectors at once”: The columns of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ are $\mathbf{A}$ times the columns of $\mathbf{B}$; $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$ applies several weighted sums to all examples at once.
  • On “Rules of calculation: the order matters”: In a matrix product you may regroup the parentheses and multiply out, but you may not swap the order.
  • On “Two linear stages are one”: Linear stages in series together form a single linear map; its matrix is the product of the individual matrices.
  • On “The outer product”: The outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top}$ is the table of all products $u_iv_j$; every matrix product is a sum of such tables.
