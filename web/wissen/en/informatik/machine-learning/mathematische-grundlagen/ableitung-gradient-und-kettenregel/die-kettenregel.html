What this stage is about:

Many functions arise by substituting one function into another. The chain rule says how the derivative of such a composition follows from the derivatives of the parts: the factors of the individual stages are multiplied. This holds for chains with any number of links. At the end comes the logistic function, whose derivative can be expressed in terms of the function itself.

Composition#

You compute the function $(3x+1)^2$ in two steps: first $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3x+1}$, then $u^2$. Many formulas are built like this, as simple steps carried out one after another. If you recognize this construction, you can assemble the derivative from the derivatives of the steps.

Definition 2.1 (Composition)

If $g$ and $f$ are functions and every value $g(x)$ lies where $f$ is defined, then the function

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)}$$

is called the composition of $f$ and $g$, read “$f$ after $g$”. The function $g$, which is applied first, is called the inner function; $f$ is called the outer function.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3x+1)^2}$, the inner function is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3x+1}$ and the outer one is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(u)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u^2}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f\circ g}$. At the point $1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16}$. The order matters: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(g\circ f)(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(f(x))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3x^2+1}$ is a different function, with the value $4$ at the point $1$.

⁠In short: A composition $f\circ g$ applies first $g$ and then $f$; the order cannot be swapped.

The chain rule#

How does $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f\circ g}$ respond to a small change in $x$? You follow the change through the two stages. By the linear approximation, the first stage passes on a small change $\Delta x$ as approximately $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta u}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ g'(x)\,\Delta x}$. The second stage passes on this change as $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta y}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ f'(u)\,\Delta u}$. Combined, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta y}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ f'(u)\,g'(x)\,\Delta x}$. As in a gear train with two gear stages, the factors of the stages multiply (Fig. 4).

Theorem 2.2 (Chain rule)

If $g$ is differentiable at the point $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at the point $g(x)$, then $f\circ g$ is differentiable at the point $x$, and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\cdot g'(x).}$$
(2.1)

What matters is where the derivatives are evaluated: $g'$ at the point $x$, but $f'$ at the point $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(x)}$, that is, where the outer function actually operates. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(x)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(u)}$, the rule is often written as $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{d} x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{d} u}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d} u}{\mathrm{d} x}}$. This looks as if $\mathrm{d} u$ cancels out. As a mnemonic this is useful, as a justification it is not, because the symbols are not fractions but limits.

Proof: Chain rule

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(x)}$. By the linear approximation (1.4), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g(x+h)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u+g'(x)\,h+r_g(h)}$ with $r_g(h)/h\to0$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(u+k)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(u)+f'(u)\,k+r_f(k)}$ with $r_f(k)/k\to0$; here let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_f(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Set $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(x+h)-u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g'(x)\,h+r_g(h)}$. Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f\bigl(g(x+h)\bigr)-f(u)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f'(u)\,k+r_f(k)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f'(u)\,g'(x)\,h+f'(u)\,r_g(h)+r_f(k).}$$

After dividing by $h$, the middle term tends to $0$. For the last one, write $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r_f(k)/h}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(r_f(k)/k\bigr)\cdot(k/h)}$ as long as $k\ne0$; the first factor tends to $0$ because $k$ tends to $0$ along with $h$, and the second tends to $g'(x)$. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, the term is $0$ anyway. What remains is $f'(u)\,g'(x)$.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3x+1)^2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{g'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(u)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2u}$. At the point $1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{g(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, so

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F'(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f'(4)\cdot g'(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8\cdot3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{24 .}$$

Check: multiplied out, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9x^2+6x+1}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{18x+6}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F'(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{24}$. Fig. 4 shows the same thing with numbers: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{F(1.01)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4.03^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16.2409}$, and the linear approximation predicts $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{16+24\cdot0.01}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16.24}$.

⁠In short: In a composition, the derivatives of the stages multiply, each evaluated at the point where its stage operates: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f'(g(x))\,g'(x)}$.

Chains with many links#

Most calculations consist of more than two steps. The chain rule can be applied repeatedly: with three stages, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_3\circ f_2\circ f_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_3\circ(f_2\circ f_1)}$, and applying (2.1) twice gives a product of three factors. The same holds for any number of stages.

Theorem 2.3 (Chain rule for many links)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_k(u_{k-1})}$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,\dots,L}$, and let each function $f_k$ be differentiable at the point $u_{k-1}$. Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} u_L}{\mathrm{d} x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_L'(u_{L-1})\cdot f_{L-1}'(u_{L-2})\cdots f_1'(u_0).}$$
(2.2)

For the calculation, this implies an order. Each factor needs the value at which its stage operates. You therefore first compute the intermediate values $u_1,u_2,\dots$, as in the ordinary evaluation, and only then the factors. In which order you then multiply the factors does not matter for numbers.

Worked example

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dfrac1{(x^2+1)^3}}$ at the point $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. The chain has three links:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{u_1^3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{u_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac18 .}$$

The factors are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} u_1}{\mathrm{d} x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} u_2}{\mathrm{d} u_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3u_1^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{12}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{d} u_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac1{u_2^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac1{64}}$, so

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{d} x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot12\cdot\Bigl(-\frac1{64}\Bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac{24}{64}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.375 .}$$

Check: by the power rule and the chain rule, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x^2+1)^{-3}}$ has the derivative $-3\,(x^2+1)^{-4}\cdot2x$, which at the point $1$ is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-6/16}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.375}$.

⁠In short: In a long chain, the derivative is the product of the derivatives of all links; you first compute the intermediate values, then the factors.

The logistic function#

Some quantities should lie smoothly between two bounds, for example a proportion between $0$ and $1$. A function that maps every real number into this range, without a jump, without a kink and always increasing, is the logistic function. It also describes growth with saturation: a quantity first grows almost exponentially and then approaches an upper limit. Its derivative is a nice example of the chain rule and has a remarkable form.

Definition 2.4 (Logistic function)

The function

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}}$$
(2.3)

is called the logistic function.

For large positive $x$, $\mathrm{e}^{-x}$ is almost $0$ and $\sigma(x)$ almost $1$. For large negative $x$, $\mathrm{e}^{-x}$ is huge and $\sigma(x)$ almost $0$. At the point $0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12}$. Moreover, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(-x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\sigma(x)}$, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac1{1+\mathrm{e}^{x}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{\mathrm{e}^{-x}+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\frac1{1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}}$; the graph is therefore symmetric about the point $(0,\,\tfrac12)$ (Fig. 5).

Theorem 2.5 (Derivative of the logistic function)

The following holds:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma(x)\,\bigl(1-\sigma(x)\bigr).}$$
(2.4)

The derivative is positive everywhere and at most $\tfrac14$; it takes its largest value $\tfrac14$ at the point $0$.

Derivation: The derivative of the logistic function

The function is a chain with three links: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-x}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+\mathrm{e}^{u_1}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/u_2}$. The factors are $-1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{u_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-x}}$ and $-1/u_2^2$. By (2.2),

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\frac1{(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x})^2}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-x}\cdot(-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x})^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}} .}$$

The first factor is $\sigma(x)$. The second is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x})-1}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\sigma(x)}$. Because $\sigma(x)$ lies between $0$ and $1$, both factors are positive. For a number $p$ between $0$ and $1$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p\,(1-p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac14-(p-\tfrac12)^2\le\tfrac14}$, with equality exactly for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12}$, that is, for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Worked example

At the point $2$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{-2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.1353}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/1.1353}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.8808}$ and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma'(2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma(2)\,\bigl(1-\sigma(2)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.8808\cdot0.1192}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.1050 .}$$

Once $\sigma(2)$ has been computed, the derivative costs only one subtraction and one multiplication, and no further exponential function. At the point $10$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(10)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.99995}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma'(10)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0000454}$: far out, the function is almost horizontal, and small changes of the input barely arrive at the output.

⁠In short: The logistic function $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x})}$ maps every number smoothly into the interval between $0$ and $1$; its derivative is $\sigma(1-\sigma)$, at most $\tfrac14$, and can be computed from the function value alone.

Exercise 5 ★ Bell curve

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}}$.

  1. Determine the inner and the outer function and compute $f'(x)$.

  2. Compute $f'(0)$, $f'(1)$ and $f'(-1)$ and interpret the signs on the graph.

Solution

(a) The inner function is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-x^2}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2x}$, the outer one is $\mathrm{e}^u$ with the derivative $\mathrm{e}^u$. By the chain rule, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}\cdot(-2x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2x\,\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: the graph has a horizontal tangent line at the point $0$; its peak is there. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2/\mathrm{e}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.7358}$: to the right of the peak, it falls. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f'(-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2/\mathrm{e}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.7358}$: to the left of the peak, it rises. Because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(-x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(x)}$, the slopes at the points $\pm1$ are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign.

Exercise 6 ★★ A chain with three links

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{1+x^4}}$.

  1. Decompose the function into three links $u_1$, $u_2$, $y$, and at the point $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ compute first all intermediate values, then all factors.

  2. Compute $\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{d} x}$ at the point $1$ as the product of the factors, and check it with the chain rule in a single step.

Solution

(a) The links are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^4}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+u_1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{u_2}}$. At the point $1$, the intermediate values are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt2}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.4142}$. The factors are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} u_1}{\mathrm{d} x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4x^3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} u_2}{\mathrm{d} u_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{d} u_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{2\sqrt{u_2}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{2\sqrt2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.3536}$.

(b) The product is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4\cdot1\cdot\frac1{2\sqrt2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac2{\sqrt2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt2}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.4142}$. In a single step: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{y'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{2\sqrt{1+x^4}}\cdot4x^3}$, so at the point $1$ it is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac4{2\sqrt2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt2}$.

Exercise 7 ★★ Properties of the logistic function

  1. Use (2.4) and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(-x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\sigma(x)}$ to show that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma'(-x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma'(x)}$. What does this mean for the graph of $\sigma'$?

  2. Compute $\sigma(1)$ and $\sigma'(1)$ to four decimal places.

  3. Compute the second derivative $\sigma''$, expressed in terms of $\sigma$, and determine its sign for $x>0$ and for $x<0$. What does this say about the curvature of the graph of $\sigma$?

Solution

(a) With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(-x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-\sigma(x)}$, we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma'(-x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma(-x)\,\bigl(1-\sigma(-x)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(1-\sigma(x)\bigr)\,\sigma(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma'(x)}$. The graph of $\sigma'$ is symmetric about the vertical axis, as Fig. 5 shows.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.3679}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/1.3679}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.7311}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma'(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.7311\cdot0.2689}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.1966}$.

(c) With the product rule and the chain rule: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma''}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\sigma-\sigma^2)'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma'-2\sigma\sigma'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma'\,(1-2\sigma)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma(1-\sigma)(1-2\sigma)}$. For $x>0$, $\sigma>\tfrac12$, so $1-2\sigma<0$ and $\sigma''<0$: the graph bends downward there. For $x<0$, $\sigma''>0$, and it bends upward. At the point $0$, the curvature changes its sign; the slope is largest there.

Exercise 8 ★★★ The derivative of an inverse function

For $x>0$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{\ln x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$.

  1. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ using the chain rule and conclude that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\ln x)'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/x}$, assuming that $\ln$ is differentiable.

  2. Proceed in the same way with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\sqrt x)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ and compare with the power rule.

  3. The inverse function of the logistic function is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ell(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\ln\frac{p}{1-p}}$ for $0<p<1$. Check that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ell(\sigma(x))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$, and compute $\ell'(p)$ once directly and once via $\sigma'$.

Solution

(a) The left-hand side is a composition with the inner function $\ln x$ and the outer function $\mathrm{e}^u$. By the chain rule, its derivative is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm{e}^{\ln x}\cdot(\ln x)'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x\cdot(\ln x)'}$. The right-hand side has the derivative $1$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x\,(\ln x)'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\ln x)'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/x}$.

(b) From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\sqrt x)^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\sqrt x\cdot(\sqrt x)'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\sqrt x)'}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{2\sqrt x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12x^{-1/2}}$, exactly the power rule with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12}$.

(c) We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1-\sigma(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\sigma(x)}{1-\sigma(x)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{\mathrm{e}^{-x}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^x}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ell(\sigma(x))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\ln\mathrm{e}^x}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$. Directly, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ell(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\ln p-\ln(1-p)}$ and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ell'(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1p+\frac1{1-p}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{1}{p\,(1-p)} .}$$

Via $\sigma'$: differentiating $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ell(\sigma(x))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ell'(\sigma(x))\cdot\sigma'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\ell'(p)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{\sigma'(x)}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{p(1-p)}}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma(x)}$. Where $\sigma$ is flat, its inverse function is steep.

Composition
Definition 2.1 (Composition)

If $g$ and $f$ are functions and every value $g(x)$ lies where $f$ is defined, then the function

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)}$$

is called the composition of $f$ and $g$, read “$f$ after $g$”. The function $g$, which is applied first, is called the inner function; $f$ is called the outer function.

The chain rule
Theorem 2.2 (Chain rule)

If $g$ is differentiable at the point $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at the point $g(x)$, then $f\circ g$ is differentiable at the point $x$, and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\cdot g'(x).}$$
(2.1)
Chains with many links
Theorem 2.3 (Chain rule for many links)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{u_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_k(u_{k-1})}$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,\dots,L}$, and let each function $f_k$ be differentiable at the point $u_{k-1}$. Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d} u_L}{\mathrm{d} x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f_L'(u_{L-1})\cdot f_{L-1}'(u_{L-2})\cdots f_1'(u_0).}$$
(2.2)
The logistic function
Definition 2.4 (Logistic function)

The function

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}}$$
(2.3)

is called the logistic function.

Theorem 2.5 (Derivative of the logistic function)

The following holds:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sigma(x)\,\bigl(1-\sigma(x)\bigr).}$$
(2.4)

The derivative is positive everywhere and at most $\tfrac14$; it takes its largest value $\tfrac14$ at the point $0$.

Key relationships
  • On “Composition”: A composition $f\circ g$ applies first $g$ and then $f$; the order cannot be swapped.
  • On “The chain rule”: In a composition, the derivatives of the stages multiply, each evaluated at the point where its stage operates: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(f\circ g)'(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f'(g(x))\,g'(x)}$.
  • On “Chains with many links”: In a long chain, the derivative is the product of the derivatives of all links; you first compute the intermediate values, then the factors.
  • On “The logistic function”: The logistic function $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sigma(x)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x})}$ maps every number smoothly into the interval between $0$ and $1$; its derivative is $\sigma(1-\sigma)$, at most $\tfrac14$, and can be computed from the function value alone.
