What this stage is about:

The gradient describes the tilt of the terrain, but not its curvature. The Hessian matrix collects the second partial derivatives. It is symmetric, and with it the linear approximation becomes the quadratic one. Its eigenvalues tell how strongly the terrain curves in different directions, and they connect this section with the quadratic forms from Chapter 2.

Second partial derivatives#

The partial derivatives $\partial_jf$ are themselves functions of $\mathbf{x}$. You can differentiate them partially once more, with respect to the same variable or a different one. This describes how the slope in the $x_j$ direction changes as you move on in the $x_i$ direction.

Definition 8.1 (Second partial derivatives)

The second partial derivatives of $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ are

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\Bigr),}$$
(8.1)

differentiated first with respect to $x_j$, then with respect to $x_i$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{j}$ you write $\partial^2f/\partial x_i^2$. For $i\ne j$ they are called mixed partial derivatives.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2x_2+x_2^2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x_1x_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_2f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+2x_2}$, so

$$\begin{gathered} \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_1^2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}(2x_1x_2)=2x_2,\qquad \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_2\,\partial x_1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2}(2x_1x_2)=2x_1,\\ \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_1\,\partial x_2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}(x_1^2+2x_2)=2x_1,\qquad \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_2^2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2}(x_1^2+2x_2)=2 . \end{gathered}$$

At the point $(1,\,2)$ these are $4$, $2$, $2$ and $2$. The two mixed derivatives are equal, although they arose in different ways.

⁠In short: Second partial derivatives differentiate a partial derivative partially once more; the mixed ones describe how the slope in one direction changes as you walk in another direction.

The Hessian matrix#

A function of $d$ variables has $d^2$ second partial derivatives. Just as the first derivatives are gathered in the gradient, you gather these in one object, here in a square matrix.

Definition 8.2 (Hessian matrix)

The $d\times d$ matrix

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_1^2}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_1\,\partial x_d}\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ \dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_d\,\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_d^2}\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\bigl(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\bigr)_{ij}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}(\mathbf{x}),}$$
(8.2)

is called the Hessian matrix of $f$ at the point $\mathbf{x}$.

The matrix is named after the mathematician Otto Hesse; some books write $\nabla^2f$. Its column $j$ is the gradient of the function $\partial_jf$. It is therefore closely related to the Jacobian matrix of the gradient, that is, of the vector-valued function $\mathbf{x}\mapsto\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$: entry $(i,j)$ of that Jacobian matrix is $\partial(\partial_if)/\partial x_j$, the derivative in the other order. The next subsection shows that both orders almost always give the same result; then the Hessian matrix is the Jacobian matrix of the gradient.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2x_2+x_2^2}$, by the previous subsection,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}2x_2&2x_1\\2x_1&2\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(1,2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}.}$$

For a quadratic function $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+c}$ with symmetric $\mathbf{A}$, Section 7 gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\nabla f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}}$ and therefore $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}}$ at every point. For the bowl $x_1^2+4x_2^2$ from Section 4, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{diag}(2,\,8)}$.

⁠In short: The Hessian matrix collects all second partial derivatives; for a quadratic function $\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+c$ it is $\mathbf{A}$ everywhere.

Schwarz's theorem#

In the worked example, the two mixed derivatives were equal. That is no coincidence; it holds for all functions whose second derivatives have no jumps.

Theorem 8.3 (Schwarz's theorem)

If all second partial derivatives of $f$ exist near $\mathbf{x}$ and are continuous at the point $\mathbf{x}$, then at that point

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_j\,\partial x_i}}$$
(8.3)

for all $i,j$. The Hessian matrix is then symmetric.

You can see the reason in a calculation in which the order does not appear at all. You move from $\mathbf{x}$ by $h$ in the $x_1$ direction and by $k$ in the $x_2$ direction and form the mixed difference

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta(h,k)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(x_1+h,\,x_2+k)-f(x_1+h,\,x_2)-f(x_1,\,x_2+k)+f(x_1,\,x_2).}$$

If you group the first term with the third and the second with the fourth, then $\Delta$ is the change in the difference $f(x_1+h,\cdot)-f(x_1,\cdot)$ under the step $k$ in the $x_2$ direction: how does the rise in the $x_1$ direction change as you walk in the $x_2$ direction? Divided by $hk$, this approaches, for small $h$ and $k$, the derivative first with respect to $x_1$, then with respect to $x_2$. If instead you group the first two and the last two terms, you read the same number as the change in the rise in the $x_2$ direction under the step in the $x_1$ direction, and the quotient approaches the derivative in the other order. Both limits belong to the same number $\Delta/(hk)$. Continuity ensures that the order of the two passages to the limit does not matter.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2x_2+x_2^2}$ at the point $(1,\,2)$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.1}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(1.1,\ 2.1)-f(1.1,\ 2)-f(1,\ 2.1)+f(1,2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.951-6.42-6.51+6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.021,}$$

so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\Delta/(hk)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.1}$, close to the mixed derivative $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$. The deviation arises because $2x_1$ is not constant between $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.1}$.

⁠In short: With continuous second derivatives, the order of differentiation does not matter, and the Hessian matrix is symmetric.

BackgroundWhen the second derivatives are not continuous

Without continuity, the theorem can fail. The classic counterexample is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x_1,x_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1x_2\,\frac{x_1^2-x_2^2}{x_1^2+x_2^2}}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(0,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. If you compute the first partial derivatives on the axes, you get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f(0,x_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-x_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_2f(x_1,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1}$. Differentiating the first with respect to $x_2$ gives $-1$ at the origin; differentiating the second with respect to $x_1$ gives $+1$. So the mixed derivatives at the origin differ, because the second derivatives jump there. For the functions in this chapter that have no kink, this does not happen; they are built from polynomials, exponential functions and basic arithmetic operations, and wherever they are defined, they have continuous derivatives of any order.

The quadratic approximation#

In one variable, the tangent line was improved by a parabola in Section 1.5. With several variables, the tangent plane is replaced by a curved surface whose curvature is described by the Hessian matrix.

Theorem 8.4 (Quadratic approximation)

If $f$ has continuous second partial derivatives near $\mathbf{x}$, then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{h}+\tfrac12\,\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{h}+r_2(\mathbf{h})}$$
$$\text{with}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lim_{\mathbf{h}\to\mathbf{0}}\frac{r_2(\mathbf{h})}{\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0 .}$$
(8.4)
Derivation: The quadratic approximation along a line

Restrict $f$ to the line through $\mathbf{x}$ in the direction $\mathbf{h}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\phi(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(\mathbf{x}+t\,\mathbf{h})}$. This is a function of one variable. By (6.4), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\phi'(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{h})^{\top}\mathbf{h}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_jh_j\,\partial_jf(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{h})}$. You differentiate each term once more with (6.4), this time for the function $\partial_jf$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\phi''(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j=1}^dh_j\sum_{i=1}^d\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{h})\,h_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{h})\,\mathbf{h} .}$$

The quadratic approximation (1.6) of $\phi$ at the point $0$, evaluated at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{t}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, reads $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\phi(1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\phi(0)+\phi'(0)+\tfrac12\phi''(0)}$, and that is (8.4). That the error vanishes faster than $\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^2$ follows from the continuity of the second derivatives.

The new term $\tfrac12\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f\mathbf{h}$ is a quadratic form in the sense of Chapter 2, Section 5.1, with the Hessian matrix as its matrix. Near $\mathbf{x}$, the function therefore looks like a plane plus a quadratic form.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2x_2+x_2^2}$ at the point $(1,\,2)$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{h}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0.01,\ 0.02)^{\top}}$ as in Section 3.4,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12\,\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f\mathbf{h}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12\bigl(4\cdot0.01^2+2\cdot2\cdot0.01\cdot0.02+2\cdot0.02^2\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12\,(0.0004+0.0008+0.0008)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.001}$$

(Fig. 15). The quadratic approximation is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{6+0.14+0.001}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.141}$, the exact value $6.141002$. The error dropped from $0.001002$ to $0.000002$. The remainder is the third-order term: $x_1^2x_2$ contains the term $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{h_1^2h_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.0001\cdot0.02}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.000002}$, which no quadratic approximation captures.

⁠In short: Near $\mathbf{x}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{h}+\tfrac12\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{h}}$: a plane plus a quadratic form.

The curvature in a direction#

How strongly is the terrain curved if you walk from $\mathbf{x}$ in the direction $\mathbf{u}$? The derivation in the previous subsection has already given the answer: the second derivative of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\phi(t)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{u})}$ at the point $0$ is $\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}$.

Definition 8.5 (Curvature in a direction)

For a vector $\mathbf{u}$ of length $1$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d} t^2}f(\mathbf{x}+t\,\mathbf{u})\Big|_{t=0}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{u}}$$
(8.5)

is called the second directional derivative of $f$ at the point $\mathbf{x}$ in the direction $\mathbf{u}$. It measures the curvature of the cross-section curve in this direction.

With the results from Chapter 2, you can say right away how large this curvature is at most and at least. The Hessian matrix is symmetric and, by the spectral theorem from Chapter 2, Section 7.6, has real eigenvalues and mutually perpendicular eigenvectors.

Theorem 8.6 (Curvature and eigenvalues)

Let $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$ be the smallest and the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})$. Then for every direction $\mathbf{u}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$

$$\lambda_{\min}\le\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{u}\le\lambda_{\max}.$$
(8.6)

In the direction of an eigenvector, the curvature is exactly the corresponding eigenvalue. If $\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})$ is positive definite, the curvature is positive in every direction; if it is indefinite, the terrain curves upward in some directions and downward in others.

The inequality is the statement about the largest and smallest value of a quadratic form on the unit circle from Chapter 2, Section 8.5. For an eigenvector $\mathbf{u}$ for the eigenvalue $\lambda$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\,\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda}$. The terms positive definite and indefinite come from Chapter 2, Section 8; there, the shape of a quadratic form could be read off from its eigenvalues, as a bowl, a trough or a saddle. Via the quadratic approximation (8.4), this carries over to the terrain near $\mathbf{x}$: for a positive definite Hessian matrix, the contour lines of the curvature term are ellipses whose principal axes, by Chapter 2, Section 7.7, lie along the eigenvectors and are short in the direction of strong curvature.

Worked example 1

For the bowl $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+4x_2^2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{diag}(2,\,8)}$ with the eigenvalues $2$ and $8$. In the $x_1$ direction the curvature is $2$, in the $x_2$ direction it is $8$, and in the direction $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$ it is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12(2+8)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$. This explains the elongated contour lines from Section 4.4: in the $x_2$ direction, the bowl curves four times as strongly, its walls are steeper there, and the ellipses are narrow in this direction.

Worked example 2

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2x_2+x_2^2}$ at the point $(1,\,2)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$. By Chapter 2, Section 6.5, trace $6$ and determinant $4$ give the eigenvalues $3\pm\sqrt5$, that is, about $5.236$ and $0.764$. Both are positive, so the Hessian matrix is positive definite: near $(1,\,2)$, the terrain curves upward in every direction. In the direction $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$, the curvature is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12\,(4+2+2+2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5,}$$

and it lies between $0.764$ and $5.236$ (Fig. 16).

⁠In short: The curvature in the direction $\mathbf{u}$ is $\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f\mathbf{u}$; it lies between the smallest and the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian matrix, and their signs decide whether the terrain curves like a bowl or like a saddle.

BackgroundThe Hessian-vector product without the Hessian matrix

With many variables, the Hessian matrix is huge: with a million variables, it has $10^{12}$ entries. Often, however, you only need its product with a vector $\mathbf{v}$. You can get it without setting up the matrix. The number $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_jv_j\,\partial_jf(\mathbf{x})}$ is a function of $\mathbf{x}$, and its gradient has the components $\sum_jv_j\,\partial^2f/\partial x_i\partial x_j$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{v}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{v}}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2x_2+x_2^2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1)^{\top}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\nabla f^{\top}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x_1x_2+x_1^2+2x_2}$ with the gradient $(2x_2+2x_1,\ 2x_1+2)^{\top}$, which at the point $(1,\,2)$ is $(6,\,4)^{\top}$, and that is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(4+2,\ 2+2)^{\top}}$. So you compute two gradients instead of a matrix with $d^2$ entries.

Exercise 28 ★ Hessian matrices

Compute the Hessian matrix at the given point and check its symmetry.

  1. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^3+x_1x_2^2}$ at the point $(1,\,2)$;

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{x_1+2x_2}}$ at the point $(0,\,0)$. What is the rank of the matrix?

  3. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1x_2x_3}$ at the point $(1,\,2,\,3)$.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3x_1^2+x_2^2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_2f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x_1x_2}$, so

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}6x_1&2x_2\\2x_2&2x_1\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(1,2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}6&4\\4&2\end{pmatrix};}$$

the mixed derivatives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_2(3x_1^2+x_2^2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1(2x_1x_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x_2}$ are equal.

(b) With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{x_1+2x_2}}$, we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_2f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2f}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f\cdot\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, and at the origin $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. The second row is twice the first; the rank is $1$, and the eigenvalues are $5$ and $0$. This fits: $f$ depends only on $x_1+2x_2$ and is constant along the direction $(2,\,-1)^{\top}$, so it is not curved there.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_2x_3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_2f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1x_3}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_3f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1x_2}$, so

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}0&x_3&x_2\\x_3&0&x_1\\x_2&x_1&0\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(1,2,3)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}0&3&2\\3&0&1\\2&1&0\end{pmatrix}.}$$

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1\,\mathrm{e}^{x_2}}$ as in Exercise 10.

  1. Compute $\mathbf{H}_f(2,0)$.

  2. Use the quadratic approximation at the point $(2,\,0)$ to compute an approximation for $f(2.1,\ -0.05)$, and compare it with the linear approximation from Exercise 10 and with the exact value.

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1^2f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1\partial_2f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm{e}^{x_2}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_2^2f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1\mathrm{e}^{x_2}}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(2,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$.

(b) With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{h}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0.1,\ -0.05)^{\top}}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12\,\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f\mathbf{h}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12\bigl(0+2\cdot1\cdot0.1\cdot(-0.05)+2\cdot0.05^2\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12\,(-0.01+0.005)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.0025 .}$$

The quadratic approximation is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2+0-0.0025}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.9975}$, the linear one was $2.0$, and the exact value is $1.997582$. The error drops from $0.0024$ to $0.00008$. The cross term $-0.005$ is the interaction from Exercise 10.

Exercise 30 ★★ Curvatures of a skewed bowl

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+4x_2^2+x_1x_2}$.

  1. Write $f$ as $\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}$ and give $\mathbf{H}$.

  2. Compute the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{H}$ and the curvatures in the directions $\mathbf{e}_1$, $\mathbf{e}_2$ and $(1,\,-1)^{\top}/\sqrt2$. Check (8.6).

  3. In which direction is the curvature largest? Give an eigenvector.

Solution

(a) $\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&8\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12\,(2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+8x_2^2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+x_1x_2+4x_2^2}$. This is also the Hessian matrix of $f$.

(b) Trace $10$, determinant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{16-1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{15}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda^2-10\lambda+15}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\pm\sqrt{10}}$, about $8.162$ and $1.838$. The curvatures are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{e}_1^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{e}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{e}_2^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{e}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8}$ and, for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12\,(2-1-1+8)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$. All three lie between $1.838$ and $8.162$.

(c) The curvature is largest in the direction of an eigenvector for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5+\sqrt{10}}$. The first row of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{H}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(2-\lambda)v_1+v_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\ 3+\sqrt{10})^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\ 6.162)^{\top}}$, normalized about $(0.160,\ 0.987)^{\top}$. This is almost the $x_2$-direction, rotated slightly by the cross term $x_1x_2$.

Exercise 31 ★★★ Different mixed derivatives

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(x_1,x_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1x_2\,\dfrac{x_1^2-x_2^2}{x_1^2+x_2^2}}$ for $\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(0,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

  1. Use the difference quotient to show that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f(0,x_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-x_2}$ for $x_2\ne0$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f(0,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Compute $\partial_2f(x_1,0)$ in the same way.

  2. Conclude that the two mixed derivatives at the origin are $-1$ and $+1$. Which hypothesis of Schwarz's theorem is violated?

Solution

(a) On the axis $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. For $x_2\ne0$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f(0,x_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,x_2)-0}{h}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lim_{h\to0}x_2\,\frac{h^2-x_2^2}{h^2+x_2^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_2\cdot\frac{-x_2^2}{x_2^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-x_2 .}$$

At the origin, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(h,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_1f(0,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$; the formula $-x_2$ thus also holds for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. Likewise, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\partial_2f(x_1,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lim_k x_1\frac{x_1^2-k^2}{x_1^2+k^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1}$.

(b) Differentiating first with respect to $x_1$, then with respect to $x_2$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2}(-x_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. First with respect to $x_2$, then with respect to $x_1$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}(x_1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{+1}$. The mixed derivatives at the origin are different. What is violated is the continuity of the second derivatives at the origin: away from the origin, the mixed derivative is $-1$ everywhere on the $x_2$-axis and $+1$ everywhere on the $x_1$-axis. So it jumps at the origin, and the two orders of differentiation approach the origin from different sides.

Second partial derivatives
Definition 8.1 (Second partial derivatives)

The second partial derivatives of $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ are

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\Bigr),}$$
(8.1)

differentiated first with respect to $x_j$, then with respect to $x_i$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{j}$ you write $\partial^2f/\partial x_i^2$. For $i\ne j$ they are called mixed partial derivatives.

The Hessian matrix
Definition 8.2 (Hessian matrix)

The $d\times d$ matrix

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_1^2}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_1\,\partial x_d}\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ \dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_d\,\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_d^2}\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\bigl(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\bigr)_{ij}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}(\mathbf{x}),}$$
(8.2)

is called the Hessian matrix of $f$ at the point $\mathbf{x}$.

Schwarz's theorem
Theorem 8.3 (Schwarz's theorem)

If all second partial derivatives of $f$ exist near $\mathbf{x}$ and are continuous at the point $\mathbf{x}$, then at that point

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_j\,\partial x_i}}$$
(8.3)

for all $i,j$. The Hessian matrix is then symmetric.

The quadratic approximation
Theorem 8.4 (Quadratic approximation)

If $f$ has continuous second partial derivatives near $\mathbf{x}$, then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{h}+\tfrac12\,\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{h}+r_2(\mathbf{h})}$$
$$\text{with}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lim_{\mathbf{h}\to\mathbf{0}}\frac{r_2(\mathbf{h})}{\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0 .}$$
(8.4)
The curvature in a direction
Definition 8.5 (Curvature in a direction)

For a vector $\mathbf{u}$ of length $1$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d} t^2}f(\mathbf{x}+t\,\mathbf{u})\Big|_{t=0}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{u}}$$
(8.5)

is called the second directional derivative of $f$ at the point $\mathbf{x}$ in the direction $\mathbf{u}$. It measures the curvature of the cross-section curve in this direction.

Theorem 8.6 (Curvature and eigenvalues)

Let $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$ be the smallest and the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})$. Then for every direction $\mathbf{u}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$

$$\lambda_{\min}\le\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{u}\le\lambda_{\max}.$$
(8.6)

In the direction of an eigenvector, the curvature is exactly the corresponding eigenvalue. If $\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})$ is positive definite, the curvature is positive in every direction; if it is indefinite, the terrain curves upward in some directions and downward in others.

Key relationships
  • On “Second partial derivatives”: Second partial derivatives differentiate a partial derivative partially once more; the mixed ones describe how the slope in one direction changes as you walk in another direction.
  • On “The Hessian matrix”: The Hessian matrix collects all second partial derivatives; for a quadratic function $\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+c$ it is $\mathbf{A}$ everywhere.
  • On “Schwarz's theorem”: With continuous second derivatives, the order of differentiation does not matter, and the Hessian matrix is symmetric.
  • On “The quadratic approximation”: Near $\mathbf{x}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{ f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{h}+\tfrac12\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{h}}$: a plane plus a quadratic form.
  • On “The curvature in a direction”: The curvature in the direction $\mathbf{u}$ is $\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_f\mathbf{u}$; it lies between the smallest and the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian matrix, and their signs decide whether the terrain curves like a bowl or like a saddle.
