What this stage is about:

A matrix rotates and stretches most vectors at the same time. Some directions, however, it does not rotate: an arrow in such a direction only becomes longer, shorter or flipped over. This section finds these directions and the stretch factors that belong to them. They are the natural axes of a matrix.

What you need for thiscomplex numbers

The search for stretch factors leads to polynomial equations, and not every one of them has real solutions. This subsection collects what this chapter uses about complex numbers.

Complex numbers. A complex number $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x+\mathrm j\,y}$ consists of the real part $x$ and the imaginary part $y$, two real numbers. The imaginary unit satisfies $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm j^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. Many mathematics books call it $\mathrm i$; this chapter, like Python, writes an upright $\mathrm j$, because Python writes the number $3+4\,\mathrm j$ as 3+4j, and $i$ remains free as an index.

Conjugation and magnitude. The complex conjugate of $z$ is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x-\mathrm j\,y}$. We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z\,z^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x^2+y^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2}$, the square of the magnitude, a real number that is zero only for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. A number is real if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z^*}$. Conjugation is compatible with sums and products: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(z+w)^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z^*+w^*}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(zw)^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{z^*w^*}$. For a vector $\mathbf{v}$ with complex components, $\mathbf{v}^*$ in this chapter denotes the vector of the conjugated components, not transposed. Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{v}^*)^{\top}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_i|v_i|^2>0}$$
$$\text{for }\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0} .$$

Euler's formula. For every real number $\alpha$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm e^{\mathrm j\alpha}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\cos\alpha+\mathrm j\sin\alpha}$, a point on the unit circle of the complex plane with magnitude $1$.

Roots of polynomials. Every polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ with real or complex coefficients has exactly $n$ complex roots if multiple roots are counted according to their multiplicity, and it factors into $n$ linear factors. If the polynomial has real coefficients, then whenever $z$ is a root, so is $z^*$: non-real roots come in pairs.

Worked example

By the quadratic formula, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda^2-1.2\lambda+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ has the roots $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6\pm\sqrt{0.36-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6\pm\mathrm j\,0.8}$, a conjugate pair with magnitude $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{0.36+0.64}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-\mathrm j)^{\top}}$, we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,\mathrm j)^{\top}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{v}^*)^{\top}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1\cdot1+\mathrm j\cdot(-\mathrm j)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ (Fig. 16).

⁠In short: Complex numbers $x+\mathrm jy$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm j^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ solve every polynomial equation; conjugation flips the sign of the imaginary part, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{zz^*}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|z|^2}$ is real.

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues#

In Chapter 1, Section 8.3, the grid of the plane was rotated, stretched and sheared by matrices. In the process, most arrows change their direction. For some matrices, however, there are directions in which an arrow stays on its line through the origin: it is only stretched, compressed or flipped over (Fig. 17).

Definition 6.1 (Eigenvector, eigenvalue)

Let $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$. A vector $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$ is called an eigenvector of $\mathbf{A}$ if there is a number $\lambda$ with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\,\mathbf{v} .}$$
(6.1)

The number $\lambda$ is called the corresponding eigenvalue.

The zero vector is excluded because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{0}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{0}}$ holds for every number $\lambda$ and reveals nothing. The eigenvalue, on the other hand, may be zero: then the eigenvector is mapped to $\mathbf{0}$. A negative eigenvalue flips the arrow over. In this chapter, eigenvalues are denoted $\lambda$ or $\lambda_i$. The prefix eigen comes from German and has been adopted into English: eigenvalue, eigenvector.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}5\\5\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}2\\-4\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}4\\2\end{pmatrix}.}$$

So $(1,\,1)^{\top}$ is an eigenvector for the eigenvalue $5$, and $(1,\,-2)^{\top}$ is one for the eigenvalue $2$. The vector $(1,\,0)^{\top}$ is not an eigenvector, because $(4,\,2)^{\top}$ is not a multiple of it.

⁠In short: An eigenvector is not rotated by the matrix, only multiplied by a number; this number is the eigenvalue.

BackgroundThe same idea for infinitely long sequences: compound interest

A balance of $100$ euros grows with $5$ % interest per year to $100$, $105$, $110.25$, … euros, in general to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100\cdot1.05^n}$ after $n$ years. The rule “replace each value by that of the following year” turns a sequence $(a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots)$ into the sequence $(a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots)$. It is linear: shifting the sum of two sequences gives the sum of the shifted sequences, and likewise for multiples. For the balance it gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(105,\ 110.25,\ \dots)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.05\cdot(100,\ 105,\ \dots) .}$$

The shape of the sequence is preserved; the only thing added is a numerical factor: the sequence $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{100\cdot1.05^n}$ is an eigenvector of shifting, and the eigenvalue is the growth factor $1.05$. Equation (6.1) says the same thing for a matrix. Shifting also has no eigenvectors other than such sequences: from $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_{n+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\,a_n}$ for all $n$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_0\,\lambda^n}$. In both cases, for sequences as for vectors, there are things that the linear rule only multiplies by a number.

The characteristic polynomial#

How do you find eigenvalues without guessing? Rewrite (6.1) so that everything is on one side: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}-\lambda\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$, that is, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$. We are looking for a $\lambda$ for which this homogeneous system has a solution $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$. By Chapter 1, Section 11.5, this means: the matrix $\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}$ is not invertible. By Chapter 1, Section 14.6, this is the case exactly when its determinant is zero.

Definition 6.2 (Characteristic polynomial)

For $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})}$$
(6.2)

is called the characteristic polynomial of $\mathbf{A}$.

Theorem 6.3 (Eigenvalues are roots)

A number $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\mathbf{A}$ if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

The characteristic polynomial has degree $n$. For a $2\times2$ matrix it is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\begin{pmatrix}a-\lambda&b\\c&d-\lambda\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2-(a+d)\,\lambda+(ad-bc) .}$$
(6.3)

By the subsection on complex numbers, it has exactly $n$ complex roots, counted with multiplicity. So an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues if complex ones are allowed and multiple ones are counted according to their multiplicity.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2-7\lambda+(12-2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2-7\lambda+10}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)}$. The eigenvalues are $5$ and $2$, as in the previous subsection. For the diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(3,\,-1)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)}$ with the roots $3$ and $-1$: the eigenvalues of a diagonal matrix are on its diagonal, and the eigenvectors are the unit vectors.

⁠In short: The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial $\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})$; counted with multiplicity and including complex ones, an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues.

Computing eigenvectors#

Once an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is known, the corresponding eigenvectors are the solutions $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$ of the homogeneous system $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$. You find them with Gaussian elimination. Together with $\mathbf{v}$, every multiple $c\,\mathbf{v}$ with $c\ne0$ is an eigenvector, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}(c\mathbf{v})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c\lambda\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda(c\mathbf{v})}$. So an eigenvector is never unique; only its direction is, and sometimes not even that.

Definition 6.4 (Eigenspace)

The null space of $\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}$ is called the eigenspace of $\mathbf{A}$ for the eigenvalue $\lambda$.

It contains all eigenvectors for $\lambda$ and the zero vector. As a null space, it is a subspace in the sense of Section 4.1.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}-5\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}.}$$

Both rows require the same thing: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-v_1+v_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. The eigenspace is the line of all multiples of $(1,\,1)^{\top}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}-2\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ with the condition $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2v_1+v_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$; the eigenspace is the line in the direction of $(1,\,-2)^{\top}$. Normalized, the eigenvectors are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-2)^{\top}/\sqrt5}$, as in Fig. 17. They are not perpendicular to each other: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1,\,1)(1,\,-2)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1\ne0}$.

⁠In short: The eigenvectors for $\lambda$ are the solutions $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$ of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$; together with the zero vector they form the eigenspace.

Trace and determinant#

Two numbers appear in (6.3) that you can read directly off the matrix: $ad-bc$ is the determinant, and $a+d$ is the sum of the diagonal entries. This sum has a name of its own. Both numbers are related to the eigenvalues in a simple way and are therefore useful as a check.

Definition 6.5 (Trace)

The trace of a square matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is the sum of its diagonal entries:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_{11}+a_{22}+\dots+a_{nn}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^na_{ii} .}$$
(6.4)
Theorem 6.6 (Trace and determinant from the eigenvalues)

If $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$, counted with multiplicity and including complex ones, then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\dots+\lambda_n,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda_1\cdot\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_n .}$$
(6.5)

Moreover, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})}$ for $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$.

Derivation: Trace and determinant

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$: the polynomial $\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc)$ factors into $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda+\lambda_1\lambda_2}$. Comparing coefficients gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a+d}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{ad-bc}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda_1\lambda_2}$.

For arbitrary $n$, $p(\lambda)$ likewise factors into $(\lambda_1-\lambda)(\lambda_2-\lambda)\cdots(\lambda_n-\lambda)$; the sign is right because the term $(-\lambda)^n$ occurs in $\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})$. Setting $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ gives $\det\mathbf{A}$ on the left and $\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n$ on the right. For the trace, compare the coefficients of $\lambda^{n-1}$. On the right, they are $(-1)^{n-1}(\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_n)$. On the left, in the Laplace expansion only the product of the diagonal entries $(a_{11}-\lambda)\cdots(a_{nn}-\lambda)$ yields such a high power of $\lambda$, with the coefficient $(-1)^{n-1}(a_{11}+\dots+a_{nn})$.

For the last statement: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_i(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{ii}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_i\sum_la_{il}b_{li}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_l\sum_ib_{li}a_{il}}$. This is the same double sum, only ordered differently.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{7}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5+2}$ (Fig. 18) and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\cdot2}$. For $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{7}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$; so the eigenvalues are the solutions of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda^2-7\lambda+6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, namely $6$ and $1$. For $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times3}$ with the rows $(1,0,2)$, $(0,1,1)$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ with trace $7$, and $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$ is a $3\times3$ matrix with the diagonal entries $1$, $1$ and $5$, also with trace $7$.

⁠In short: The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries and the sum of the eigenvalues; the determinant is the product of the eigenvalues.

Complex eigenvalues#

A rotation by $90^\circ$ leaves no arrow on its line: every arrow is turned further by a right angle. So it cannot have a real eigenvector. The characteristic polynomial shows what happens instead.

Worked example 1

For the rotation $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2+1}$ with the roots $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm j}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mathrm j}$. The eigenvalues are purely imaginary. The complex eigenvector $(1,\,-\mathrm j)^{\top}$ belongs to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm j}$, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)(1,\,-\mathrm j)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\mathrm j,\,1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm j\,(1,\,-\mathrm j)^{\top}}$.

Worked example 2

The rotation $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0.6&-0.8\\0.8&0.6\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ from Section 1.4 has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2-1.2\lambda+1}$ and, by the worked example on complex numbers, the eigenvalues $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.6\pm\mathrm j\,0.8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\cos\alpha\pm\mathrm j\sin\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm e^{\pm\mathrm j\alpha}}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{53.13^\circ}$. In general, the rotation by the angle $\alpha$ has the eigenvalues $\mathrm e^{\pm\mathrm j\alpha}$.

So a real matrix can have complex eigenvalues. Because its characteristic polynomial has real coefficients, they occur in conjugate pairs. A real eigenvector belongs only to a real eigenvalue: if $\mathbf{v}$ were real and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{v}}$, then $\lambda\mathbf{v}$, as the image of a real vector, would be real, and hence so would $\lambda$. Complex eigenvalues indicate that the matrix rotates in a plane.

⁠In short: A real matrix can have complex eigenvalues, always in conjugate pairs; a rotation by $\alpha$ has the eigenvalues $\mathrm e^{\pm\mathrm j\alpha}$.

BackgroundRotating means multiplying by $\mathrm e^{\mathrm j\alpha}$

With complex numbers you can see the result directly. Identify the arrow $(x_1,\,x_2)^{\top}$ with the complex number $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1+\mathrm jx_2}$, that is, with a point of the complex plane. A rotation by $\alpha$ is then multiplication by $\mathrm e^{\mathrm j\alpha}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathrm e^{\mathrm j\alpha}z}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1\cos\alpha-x_2\sin\alpha)+\mathrm j\,(x_1\sin\alpha+x_2\cos\alpha)}$, and these are exactly the two components of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}$ for the rotation matrix from Chapter 1, Section 8.3. In complex notation, the rotation is thus a multiplication by a number, and this number is an eigenvalue. The second eigenvalue $\mathrm e^{-\mathrm j\alpha}$ belongs to the conjugate notation $z^*$, which rotates in the opposite direction.

Multiple eigenvalues#

The characteristic polynomial can have a double root. Then two of the $n$ eigenvalues coincide. How often an eigenvalue occurs is a number in its own right.

Definition 6.7 (Algebraic multiplicity)

The algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

Worked example

The matrix $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{diag}(2,\,2)}$ has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2-\lambda)^2}$: the eigenvalue $2$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$. The shear $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ from Chapter 1, Section 8.3, has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1-\lambda)^2}$: the eigenvalue $1$ also has algebraic multiplicity $2$. The matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ has two different eigenvalues, each with algebraic multiplicity $1$.

⁠In short: The algebraic multiplicity counts how often an eigenvalue occurs as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

How many eigenvectors there are#

The two examples with a double eigenvalue behave completely differently. The matrix $2\mathbf{I}$ stretches every arrow to twice its length; every vector $\ne\mathbf{0}$ is an eigenvector, and the eigenspace is the whole plane. The shear pushes every point to the right by its height; only arrows on the $x_1$-axis stay on their line. Its eigenspace is $\ker\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, the line in the direction of $(1,\,0)^{\top}$. The difference is the dimension of the eigenspace.

Definition 6.8 (Geometric multiplicity)

The geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue is the dimension of its eigenspace.

It is at least $1$, because every eigenvalue has an eigenvector, and at most as large as the algebraic multiplicity; this chapter does not prove the second part. For $2\mathbf{I}$ both multiplicities are $2$; for the shear the algebraic one is $2$ and the geometric one is $1$: the shear is missing an eigenvector.

Theorem 6.9 (Different eigenvalues, independent eigenvectors)

Eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_k$ for pairwise different eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k$ are linearly independent. If an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ different eigenvalues, its eigenvectors form a basis.

Proof: Independent eigenvectors

For two eigenvectors: let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1\mathbf{v}_1+c_2\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$. Applying $\mathbf{A}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1+c_2\lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$. Subtracting $\lambda_2$ times the first equation from this leaves $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$. Since $\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$ and $\mathbf{v}_1\ne\mathbf{0}$, we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ as well. For more eigenvectors, proceed in the same way: if there were a dependence, take one with as few nonzero coefficients as possible. Applying $\mathbf{A}$ and subtracting the dependence multiplied by $\lambda$, one of the eigenvalues involved, would give a dependence with even fewer nonzero coefficients, a contradiction.

Worked example

The eigenvectors $(1,\,1)^{\top}$ and $(1,\,-2)^{\top}$ of $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ belong to the different eigenvalues $5$ and $2$; they are linearly independent, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3\ne0}$. The shear, by contrast, has only the eigendirection $(1,\,0)^{\top}$; it does not have two independent eigenvectors.

⁠In short: The geometric multiplicity counts the independent eigenvectors for an eigenvalue; it can be smaller than the algebraic one, but different eigenvalues always have independent eigenvectors.

Rules for eigenvalues#

If you know the eigenvectors of a matrix, you also know them for many matrices built from it. The rules all follow directly from (6.1).

Theorem 6.10 (Rules)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{v}}$ with $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$. Then:

  1. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^k\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^k\mathbf{v}}$ for every natural number $k$;

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}+\beta\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\lambda+\beta)\mathbf{v}}$ for every number $\beta$;

  3. $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible if and only if no eigenvalue is zero, and then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1\lambda\mathbf{v}}$;

  4. $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ has the same eigenvalues as $\mathbf{A}$, but in general different eigenvectors.

Proof: Rules

(i) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(\lambda\mathbf{v})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2\mathbf{v}}$, and so on. (ii) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}+\beta\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{v}+\beta\mathbf{v}}$. (iii) By (6.5), $\det\mathbf{A}$ is the product of the eigenvalues, so it is nonzero exactly when no eigenvalue is zero. Multiplying $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{v}}$ by $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{v}}$. (iv) By Chapter 1, Section 14.5, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det(\mathbf{A}^{\top}-\lambda\mathbf{I})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det\bigl((\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})^{\top}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})}$; both have the same characteristic polynomial.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, the matrix $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}18&7\\14&11\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ has the eigenvalues $25$ and $4$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^2(1,\,1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(25,\,25)^{\top}}$. The matrix $\mathbf{A}-5\mathbf{I}$ has the eigenvalues $0$ and $-3$ and is not invertible. The inverse $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac1{10}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}3&-1\\-2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ has the eigenvalues $\tfrac15$ and $\tfrac12$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{-1}(1,\,-2)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac1{10}(5,\,-10)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12(1,\,-2)^{\top}}$. The transpose $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&2\\1&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ also has the eigenvalues $5$ and $2$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}(1,\,1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(6,\,4)^{\top}}$: its eigenvectors are $(2,\,1)^{\top}$ and $(1,\,-1)^{\top}$.

⁠In short: Powers, shifts by $\beta\mathbf{I}$ and the inverse keep the eigenvectors and change the eigenvalues in the same way; the transpose keeps only the eigenvalues.

Exercise 17 ★ Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Determine the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\3&1\end{pmatrix}.}$$

In each case, check the trace and the determinant. For which matrix are the eigenvectors perpendicular to each other?

Solution

$\mathbf{A}_1$: trace $5$, determinant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{6-2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda^2-5\lambda+4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\lambda-4)(\lambda-1)}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}_1-4\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-2&2\\1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, eigenspace in the direction of $(1,\,1)^{\top}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}_1-\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, eigenspace in the direction of $(2,\,-1)^{\top}$. Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4\cdot1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$. The eigenvectors are not perpendicular: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1,\,1)(2,\,-1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$.

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\mathbf{I}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3-\lambda)^2}$, the eigenvalue $3$ twice; the eigenspace is the whole plane. Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3+3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3\cdot3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9}$. You can choose two perpendicular eigenvectors, for example $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$, but also two oblique ones.

$\mathbf{A}_3$: trace $2$, determinant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1-9}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-8}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda^2-2\lambda-8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\lambda-4)(\lambda+2)}$. Eigenvectors $(1,\,1)^{\top}$ for $4$ and $(1,\,-1)^{\top}$ for $-2$. Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4-2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4\cdot(-2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-8}$. They are perpendicular to each other; $\mathbf{A}_3$ is symmetric, and Section 7.5 shows that this is no coincidence.

Exercise 18 ★ Triangular matrices

Show: the eigenvalues of an upper triangular matrix are its diagonal entries. Determine the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1&0\\0&3&1\\0&0&5\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)$.

Solution

For an upper triangular matrix, $\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}$ is again an upper triangular matrix, with the diagonal entries $a_{ii}-\lambda$. By Chapter 1, Section 14.4, its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(a_{11}-\lambda)\cdots(a_{nn}-\lambda)}$. The roots are the diagonal entries.

For the given matrix, the eigenvalues are therefore $2$, $3$ and $5$. For $2$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}-2\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&0\\0&1&1\\0&0&3\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)}$ requires $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, eigenvector $(1,\,0,\,0)^{\top}$. For $3$: $\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&1&0\\0&0&1\\0&0&2\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)$ requires $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{v_1}$, eigenvector $(1,\,1,\,0)^{\top}$. For $5$: $\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-3&1&0\\0&-2&1\\0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)$ requires $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3v_1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2v_2}$, eigenvector $(1,\,3,\,6)^{\top}$. Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}(1,\,3,\,6)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2+3,\ 9+6,\ 30)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\,(1,\,3,\,6)^{\top}}$.

Exercise 19 ★★ Multiplicities

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)}$, determine all eigenvalues with their algebraic and geometric multiplicities.

Solution

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\mathbf{I}}$: eigenvalue $2$, algebraic multiplicity $2$, eigenspace the whole plane, geometric multiplicity $2$.

$\mathbf{B}_2$: triangular matrix, eigenvalue $2$ with algebraic multiplicity $2$. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}_2-2\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&3\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ requires $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$; the eigenspace is the $x_1$-axis, geometric multiplicity $1$. Like the shear, $\mathbf{B}_2$ is missing an eigenvector.

$\mathbf{B}_3$: triangular matrix, eigenvalue $1$ with algebraic multiplicity $3$. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{B}_3-\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)}$ requires only $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{v_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$; the eigenspace is $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2\}$, geometric multiplicity $2$. Here the geometric multiplicity lies between $1$ and the algebraic one.

Exercise 20 ★★ A rotation and a reflection

  1. Compute the eigenvalues of the rotation by $30^\circ$ and write them in the form $\mathrm e^{\pm\mathrm j\alpha}$.

  2. The reflection $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0.6&0.8\\0.8&-0.6\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ from Section 1.5 is also orthogonal. Determine its eigenvalues and eigenvectors and interpret them geometrically.

  3. Show: every real eigenvalue of an orthogonal matrix is $1$ or $-1$.

Solution

(a) The rotation by $30^\circ$ is $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\sqrt3/2&-1/2\\1/2&\sqrt3/2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ with trace $\sqrt3$ and determinant $1$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2-\sqrt3\,\lambda+1}$ and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\sqrt3\pm\sqrt{3-4}}2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\sqrt3}2\pm\mathrm j\,\frac12}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\cos30^\circ\pm\mathrm j\sin30^\circ}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathrm e^{\pm\mathrm j\,30^\circ} .}$$

(b) Trace $0$, determinant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-0.36-0.64}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2-1}$ with the eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$. For $1$: $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-0.4&0.8\\0.8&-1.6\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, eigenvector $(2,\,1)^{\top}$. For $-1$: $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1.6&0.8\\0.8&0.4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, eigenvector $(1,\,-2)^{\top}$. Geometrically: the points of the axis of reflection in the direction of $(2,\,1)^{\top}$ stay fixed, and the eigenvalue is $1$; the direction perpendicular to it is flipped over, and the eigenvalue is $-1$. The two eigenvectors are perpendicular because the matrix is symmetric.

(c) Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{v}}$ with real $\lambda$ and real $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$. By (1.3), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{v}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|\lambda|\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\lambda|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm1}$. A rigid motion can only leave an arrow on its line as it is or flip it over; it cannot stretch it.

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues
Definition 6.1 (Eigenvector, eigenvalue)

Let $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$. A vector $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$ is called an eigenvector of $\mathbf{A}$ if there is a number $\lambda$ with

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\,\mathbf{v} .}$$
(6.1)

The number $\lambda$ is called the corresponding eigenvalue.

The characteristic polynomial
Definition 6.2 (Characteristic polynomial)

For $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})}$$
(6.2)

is called the characteristic polynomial of $\mathbf{A}$.

Theorem 6.3 (Eigenvalues are roots)

A number $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\mathbf{A}$ if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{p(\lambda)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\begin{pmatrix}a-\lambda&b\\c&d-\lambda\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^2-(a+d)\,\lambda+(ad-bc) .}$$
(6.3)
Computing eigenvectors
Definition 6.4 (Eigenspace)

The null space of $\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}$ is called the eigenspace of $\mathbf{A}$ for the eigenvalue $\lambda$.

Trace and determinant
Definition 6.5 (Trace)

The trace of a square matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is the sum of its diagonal entries:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_{11}+a_{22}+\dots+a_{nn}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^na_{ii} .}$$
(6.4)
Theorem 6.6 (Trace and determinant from the eigenvalues)

If $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$, counted with multiplicity and including complex ones, then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\dots+\lambda_n,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda_1\cdot\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_n .}$$
(6.5)

Moreover, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})}$ for $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$.

Multiple eigenvalues
Definition 6.7 (Algebraic multiplicity)

The algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

How many eigenvectors there are
Theorem 6.9 (Different eigenvalues, independent eigenvectors)

Eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_k$ for pairwise different eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k$ are linearly independent. If an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ different eigenvalues, its eigenvectors form a basis.

Rules for eigenvalues
Theorem 6.10 (Rules)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{v}}$ with $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$. Then:

  1. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^k\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda^k\mathbf{v}}$ for every natural number $k$;

  2. $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}+\beta\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\lambda+\beta)\mathbf{v}}$ for every number $\beta$;

  3. $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible if and only if no eigenvalue is zero, and then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1\lambda\mathbf{v}}$;

  4. $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ has the same eigenvalues as $\mathbf{A}$, but in general different eigenvectors.

Key relationships
  • On “Eigenvectors and eigenvalues”: An eigenvector is not rotated by the matrix, only multiplied by a number; this number is the eigenvalue.
  • On “The characteristic polynomial”: The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial $\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})$; counted with multiplicity and including complex ones, an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues.
  • On “Computing eigenvectors”: The eigenvectors for $\lambda$ are the solutions $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$ of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$; together with the zero vector they form the eigenspace.
  • On “Trace and determinant”: The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries and the sum of the eigenvalues; the determinant is the product of the eigenvalues.
  • On “Complex eigenvalues”: A real matrix can have complex eigenvalues, always in conjugate pairs; a rotation by $\alpha$ has the eigenvalues $\mathrm e^{\pm\mathrm j\alpha}$.
Show 3 more
  • On “Multiple eigenvalues”: The algebraic multiplicity counts how often an eigenvalue occurs as a root of the characteristic polynomial.
  • On “How many eigenvectors there are”: The geometric multiplicity counts the independent eigenvectors for an eigenvalue; it can be smaller than the algebraic one, but different eigenvalues always have independent eigenvectors.
  • On “Rules for eigenvalues”: Powers, shifts by $\beta\mathbf{I}$ and the inverse keep the eigenvectors and change the eigenvalues in the same way; the transpose keeps only the eigenvalues.
