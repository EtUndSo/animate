Quadratic forms

What this stage is about:

After the linear functions, the quadratic ones are the simplest functions of several variables. They describe squared distances, squared errors and curved surfaces. This section writes them with a matrix, shows the three basic shapes bowl, trough and saddle, and explains how to tell from the matrix which shape you have, as long as it has no cross terms.

Quadratic forms

In Chapter 1, Section 8.4, every linear function with one output was a weighted sum $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ of the components. The next simplest kind of function sums products of two components each: squares such as $x_1^2$ and cross terms such as $x_1x_2$. An example from everyday life is a walk along two straight roads that are not perpendicular to each other. Someone walks $x_1$ km in the direction of the unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ of the first road and then $x_2$ km in the direction of the unit vector $\mathbf{v}$ of the second; negative distances mean the opposite direction. They then stand at $x_1\mathbf{u}+x_2\mathbf{v}$, and by the rules of the dot product the square of their distance from the start is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert x_1\mathbf{u}+x_2\mathbf{v}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+2\,(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v})\,x_1x_2+x_2^2 .}$$

Such sums can be written with a matrix in the middle.

Definition 5.1 (Quadratic form)

For a square matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, the function

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{l=1}^na_{il}\,x_ix_l}$$
(5.1)

is called the quadratic form of $\mathbf{A}$.

The result is a number: $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ is a vector, and $\mathbf{x}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})$ is the dot product of $\mathbf{x}$ with it. Written out for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_{11}x_1^2+(a_{12}+a_{21})\,x_1x_2+a_{22}x_2^2 .}$$

If you double $\mathbf{x}$, then $f$ quadruples: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(c\,\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c^2f(\mathbf{x})}$. Hence the name.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5x_1^2+4x_1x_2+2x_2^2}$. At the point $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-1)^{\top}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5-4+2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$; as a check, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,0)^{\top}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$. Fig. 14 works out the double sum (5.1) at this point term by term. At the point $(1,\,1)^{\top}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11}$; at the origin, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. The squared distance of the walk is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\\\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\,\mathbf{x}}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1,\,x_2)^{\top}}$; the matrix is the Gram matrix of the two directions $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ from Chapter 1, Section 10. If the roads enclose the angle $60^\circ$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\cos60^\circ}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac12}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+x_1x_2+x_2^2}$: after $1$ km on each of the two roads, you are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt3}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.73}$ km from the start.

⁠In short: A quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ is a sum of squares and mixed products of the components, with the entries of $\mathbf{A}$ as weights.

Only the symmetric part counts

In the expression for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $a_{12}$ and $a_{21}$ occur only as a sum. The matrices $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}5&4\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ therefore have the same quadratic form. You may always pick the symmetric one.

Theorem 5.2 (The symmetric part)

For every square matrix $\mathbf{A}$ and every $\mathbf{x}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\Bigl(\frac{\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\top}}2\Bigr)\mathbf{x} .}$$
(5.2)
Proof: The symmetric part

The number $\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ equals its own transpose, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}$. The mean of the two expressions is $\mathbf{x}^{\top}\frac{\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\top}}2\mathbf{x}$.

The matrix $\frac12(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\top})$ is symmetric. In this chapter, a quadratic form therefore always has a symmetric matrix. You read it off as follows: the diagonal holds the coefficients of the squares, and each off-diagonal entry holds half the coefficient of the corresponding cross term.

Worked example

The form $3x_1^2-6x_1x_2+x_2^2$ has the symmetric matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}3&-3\\-3&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. The form $x_1^2+2x_2^2+4x_3^2+2x_1x_3$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ has the matrix $\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&1\\0&2&0\\1&0&4\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)$; the cross term $2x_1x_3$ is split between the entries $(1,3)$ and $(3,1)$.

⁠In short: A quadratic form sees only the symmetric part of its matrix; that is why it is always written with a symmetric matrix.

Bowl, trough and saddle

With two variables you can draw the quadratic form as a surface above the plane, with $f(\mathbf{x})$ as the height, and, as on a map, its contour lines, the lines of equal height. This is easiest when the matrix is a diagonal matrix. Then there are no cross terms, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a_{11}x_1^2+a_{22}x_2^2}$. Depending on the signs of the two entries, three basic shapes arise (Fig. 15).

  • ⁠Bowl: Both diagonal entries are positive, for example $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+2x_2^2}$. Then $f(\mathbf{x})>0$ for every $\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ only at the origin. The contour lines are ellipses around the origin, with their semi-axes on the coordinate axes.

  • ⁠Trough: One entry is positive, the other zero, for example $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2}$. Then $f\ge0$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ on the entire line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: there the surface has a straight valley floor. The contour lines are pairs of parallel lines.

  • ⁠Saddle: The entries have different signs, for example $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2-x_2^2}$. Along the $x_1$-axis the surface rises on both sides; along the $x_2$-axis it falls on both sides. The contour lines are hyperbolas.

If both entries are negative, the bowl is upside down; that is a dome. A negative and a zero entry give an upside-down trough, a ridge. With more than two variables the same holds: the signs of the diagonal entries decide the shape.

Worked example

For the bowl $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+2x_2^2}$, the contour line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ is the ellipse with the semi-axes $1$ on the $x_1$-axis and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1/\sqrt2}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.707}$ on the $x_2$-axis: at $(1,\,0)^{\top}$ and at $(0,\,1/\sqrt2)^{\top}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. For the saddle $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2-x_2^2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(1,\,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(0,\,1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(1,\,1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

⁠In short: Without cross terms, you read off the shape from the signs on the diagonal: all positive gives a bowl, positive and zero a trough, mixed signs a saddle.

Completing the square

With cross terms, you can no longer see the shape right away. Is $5x_1^2+4x_1x_2+2x_2^2$ a bowl? The cross term can become negative. From school you know a tool that removes cross terms: completing the square. It writes the form as a sum of squares, and from the coefficients of the squares you read off the shape as in the previous subsection.

Derivation: Completing the square in two variables

Let $a\ne0$. Factor $a$ out of the terms containing $x_1$ and complete the square:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{ax_1^2+2bx_1x_2+cx_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\Bigl(x_1^2+\frac{2b}ax_1x_2\Bigr)+cx_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\Bigl(x_1+\frac bax_2\Bigr)^2-\frac{b^2}ax_2^2+cx_2^2 .}$$

Hence

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{ax_1^2+2bx_1x_2+cx_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{a\Bigl(x_1+\frac bax_2\Bigr)^2+\Bigl(c-\frac{b^2}a\Bigr)x_2^2 .}$$
(5.3)

The right-hand side is a sum of two squares. The new variables $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1+\frac bax_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{z_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_2}$ take on every pair of numbers as $\mathbf{x}$ runs through the plane. So the form in the variables $z_1,z_2$ has no cross terms anymore, and the signs of $a$ and $c-b^2/a$ decide between bowl, trough and saddle.

Worked example 1

$5x_1^2+4x_1x_2+2x_2^2$ has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{5x_1^2+4x_1x_2+2x_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\,(x_1+0.4\,x_2)^2+1.2\,x_2^2 .}$$

Both coefficients are positive: the form is a bowl. Check at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-1)^{\top}}$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{5\cdot0.36+1.2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$.

Worked example 2

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1^2+6x_1x_2+x_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1+3x_2)^2-8x_2^2}$. The coefficients have different signs: a saddle. Indeed, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(1,\,0)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1>0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f(1,\,-1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1-6+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4<0}$.

⁠In short: Completing the square writes a quadratic form as a sum of squares; the signs of the coefficients of the squares show whether it is a bowl, a trough or a saddle.

BackgroundCompleting the square is Gaussian elimination

The numbers in (5.3) are the same as in Gaussian elimination from Chapter 1, Section 12.3, applied to the symmetric matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. To produce a zero below the diagonal, you subtract $\frac ba$ times the first row from the second and obtain $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\0&c-b^2/a\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. The diagonal entries of this echelon form, the so-called pivots, are exactly the coefficients of the squares, and the elimination factor $\frac ba$ appears in the first parenthesis. For the matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$: factor $0.4$, pivots $5$ and $1.2$. With $n$ variables you continue in the same way: each elimination step completes the square for one variable. The form is a bowl if and only if all pivots are positive. By the rules from Chapter 1, Section 14.5, the product of the pivots is the determinant, here $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{5\cdot1.2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5\cdot2-2\cdot2}$.

Exercise 14 ★ Forms and matrices

  1. Give the symmetric matrix of the forms $2x_1^2-2x_1x_2+3x_2^2$ and $x_1x_2$.

  2. Compute the form of $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and its symmetric matrix.

  3. Determine without calculation whether $3x_1^2+x_2^2$, $-x_1^2$ and $2x_1^2-x_2^2+x_3^2$ are a bowl, a trough, a saddle or something else.

Solution

(a) The diagonal holds the coefficients of the squares, the off-diagonal entries half the coefficient of the cross term: $2x_1^2-2x_1x_2+3x_2^2$ has the matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, and $x_1x_2$ has $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1/2\\1/2&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+(3-1)\,x_1x_2+2x_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2x_2^2}$. The symmetric matrix is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac12(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\top})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$.

(c) $3x_1^2+x_2^2$: both coefficients positive, a bowl. $-x_1^2$: one negative and one vanishing coefficient, so an upside-down trough, a ridge along the $x_2$-axis. $2x_1^2-x_2^2+x_3^2$: mixed signs, a saddle; along $x_1$ and $x_3$ the form rises, along $x_2$ it falls.

Exercise 15 ★★ Completing the square

Complete the square and determine the shape:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2x_1^2+8x_1x_2+11x_2^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+4x_1x_2+4x_2^2,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+4x_1x_2+x_2^2 .}$$

For the trough, give the line on which $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and for the saddle, a point with $f<0$.

Solution

With (5.3):

  • $f_1$: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{11}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2(x_1+2x_2)^2+(11-8)\,x_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2(x_1+2x_2)^2+3x_2^2}$. Both coefficients positive: a bowl.

  • $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1+2x_2)^2+0\cdot x_2^2}$: a trough. It is zero on the line $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1+2x_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, that is, in the direction of $(2,\,-1)^{\top}$.

  • $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(x_1+2x_2)^2-3x_2^2}$: different signs, a saddle. If you choose $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2}$, the first square vanishes: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{f_3(-2,\,1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4-8+1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-3<0}$.

Exercise 16 ★★ Two roads

For the walk along two straight roads with the directions $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ from Subsection 5.1, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1^2+2\,(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v})\,x_1x_2+x_2^2}$.

  1. Complete the square in $d^2$.

  2. For which values of $\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}$ is $d^2>0$ for all distances $(x_1,\,x_2)\ne(0,\,0)$? Why can $|\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}|$ never be greater than $1$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality from Chapter 1, Section 5.1, and what does $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ mean for the two roads?

Solution

(a) In (5.3), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b/a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{b^2/a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^2}$:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(x_1+(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v})\,x_2\bigr)^2+\bigl(1-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^2\bigr)\,x_2^2 .}$$

(b) The first coefficient is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{a}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1>0}$. $d^2>0$ for all distances $\ne(0,\,0)$ holds if and only if the second is positive as well: $1-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^2>0$, so $|\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}|<1$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}|\le\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, with equality if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\pm\mathbf{u}}$: when the two roads run parallel. Then the form would only be a trough: for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v})\,x_2}$, for instance $1$ km out and $1$ km back, you are standing at the start again. For two roads that really cross, $|\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}|<1$, and every walk with $(x_1,\,x_2)\ne(0,\,0)$ ends at a positive distance from the start.
