What this stage is about:

Some coordinate systems are especially convenient: their axes are perpendicular to one another, and the unit has the same length on every axis. In such a system you need no system of equations to find the coordinates of a point, and lengths follow from the Pythagorean theorem. This section introduces such coordinate systems and the matrices that belong to them.

Orthonormal vectors#

In Chapter 1, Section 5.3, two vectors were called orthogonal if their dot product is zero. The constant pattern $(1,1,1,1)^{\top}$ and the alternating pattern $(1,-1,1,-1)^{\top}$ were an example: comparing a time series with one pattern was not disturbed by the other pattern. The comparison did, however, return the share multiplied by $4$, because both patterns have length $2$. If orthogonal vectors also have length $1$, this factor disappears as well.

Picture the two axes $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ of the plane, rotated as a whole by some angle. The two arrows still have length $1$ and are still perpendicular to each other; they just point in other directions.

Definition 1.1 (Orthonormal)

Vectors $\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_k\in\mathbb{R}^n$ are called orthonormal if each of them has length $1$ and any two different ones are orthogonal:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{q}_l}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{cases}1, & i=l,\\ 0, & i\ne l.\end{cases}}$$
(1.1)

The word combines “orthogonal” and “normalized”. The unit vectors $\mathbf{e}_1,\dots,\mathbf{e}_n$ are the simplest example.

Theorem 1.2 (Orthonormal vectors are linearly independent)

If $\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_k$ are orthonormal, then they are linearly independent.

Proof: Linear independence

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1\mathbf{q}_1+\dots+c_k\mathbf{q}_k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$. Take the dot product of both sides with $\mathbf{q}_l$. By (1.1), only the term $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_l\,\mathbf{q}_l^{\top}\mathbf{q}_l}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c_l}$ remains on the left; the right side is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_l^{\top}\mathbf{0}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_l}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, and this holds for every $l$. By the definition in Chapter 1, Section 13.2, the vectors are therefore linearly independent.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0.6,\ 0.8)^{\top}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-0.8,\ 0.6)^{\top}}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{q}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.36+0.64}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_2^{\top}\mathbf{q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.64+0.36}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-0.48+0.48}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0 .}$$

The two vectors are orthonormal; Fig. 1 shows the last dot product step by step. The constant and the alternating pattern become orthonormal when you divide them by their length $2$: $\tfrac12(1,1,1,1)^{\top}$ and $\tfrac12(1,-1,1,-1)^{\top}$.

⁠In short: Orthonormal vectors have length $1$ and are pairwise perpendicular; they are always linearly independent.

Orthonormal bases#

By Chapter 1, Section 13.3, a basis is a coordinate system: every vector has exactly one representation in it. The most convenient bases are those whose vectors are orthonormal. They are like the familiar set of axes, only rotated.

Definition 1.3 (Orthonormal basis)

A basis of orthonormal vectors is called an orthonormal basis.

For all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ it is enough to find $n$ orthonormal vectors. By the theorem in the previous subsection they are linearly independent. If you write them as the columns of an $n\times n$ matrix, this matrix has rank $n$, and by Chapter 1, Section 13.5, its column space is all of $\mathbb{R}^n$. So the $n$ vectors span $\mathbb{R}^n$ and form a basis. In the same way you can look for an orthonormal basis of a span, say a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$: orthonormal vectors whose span is exactly the given one.

Worked example

The vectors $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0.6,\ 0.8)^{\top}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-0.8,\ 0.6)^{\top}}$ are two orthonormal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$, hence an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$. The unit vectors $\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2,\mathbf{e}_3$ form the orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ that is called the standard basis. The plane $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ has the orthonormal basis $\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2$.

⁠In short: An orthonormal basis is a rotated set of axes: $n$ orthonormal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ always form a basis.

Coordinates in an orthonormal basis#

In an ordinary basis you have to solve a system of equations to find the coordinates of a vector. In Chapter 1, Section 13.3, $(3,\,2)^{\top}$ had the coordinates $1$ and $2$ in the basis $(1,\,0)^{\top}$, $(1,\,1)^{\top}$. The dot products $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1,0)(3,2)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1,1)(3,2)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{5}$ are something entirely different. In an orthonormal basis, by contrast, the coordinates are exactly the dot products.

The reason is shown by the picture of the shadow length from Chapter 1, Section 5.5. For a normalized vector $\mathbf{q}$, the number $\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ is the length of the shadow of $\mathbf{x}$ on the line in the direction of $\mathbf{q}$. In an orthonormal basis, every coordinate is the shadow on its own axis (Fig. 2).

Theorem 1.4 (Coordinates in an orthonormal basis)

If $\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$, then for every vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n(\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{q}_i}$$
$$\text{and}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n(\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x})^2 .}$$
(1.2)
Proof: Coordinates in an orthonormal basis

Because the vectors form a basis, there are numbers $c_1,\dots,c_n$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_ic_i\mathbf{q}_i}$. By (1.1), the dot product with $\mathbf{q}_l$ gives

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_l^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^nc_i\,\mathbf{q}_l^{\top}\mathbf{q}_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{c_l ,}$$

since only the term with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{l}$ is nonzero. For the length, multiply out:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{l=1}^nc_i\,c_l\,\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{q}_l}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^nc_i^2 ,}$$

again because only the terms with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{l}$ remain.

The second formula is the Pythagorean theorem in $\mathbb{R}^n$: the length of an arrow comes out the same from its coordinates in every set of perpendicular axes, as long as the unit has length $1$ on all axes.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,1)^{\top}}$ and the basis from the previous subsection,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1.8+0.8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.6,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_2^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-2.4+0.6}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1.8 .}$$

Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{2.6\,(0.6,\ 0.8)^{\top}-1.8\,(-0.8,\ 0.6)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1.56+1.44,\ 2.08-1.08)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,1)^{\top}}$. And $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1^2+c_2^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{6.76+3.24}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{10}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2}$. Fig. 3 shows the three sums with fractions.

⁠In short: In an orthonormal basis, every coordinate is a dot product, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}}$, and the sum of the squared coordinates is the squared length.

BackgroundOscillations as an orthonormal basis, and Parseval's theorem

The four vectors

$$\begin{gathered} \mathbf{q}_1=\tfrac12(1,\,1,\,1,\,1)^{\top},\qquad \mathbf{q}_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,\,0,\,-1,\,0)^{\top},\\ \mathbf{q}_3=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(0,\,1,\,0,\,-1)^{\top},\qquad \mathbf{q}_4=\tfrac12(1,\,-1,\,1,\,-1)^{\top} \end{gathered}$$

are the values of a constant, of a cosine and a sine oscillation with one period of four steps, and of an oscillation that changes sign at every step, each normalized. It is easy to check that they are orthonormal. So they form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^4$. Every time series of four values, such as the temperature at four times of day, is a sum of these four oscillations, and by (1.2) the contributions are simply dot products. For the time series $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(4,\,2,\,0,\,2)^{\top}}$ we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_2^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4/\sqrt2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\sqrt2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_3^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_4^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$: the time series consists only of a constant and a cosine.

The second formula in (1.2) says that the sum of squares $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{24}$ of the time series equals the sum of the squared contributions, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{16+8+0+0}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{24}$. In mathematics this statement is called Parseval's theorem. The discrete Fourier transform is the same decomposition with complex oscillations; with it, for example, a music app breaks a sound down into its tones. Its matrix for $N$ values preserves lengths exactly when it is normalized by $1/\sqrt N$; without this factor, Parseval's theorem contains an additional factor $N$.

Orthogonal matrices#

If you write the vectors of an orthonormal basis as the columns of a matrix $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_1&\cdots&\mathbf{q}_n\end{pmatrix}}$, both formulas (1.2) can be written as matrix products. By the row view from Chapter 1, Section 7.2, all coordinates at once form the vector $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{c}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}$, because the rows of $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}$ are the vectors $\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}$. Conversely, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_ic_i\mathbf{q}_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{c}}$ by the column view. So the matrix $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}$ undoes what $\mathbf{Q}$ does.

Definition 1.5 (Orthogonal matrix)

A square matrix $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ whose columns are orthonormal is called an orthogonal matrix. Equivalently: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$.

The two formulations are equivalent because the entry $(i,l)$ of $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}$ is the dot product $\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{q}_l$ of columns $i$ and $l$. So the condition $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$ is exactly (1.1).

Theorem 1.6 (The inverse of an orthogonal matrix)

If $\mathbf{Q}$ is orthogonal, then $\mathbf{Q}$ is invertible with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}$. Moreover, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$; so the rows of $\mathbf{Q}$ are orthonormal too, and $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}$ is orthogonal as well.

Proof: Inverse of an orthogonal matrix

The columns are orthonormal, so by the first subsection they are linearly independent. By the theorem in Chapter 1, Section 13.5, $\mathbf{Q}$ is therefore invertible. Multiplying $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$ from the right by $\mathbf{Q}^{-1}$ gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}$, and hence also $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$.

A word about the name. An orthogonal matrix has orthonormal columns, not merely orthogonal ones; the name has grown historically. There is no special name for matrices whose columns are only orthogonal but not normalized. For them, $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}$ is a diagonal matrix with the squared lengths of the columns on the diagonal, but not $\mathbf{I}$.

Worked example

With the vectors from the first subsection as columns,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}0.6&-0.8\\0.8&0.6\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}0.36+0.64&-0.48+0.48\\-0.48+0.48&0.64+0.36\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I} .}$$

The entries of $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}$ are the dot products of the columns from the first subsection; Fig. 1 shows the off-diagonal entry. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}$, without any calculation. This matrix is the rotation matrix from Chapter 1, Section 8.3, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\cos\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.6}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sin\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0.8}$, that is, a counterclockwise rotation by $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\alpha}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{53.13^\circ}$. The inverse $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}$ rotates back by the same angle.

⁠In short: An orthogonal matrix is square with orthonormal columns; its inverse is simply its transpose.

Lengths and angles are preserved#

A rotation changes neither the length of an arrow nor the angle between two arrows. All orthogonal matrices have this property, and it is the reason why people like to compute with them so much.

Theorem 1.7 (Orthogonal matrices preserve dot products)

If $\mathbf{Q}$ is orthogonal, then for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^n$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x})^{\top}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert ,}$$
(1.3)

and the angle between $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}$ is the same as the angle between $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$. The determinant is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$.

Proof: Preservation of dot products

By the rule $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}$ from Chapter 1, Section 10.1, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x})^{\top}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{y}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{y}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}}$ the statement about the length follows, and because the angle is computed only from dot products and lengths, it stays the same too. For the determinant, use the rules from Chapter 1, Section 14.5: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\cdot\det\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\det\mathbf{Q})^2}$.

So an orthogonal matrix moves space rigidly, without distorting it. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, it is a rotation. If $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$, it contains a reflection, because by Chapter 1, Section 14.2, a negative determinant reverses the orientation.

Worked example

The rotation from the previous subsection maps $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,1)^{\top}}$ to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1.8-0.8,\ 2.4+0.6)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,3)^{\top}}$. Both vectors have the squared length $10$. The determinant of the rotation is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{0.36+0.64}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$. The matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0.6&0.8\\0.8&-0.6\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ also has orthonormal columns, but the determinant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{-0.36-0.64}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$: it reflects across the line in the direction of $(2,\,1)^{\top}$, because it maps $(2,\,1)^{\top}$ to itself and $(-1,\,2)^{\top}$ to $(1,\,-2)^{\top}$.

⁠In short: Orthogonal matrices preserve dot products, lengths and angles; they rotate or reflect, and their determinant is $\pm1$.

BackgroundWhy numerical analysts like to compute with orthogonal matrices

A computer works with rounded numbers, so every intermediate result carries a small error $\boldsymbol{\delta}$. When you multiply such an intermediate result by a matrix, the error is multiplied by it as well. An arbitrary matrix can enlarge the error in the process; an orthogonal one cannot, because by (1.3) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert}$. Computations that consist only of multiplications by orthogonal matrices therefore do not let rounding errors grow. For the same reason, the inverse is no problem: you do not have to compute it, the transpose suffices, and it is exact.

Matrices with orthonormal columns#

Often you have fewer orthonormal vectors than the space has dimensions. A plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$ has an orthonormal basis of two vectors. If you write them as the columns of a matrix, it is not square but has three rows and two columns. Some of the rules carry over, some do not, and the difference matters.

Theorem 1.8 (Rectangular matrices with orthonormal columns)

If $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$ has orthonormal columns and $k<n$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}_k}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{c}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{c}\rVert}$ for every $\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^k$. The matrix $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, on the other hand, is not the identity matrix.

Proof: Rectangular matrices

The first two statements follow from (1.1) exactly as for square matrices. For the third: the matrix $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}$ has $k$ rows and $n$ columns. By the rank–nullity theorem from Chapter 1, Section 13.5, its null space has dimension $n-\operatorname{rank}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\ge n-k\ge1$. So there is a vector $\mathbf{z}\ne\mathbf{0}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$, and for it $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{z}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}\ne\mathbf{z}}$. Hence $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\ne\mathbf{I}$.

Worked example 1

The matrix with the single column $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0.6,\ 0.8)^{\top}}$ satisfies $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, but

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}0.36&0.48\\0.48&0.64\end{pmatrix}\ne\mathbf{I} .}$$

It maps $(-0.8,\ 0.6)^{\top}$ to $\mathbf{0}$. It maps the vector $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,1)^{\top}}$ to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1.08+0.48,\ 1.44+0.64)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1.56,\ 2.08)^{\top}}$. This is exactly the endpoint of the shadow of $\mathbf{x}$ from Chapter 1, Section 5.5, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}\,(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{x})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2.6\,\mathbf{q}}$.

Worked example 2

The columns of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)}$ are $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}_2}$, but $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{diag}(1,\,1,\,0)}$. This matrix keeps the first two components and sets the third to zero.

⁠In short: For a rectangular matrix with orthonormal columns, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$, but $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\ne\mathbf{I}$: the product in this order throws away part of the vector.

Exercise 1 ★ Orthonormality and coordinates

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13(1,\,2,\,2)^{\top}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13(2,\,1,\,-2)^{\top}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13(2,\,-2,\,1)^{\top}}$.

  1. Check that the three vectors are orthonormal.

  2. Compute the coordinates of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,0,\,3)^{\top}}$ in this orthonormal basis and verify the result. Check the formula for $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$.

Solution

(a) Form all six dot products; the common factor $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac13}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac19}$ is placed in front:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{q}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac19(1+4+4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,\quad \mathbf{q}_2^{\top}\mathbf{q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac19(4+1+4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,\quad \mathbf{q}_3^{\top}\mathbf{q}_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac19(4+4+1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1,}$$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac19(2+2-4)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,\quad \mathbf{q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{q}_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac19(2-4+2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,\quad \mathbf{q}_2^{\top}\mathbf{q}_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac19(4-2-2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0 .}$$

All three have length $1$ and are pairwise perpendicular: they are orthonormal, and by Section 1.2 they therefore form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

(b) By (1.2), the coordinates are dot products, with no system of equations:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13(3+0+6)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_2^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13(6+0-6)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_3^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13(6+0+3)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3 .}$$

Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3\mathbf{q}_1+0\cdot\mathbf{q}_2+3\mathbf{q}_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,2,\,2)^{\top}+(2,\,-2,\,1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(3,\,0,\,3)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}}$. Length formula: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1^2+c_2^2+c_3^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9+0+9}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{18}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{9+0+9}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{18}$.

Exercise 2 ★ An orthogonal matrix

Let $\mathbf{Q}$ be the matrix with the columns $\mathbf{q}_1,\mathbf{q}_2,\mathbf{q}_3$ from Exercise 1.

  1. Give $\mathbf{Q}^{-1}$ without computing. Check $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$ for an off-diagonal entry of your choice.

  2. Compute $\mathbf{Q}\,(1,\,1,\,1)^{\top}$ and compare the lengths. Compute $\det\mathbf{Q}$. Does $\mathbf{Q}$ rotate or reflect?

Solution

(a) The columns are orthonormal, so $\mathbf{Q}$ is orthogonal and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}$, without any computation. Here

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac13\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\2&1&-2\\2&-2&1\end{pmatrix}}$$

is even symmetric, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}}$. The entry $(1,2)$ of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}$ is the dot product of the first row with the second: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac19(1\cdot2+2\cdot1+2\cdot(-2))}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

(b) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\,(1,\,1,\,1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13(5,\,1,\,1)^{\top}}$ with the squared length $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac19(25+1+1)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$, exactly the squared length of $(1,\,1,\,1)^{\top}$, as (1.3) requires. The determinant is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac1{27}\bigl(1\cdot(1-4)-2\cdot(2+4)+2\cdot(-4-2)\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac1{27}(-3-12-12)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1 .}$$

So $\mathbf{Q}$ reflects. More precisely: because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}$, we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$; applied twice, it brings everything back. The vector $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{k}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-1,\,-1)^{\top}}$ is flipped over, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{k}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac13(1-2-2,\ 2-1+2,\ 2+2-1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-1,\,1,\,1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mathbf{k}}$, and the vectors perpendicular to $\mathbf{k}$ stay fixed. $\mathbf{Q}$ is the reflection across the plane $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_1-x_2-x_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Exercise 3 ★★ Computing with orthogonal matrices

  1. Show: if $\mathbf{Q}_1$ and $\mathbf{Q}_2$ are orthogonal, then $\mathbf{Q}_1\mathbf{Q}_2$ is orthogonal too.

  2. Is the sum of two orthogonal matrices orthogonal again? Justify your answer or give a counterexample.

  3. Show that a diagonal matrix is orthogonal if and only if every diagonal entry is $1$ or $-1$. What does such a matrix do geometrically?

Solution

(a) With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}$, we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{Q}_1\mathbf{Q}_2)^{\top}(\mathbf{Q}_1\mathbf{Q}_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}_2^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_1^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_1\mathbf{Q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}_2^{\top}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{Q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$. Intuitively: two rigid motions in succession are again a rigid motion.

(b) No. Counterexample: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{I}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\mathbf{I}}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(2\mathbf{I})^{\top}(2\mathbf{I})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4\mathbf{I}\ne\mathbf{I}}$; the sum doubles all lengths. Likewise, $\mathbf{I}+(-\mathbf{I})$ is the zero matrix.

(c) For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n)}$, we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{diag}(d_1^2,\dots,d_n^2)}$. This is $\mathbf{I}$ if and only if every $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_i^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, that is, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$. Such a matrix reverses the axes with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$ and leaves the others alone: it reflects across the coordinate planes. For example, $\operatorname{diag}(1,\,-1)$ reflects across the $x_1$-axis, and $\operatorname{diag}(-1,\,-1)$ is the rotation by $180^\circ$.

Orthonormal vectors
Definition 1.1 (Orthonormal)

Vectors $\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_k\in\mathbb{R}^n$ are called orthonormal if each of them has length $1$ and any two different ones are orthogonal:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{q}_l}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{cases}1, & i=l,\\ 0, & i\ne l.\end{cases}}$$
(1.1)
Theorem 1.2 (Orthonormal vectors are linearly independent)

If $\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_k$ are orthonormal, then they are linearly independent.

Coordinates in an orthonormal basis
Theorem 1.4 (Coordinates in an orthonormal basis)

If $\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$, then for every vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n(\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{q}_i}$$
$$\text{and}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{i=1}^n(\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x})^2 .}$$
(1.2)
Orthogonal matrices
Definition 1.5 (Orthogonal matrix)

A square matrix $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ whose columns are orthonormal is called an orthogonal matrix. Equivalently: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$.

Theorem 1.6 (The inverse of an orthogonal matrix)

If $\mathbf{Q}$ is orthogonal, then $\mathbf{Q}$ is invertible with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}$. Moreover, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$; so the rows of $\mathbf{Q}$ are orthonormal too, and $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}$ is orthogonal as well.

Lengths and angles are preserved
Theorem 1.7 (Orthogonal matrices preserve dot products)

If $\mathbf{Q}$ is orthogonal, then for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^n$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x})^{\top}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert ,}$$
(1.3)

and the angle between $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}$ is the same as the angle between $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$. The determinant is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ or $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-1}$.

Matrices with orthonormal columns
Theorem 1.8 (Rectangular matrices with orthonormal columns)

If $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$ has orthonormal columns and $k<n$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}_k}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{c}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{c}\rVert}$ for every $\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^k$. The matrix $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, on the other hand, is not the identity matrix.

Summary

Terms

  • orthonormal basis – A basis of orthonormal vectors is called an orthonormal basis.
  • orthogonal matrix – A square matrix $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ whose columns are orthonormal is called an orthogonal matrix.

Key relationships
  • On “Orthonormal vectors”: Orthonormal vectors have length $1$ and are pairwise perpendicular; they are always linearly independent.
  • On “Orthonormal bases”: An orthonormal basis is a rotated set of axes: $n$ orthonormal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ always form a basis.
  • On “Coordinates in an orthonormal basis”: In an orthonormal basis, every coordinate is a dot product, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}}$, and the sum of the squared coordinates is the squared length.
  • On “Orthogonal matrices”: An orthogonal matrix is square with orthonormal columns; its inverse is simply its transpose.
  • On “Lengths and angles are preserved”: Orthogonal matrices preserve dot products, lengths and angles; they rotate or reflect, and their determinant is $\pm1$.
  • On “Matrices with orthonormal columns”: For a rectangular matrix with orthonormal columns, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$, but $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\ne\mathbf{I}$: the product in this order throws away part of the vector.
