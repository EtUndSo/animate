Key points so far

  • Orthonormal vectors: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$; coordinates are dot products $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}}$; orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles. If $\mathbf{Q}$ is not square, then $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\ne\mathbf{I}$.

  • Projection onto a line: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{a}}\,\mathbf{a}}$; the remainder is perpendicular to $\mathbf{a}$, and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is the closest point. Projection matrices satisfy $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{P}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{P}}$, orthogonal ones also $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{P}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{P}}$.

  • Best approximate solution: the residual $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{r}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}$ is perpendicular to the column space, hence $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}$; unique exactly when the columns are independent.

  • Projection onto a subspace: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{P}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}$; Gram–Schmidt yields $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}}$, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}$.

  • Quadratic forms $\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ depend only on the symmetric part; bowl, trough and saddle are recognized by completing the square.

  • Eigenvalues: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda\mathbf{v}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$; trace $=\sum\lambda_i$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\prod\lambda_i}$; rotations have complex eigenvalues.

  • $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{S}^{-1}}$ decouples, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^k}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^k\mathbf{S}^{-1}}$. Symmetric: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}$ with real $\lambda_i$ and perpendicular principal axes, semi-axes $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

  • Positive definite $\Leftrightarrow$ all eigenvalues $>0$ $\Leftrightarrow$ all pivots $>0$ $\Leftrightarrow$ all leading principal minors $>0$; $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ is positive semidefinite; Cholesky $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf L\mathbf L^{\top}}$.

  • Singular value decomposition $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_jd_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}}$ for every matrix: rotate, stretch, rotate; the $d_j^2$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1}$.

  • $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_F^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_jd_j^2}$; $\mathbf{A}_k$ is the best approximation of rank $k$, with the error $d_{k+1}$ in the spectral norm.

  • $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^+}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}^+\mathbf{U}^{\top}}$ delivers the shortest best approximate solution; special cases $(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}$.

  • $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\kappa(\mathbf{A})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1/d_n}$ bounds the amplification of relative errors; $1/\kappa$ is the relative distance to singularity; $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\kappa(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\kappa(\mathbf{A})^2}$.

The answer in three sentences

  1. A system of equations without a solution is best solved by projecting the right-hand side perpendicularly onto the column space: the residual is then perpendicular to all columns, the normal equations follow from this, and by Pythagoras no other point is closer.

  2. Eigenvectors are the directions that a matrix only stretches; in their basis the matrix becomes diagonal, and for symmetric matrices they form a set of perpendicular axes from which you read off the shape of every quadratic form.

  3. Every matrix is a sum, ordered by size, of building blocks $d_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}$ of rank $1$: the first $k$ give the best approximation of rank $k$, and the ratio $d_1/d_n$ tells how strongly errors in the data can distort the solution.

One principle behind all the results. Almost everything in this chapter follows from one principle: in a set of perpendicular axes, a squared length splits into independent parts by Pythagoras, and the shortest path to a line, a plane or a subspace is the perpendicular one.
