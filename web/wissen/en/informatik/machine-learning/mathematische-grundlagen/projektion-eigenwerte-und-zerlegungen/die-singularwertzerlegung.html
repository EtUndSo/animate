What this stage is about:

Eigenvectors exist only for square matrices, and they are perpendicular to each other only for symmetric ones. This section shows that every matrix, even a rectangular one, has a pair of sets of perpendicular axes: one in the space of the inputs and one in the space of the outputs. The matrix maps each axis of the one onto a multiple of an axis of the other. This yields a decomposition of every matrix into rotation, stretch and rotation.

Perpendicular directions that stay perpendicular#

The matrix $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, being a triangular matrix, has the double eigenvalue $2$, but only one eigendirection: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}-2\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$ means $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3v_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so only multiples of $\mathbf{e}_1$ come into question (Exercise 19). By Section 7.2, it is not diagonalizable. Nevertheless, it turns the unit circle into an ellipse (Fig. 23), and an ellipse has two perpendicular axes. Which arrows of the circle land on these axes?

What we are looking for is less than an eigenvector: an arrow may change its direction. We are looking for two perpendicular unit vectors $\mathbf{v}_1$, $\mathbf{v}_2$ whose images $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_2$ are again perpendicular to each other. Their dot product tells whether two images are perpendicular:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1)^{\top}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_2)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}_1^{\top}\,\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{v}_2 .}$$

Here the Gram matrix $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ appears. It is symmetric even if $\mathbf{A}$ is not, and by the spectral theorem it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. If $\mathbf{v}_2$ is an eigenvector of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ for the eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ and $\mathbf{v}_1$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}_2$, then the dot product is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda_2\,\mathbf{v}_1^{\top}\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. So the eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ do what we want. The same calculation with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}_2}$ gives the lengths of the images: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_i\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}_i^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lambda_i}$.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}4&6\\6&13\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ with the trace $17$ and the determinant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{52-36}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16}$. The characteristic polynomial $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda^2-17\lambda+16}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\lambda-16)(\lambda-1)}$ has the roots $16$ and $1$. Normalized eigenvectors are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,2)^{\top}/\sqrt5}$ for $16$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-2,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt5}$ for $1$. Their images

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}8\\4\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\end{pmatrix}}$$

have the dot product $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tfrac15(-8+8)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and the lengths $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{80/5}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{16}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt1}$ (Fig. 22).

⁠In short: The eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ are perpendicular directions whose images under $\mathbf{A}$ are again perpendicular to each other; the images have the lengths $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

Singular values#

The lengths of the images are the most important characteristic numbers of a matrix: they tell how strongly the matrix stretches in its principal directions. By Section 8.4, $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ is positive semidefinite, so its eigenvalues are not negative, and you may take the square root.

Definition 9.1 (Singular values)

Let $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, and let $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots\ge\lambda_n\ge0$ be the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, sorted in descending order, with multiple ones counted according to their multiplicity. The numbers

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{\lambda_j},}$$
$$d_1\ge d_2\ge\dots\ge d_n\ge0 ,$$
(9.1)

are called the singular values of $\mathbf{A}$.

Many books write $\sigma_j$ instead of $d_j$. Here the singular values always carry an index; the $d$ without an index from Chapter 1, the number of features, is something else.

Theorem 9.2 (Singular values and rank)

The number of singular values that are not zero is the rank of $\mathbf{A}$.

Proof: Singular values and rank

Let $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$ be orthonormal eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ for $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Every vector is $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_jc_j\mathbf{v}_j}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_jc_j\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}$: the column space is spanned by the images $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_n$. By the previous subsection, these are pairwise perpendicular and have the lengths $d_j$. The images with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ are the zero vector and contribute nothing. The others are perpendicular to each other and nonzero; divided by their lengths, they are orthonormal and, by the theorem in Section 1.1, linearly independent. They form a basis of the column space, and their number is the rank.

In what follows, the rank of a matrix is denoted by $r$. We have $d_1\ge\dots\ge d_r>0$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_{r+1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\dots}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$.

Worked example

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ has the singular values $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$ and rank $2$. The eigenvalues are $2$ and $2$: singular values and eigenvalues are in general different numbers. The matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}5&10\\10&20\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ with the eigenvalues $25$ and $0$, hence the singular values $5$ and $0$ and rank $1$.

⁠In short: The singular values $d_j$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$; they measure how strongly $\mathbf{A}$ stretches in its principal directions, and the number of positive ones is the rank.

Singular vectors#

Each positive singular value comes with two directions: an input direction $\mathbf{v}_j$ and the direction of its image.

Definition 9.3 (Singular vectors)

Orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$ of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ for the eigenvalues $d_1^2,\dots,d_n^2$ are called right singular vectors of $\mathbf{A}$. For $d_j>0$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}{d_j},}$$
$$\text{so}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\mathbf{u}_j ,}$$
(9.2)

is called the corresponding left singular vector.

The names come from the position the vectors occupy in the decomposition of the next subsection. The left singular vectors are again orthonormal, because

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_i^{\top}\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{v}_i^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}{d_id_j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{d_j^2\,\mathbf{v}_i^{\top}\mathbf{v}_j}{d_id_j},}$$

and this is $1$ for $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{i}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{j}$ and $0$ otherwise. They are the eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}$, and the equation in the opposite direction holds:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}{d_j}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\mathbf{v}_j,}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j^2\,\mathbf{u}_j .}$$

$\mathbf{A}$ maps $\mathbf{v}_j$ to $d_j\mathbf{u}_j$, and $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ maps $\mathbf{u}_j$ to $d_j\mathbf{v}_j$.

Caution: The signs belong together

An eigenvector is determined only up to its sign. If you compute the $\mathbf{v}_j$ as eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ and, separately, the $\mathbf{u}_j$ as eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}$, a $\mathbf{u}_j$ may end up with the wrong sign, and then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-d_j\mathbf{u}_j}$. The safe way is via (9.2): first choose $\mathbf{v}_j$, then compute $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j/d_j}$. If you flip $\mathbf{v}_j$ and $\mathbf{u}_j$ together, everything stays correct.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1/4}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt5}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_2/1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-1,\,2)^{\top}/\sqrt5}$. As a check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}13&6\\6&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ maps $(2,\,1)^{\top}$ to $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(32,\,16)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{16\,(2,\,1)^{\top}}$. The vector $(-2,\,-1)^{\top}/\sqrt5$ would also be a normalized eigenvector of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ for $16$, but with it, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-4\,\mathbf{u}_1}$.

⁠In short: The right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_j$ are eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, the left ones are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j/d_j}$; both families are orthonormal, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\mathbf{u}_j}$.

The decomposition#

Writing the equations $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\mathbf{u}_j}$ for all $j$ side by side produces a decomposition of the whole matrix.

Theorem 9.4 (Singular value decomposition)

Every matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$ can be written as

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$$
(9.3)

with orthogonal matrices $\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ and a matrix $\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ that carries the singular values $d_1\ge\dots\ge d_r>0$ on its diagonal and only zeros elsewhere. The columns of $\mathbf{V}$ are right singular vectors, and the first $r$ columns of $\mathbf{U}$ are the corresponding left ones. An equivalent form is the compact form

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j=1}^rd_j\,\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top} .}$$
(9.4)
Proof: Singular value decomposition

For $j\le r$ we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\mathbf{u}_j}$. For $j>r$ we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$. If $r<m$, extend $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r$ to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^m$, for instance with the Gram–Schmidt process as in the proof of the spectral theorem. By Chapter 1, Section 9.2, column $j$ of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}$ is the vector $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j$, and column $j$ of $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}$ is $d_j\mathbf{u}_j$ for $j\le r$ and the zero vector otherwise. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}}$. Because $\mathbf{V}$ is orthogonal, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{I}}$, and multiplying by $\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ from the right gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$. The compact form follows from Chapter 1, Section 9.5: column $j$ of $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}$ times row $j$ of $\mathbf{V}^{\top}$, summed over $j$; only the terms with $j\le r$ are nonzero.

The shape of $\mathbf{D}$ follows the shape of $\mathbf{A}$. For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{m}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$, for example, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}d_1&0\\0&d_2\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)}$. The usual abbreviation is SVD, for singular value decomposition.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\underbrace{\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{D}}\underbrace{\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{V}^{\top}},}$$

because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac1{\sqrt5}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}8&-1\\4&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac15\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}8+2&16-1\\4-4&8+2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$. In compact form:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4\cdot\frac15\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\1&2\end{pmatrix}+1\cdot\frac15\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-4&2\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}1.6&3.2\\0.8&1.6\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0.4&-0.2\\-0.8&0.4\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.}$$

⁠In short: Every matrix is $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ with two orthogonal matrices and the singular values on the diagonal of $\mathbf{D}$, or, equivalently, a sum $\sum_jd_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}$.

BackgroundWhy the decomposition exists for every matrix

Not every matrix has eigenvectors, and its eigenvectors are not always perpendicular. The singular value decomposition gets around both obstacles with a trick: it does not use the eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A}$ but those of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$. This matrix is symmetric and positive semidefinite for every $\mathbf{A}$, and for it the spectral theorem guarantees an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with eigenvalues $\ge0$. The price: you need two bases instead of one, one for the inputs and one for the outputs.

The singular vectors can also be described without $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$. By (8.2), applied to $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, every unit vector satisfies $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\le\lambda_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1^2}$, with equality at $\mathbf{v}_1$. So $\mathbf{v}_1$ is the direction that $\mathbf{A}$ stretches most, and $d_1$ is this largest stretch. Among all unit vectors perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}_1$, the one stretched most is $\mathbf{v}_2$, and so on; this is shown by the same weighted-mean calculation as in the proof of (8.2), with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$. So the decomposition lists the directions in order, from the strongest to the weakest stretch.

The singular values are unique. If all $d_j$ are different and positive, each pair $\mathbf{v}_j,\mathbf{u}_j$ is fixed up to a common sign. With equal singular values there is more freedom: an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ has all singular values equal to $1$, and for every orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{V}$, the factorization $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{Q}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{V})\,\mathbf{I}\,\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$ is a singular value decomposition.

Rotation, stretch, rotation#

Reading $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\bigl(\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{x})\bigr)}$ from right to left, every matrix acts in three steps (Fig. 23):

  1. By (1.2), $\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ computes the coordinates $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{c_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x}}$ of $\mathbf{x}$ in the basis of the $\mathbf{v}_j$. Geometrically, this is a rotation or reflection that puts $\mathbf{v}_j$ onto $\mathbf{e}_j$.

  2. $\mathbf{D}$ multiplies each coordinate $c_j$ by $d_j$. If $m>n$, $\mathbf{D}$ appends zeros; if $m<n$, $\mathbf{D}$ drops the last coordinates. The unit circle becomes an ellipse whose axes lie on the coordinate axes.

  3. $\mathbf{U}$ puts $\mathbf{e}_j$ onto $\mathbf{u}_j$, again a rotation or reflection.

So every linear map is, up to reflections, a rotation, a stretch along the axes and another rotation. It turns the unit circle into an ellipse and the unit sphere into an ellipsoid, with the semi-axes $d_j\mathbf{u}_j$. If $\det\mathbf{A}<0$, at least one reflection is involved, because by Chapter 1, Section 14.5, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det\mathbf{U}\cdot\det\mathbf{D}\cdot\det\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$, and $\det\mathbf{D}\ge0$.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{e}_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, the coordinates are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(2,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt5}$. Stretched, this gives $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(8,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt5}$, and rotated,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac15\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}8\\1\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac15\begin{pmatrix}15\\10\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_2 .}$$

$\mathbf{V}$ has the columns $(\cos\theta,\,\sin\theta)^{\top}$ and $(-\sin\theta,\,\cos\theta)^{\top}$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\cos\theta}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1/\sqrt5}$, so it is the rotation by $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\theta}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{63.4^\circ}$, and $\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ rotates back by $-63.4^\circ$. $\mathbf{U}$ is the rotation by $26.6^\circ$, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\cos26.6^\circ}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2/\sqrt5}$.

⁠In short: Every matrix rotates, stretches along the axes and rotates again, up to reflections; the unit circle becomes an ellipse with the semi-axes $d_j\mathbf{u}_j$.

Building blocks of rank 1 and the spaces of a matrix#

Each term $d_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}$ of the compact form (9.4) has rank $1$, because all its columns are multiples of $\mathbf{u}_j$. It acts on a vector as follows:

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\,(\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{u}_j .}$$

It reads off the coordinate of $\mathbf{x}$ along $\mathbf{v}_j$, multiplies it by $d_j$ and outputs the result in the direction of $\mathbf{u}_j$. The matrix is the sum of $r$ such building blocks, ordered by their size $d_j$. Along the way, the decomposition provides orthonormal bases for the subspaces that belong to a matrix.

Theorem 9.5 (What the singular vectors span)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$ with rank $r$. Then:

  1. $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r$ is an orthonormal basis of the column space of $\mathbf{A}$, and $\mathbf{u}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_m$ is an orthonormal basis of its orthogonal complement;

  2. $\mathbf{v}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$ is an orthonormal basis of the null space of $\mathbf{A}$, and $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_r$ is an orthonormal basis of the column space of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$.

Proof: Spaces

(i) Every $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j\le r}d_j(\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}_j}$ lies in the span of $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r$, and conversely $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{v}_j/d_j)}$ is an image. The remaining columns of $\mathbf{U}$ are perpendicular to these, so by Section 4.4 they span the orthogonal complement. (ii) Because the $\mathbf{u}_j$ are independent and the $d_j$ positive, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{0}}$ if and only if $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ for all $j\le r$, that is, if $\mathbf{x}$ has coordinates only along $\mathbf{v}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$. Finally, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\top}}$ is a singular value decomposition of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ in which $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ swap roles; (i) for $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ gives the rest.

The numbers match the rank–nullity theorem from Chapter 1, Section 13.5: the null space has dimension $n-r$.

Worked example

The matrix $\mathbf{H}$ with the columns $(1,\,0,\,1)^{\top}$ and $(0,\,1,\,1)^{\top}$ from Section 3.2 has $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ with the eigenvalues $3$ and $1$, hence the singular values $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt3}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{1}$, and the right singular vectors $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$. The left ones are

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_1}{\sqrt3}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\2\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_2}{1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}.}$$

They form an orthonormal basis of the plane $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{x_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{x_1+x_2}$, a different one from that in Section 4.3. It is completed by $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_3}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1,\,-1)^{\top}/\sqrt3}$, the normalized normal vector from Section 4.4. In the full decomposition, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\sqrt3&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)}$. The null space consists only of the zero vector, because $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{r}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{n}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$.

⁠In short: A matrix is the sum of $r$ building blocks $d_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}$ of rank $1$; the $\mathbf{u}_j$ with $d_j>0$ span the column space, and the $\mathbf{v}_j$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ span the null space.

Symmetric matrices#

For a symmetric matrix there is already a decomposition with an orthonormal basis, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}$. How is it related to the singular value decomposition?

Theorem 9.6 (Singular values of symmetric matrices)

The singular values of a symmetric matrix are the magnitudes $|\lambda_i|$ of its eigenvalues. If $\mathbf{q}_i$ is a normalized eigenvector for $\lambda_i\ne0$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_i}$ is a right singular vector and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_i)\,\mathbf{q}_i}$ the corresponding left singular vector.

Proof: Symmetric matrices

We have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^2\mathbf{Q}^{\top}}$, so $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ has the eigenvectors $\mathbf{q}_i$ with the eigenvalues $\lambda_i^2$, and the singular values are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\sqrt{\lambda_i^2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|\lambda_i|}$. By (9.2), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{q}_i/|\lambda_i|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\lambda_i/|\lambda_i|)\,\mathbf{q}_i}$.

If $\mathbf{A}$ is positive semidefinite, the spectral decomposition and the singular value decomposition are the same decomposition, with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{U}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{V}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{Q}}$. For negative eigenvalues, the sign moves into $\mathbf{U}$. For non-symmetric matrices, eigenvalues and singular values generally have nothing to do with each other, as $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ with the eigenvalues $2,2$ and the singular values $4,1$ shows. There is always one connection, though: every eigenvalue is at most $d_1$ in magnitude; this follows from the next subsection.

Worked example

$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\3&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ has the eigenvalues $4$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$ and $-2$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{q}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$. The singular values are $4$ and $2$. With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_2}$ we get $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(-2,\,2)^{\top}/\sqrt2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\cdot(-1,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{-\mathbf{q}_2}$.

⁠In short: For symmetric matrices, the singular values are the magnitudes of the eigenvalues, and for positive semidefinite ones the singular value decomposition is the spectral decomposition.

The spectral norm#

By how much can a matrix lengthen a vector at most? For vectors, the norm measures size. For a matrix, a natural measure of size is its largest lengthening factor.

Definition 9.7 (Spectral norm)

The spectral norm of a matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\max_{\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert} .}$$

Theorem 9.8 (Largest and smallest stretch)

For every $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with the singular values $d_1\ge\dots\ge d_n$ and every $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$,

$$d_n\,\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert\le d_1\,\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert .$$
(9.5)

The bounds are attained at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}_1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}_n}$. In particular, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1}$.

Proof: Largest and smallest stretch

With $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{c}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}$, we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{c}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_jd_j^2c_j^2}$. This lies between $d_n^2\sum_jc_j^2$ and $d_1^2\sum_jc_j^2$. It is the Rayleigh quotient (8.2) of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, multiplied by $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$.

Two consequences. First, $\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert$ and therefore $\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2\lVert\mathbf{B}\rVert_2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert$, so $\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\rVert_2\le\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2\lVert\mathbf{B}\rVert_2$: when two maps are applied one after the other, their largest stretch factors at most multiply. Second, every eigenvector satisfies $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|\lambda|\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}$, so $|\lambda|\le d_1$ for every eigenvalue of a square matrix. For a complex eigenvector $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{a}+\mathrm j\mathbf{b}}$ with real $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}$ the same holds if you set $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\mathbf{v}^*)^{\top}\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2}$, because by (9.5), $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert^2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{a}\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}\rVert^2}$ lies between $d_n^2\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2$ and $d_1^2\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2$; for square $\mathbf{A}$, therefore, $|\lambda|\ge d_n$ as well.

Worked example

For $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$, we have $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{4}$. The unit vectors are stretched less: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_1\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert(2,\,0)^{\top}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_2\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert(3,\,2)^{\top}\rVert}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{13}}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3.61}$. The factor $4$ is reached only at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,2)^{\top}/\sqrt5}$, the smallest factor $1$ at $\mathbf{v}_2$. The eigenvalue $2$ lies between $1$ and $4$.

⁠In short: The spectral norm $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1}$ is the largest factor by which $\mathbf{A}$ lengthens a vector; the smallest is $d_n$.

BackgroundTwo handles, one lever: separate controls from the singular value decomposition

On a two-handle faucet, with one handle for hot and one for cold water, each handle changes the flow and the temperature at the same time. In a simplified model for small turns around a setting with equal amounts of hot and cold water, the turns $\Delta w$ of the hot handle and $\Delta k$ of the cold handle change the flow and the temperature by

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{y}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\Delta Q\\\Delta T\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}\Delta w\\\Delta k\end{pmatrix},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{K}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix},}$$

in suitable units. The matrix $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{K}^{\top}\mathbf{K}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}5&-3\\-3&5\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ has the eigenvalues $8$ and $2$ with the eigenvectors $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,-1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(1,\,1)^{\top}/\sqrt2}$. So $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt8}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{2\sqrt2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt2}$ are the singular values, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1\,(0,\,1)^{\top}}$, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_2\,(1,\,0)^{\top}}$. In words: turning both handles in opposite directions changes only the temperature, turning both in the same direction changes only the flow.

With the decomposition $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{K}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$, you no longer set the handles but the coordinates $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}$ in the directions $\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2$, and you read off the result in the coordinates $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}$. Then

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{D}\tilde{\mathbf{x}} .}$$

The two coupled handles have turned into two separate controls: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\tilde y_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\tilde x_j}$. A single-lever mixer tap puts this idea into practice. If you lift the lever, both supplies open together, and more water comes out at the same temperature; if you swing it, one supply opens and the other closes, and only the temperature changes. The number of separate controls is the rank of $\mathbf{K}$, and its singular value tells how strongly each one acts. This is rotation, stretch, rotation in everyday life: the lever rotates the inputs into the directions of the singular vectors, the water stretches each one by its singular value, and you read off the result again as flow and temperature.

Exercise 27 ★★ A decomposition by hand

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$.

  1. Compute $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, its eigenvalues and the singular values of $\mathbf{A}$.

  2. Determine $\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2$ and from them $\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2$ with (9.2). Check $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$.

  3. Compare the singular values with the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$. What agrees, and what does not?

Solution

(a) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}9+16&20\\20&25\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ with trace $50$ and determinant $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{625-400}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{225}$, so $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lambda^2-50\lambda+225}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(\lambda-45)(\lambda-5)}$. $$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1}{d_1}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(3,\,9)^{\top}}{\sqrt2\cdot3\sqrt5}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(1,\,3)^{\top}}{\sqrt{10}},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_2}{d_2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(-3,\,1)^{\top}}{\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt5}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(-3,\,1)^{\top}}{\sqrt{10}} .}$$

Check: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac1{\sqrt{10}}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}3\sqrt5&-3\sqrt5\\9\sqrt5&\sqrt5\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}3&-3\\9&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}$ and

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}3&-3\\9&1\end{pmatrix}\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac12\begin{pmatrix}6&0\\8&10\end{pmatrix}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{pmatrix}.}$$

(c) The eigenvalues of the triangular matrix are $3$ and $5$. The products agree: $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{3\cdot5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{15}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{3\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt5}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{|\det\mathbf{A}|}$. The sums do not: $8$ versus $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{4\sqrt5}\textcolor{#15633a}{\approx}\textcolor{#c2410c}{8.94}$. In magnitude, the eigenvalues lie between $d_2$ and $d_1$: $2.236\le3$ and $5\le6.708$.

Exercise 28 ★★ A rectangular matrix

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\Bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1\\1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr)}$.

  1. Determine the singular values and the singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2$.

  2. Add $\mathbf{u}_3$ and write down the full decomposition $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$.

Solution

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_1}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1}2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(2,\,2,\,0)^{\top}}{2\sqrt2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(1,\,1,\,0)^{\top}}{\sqrt2},}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_2}{\sqrt2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{(0,\,0,\,2)^{\top}}{\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{(0,\,0,\,1)^{\top} .}$$

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2&0&1/\sqrt2\\1/\sqrt2&0&-1/\sqrt2\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&\sqrt2\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.}$$

(c) By the theorem in Section 9.6, $\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2$ is an orthonormal basis of the column space and $\mathbf{u}_3$ one of the orthogonal complement. As a check: the columns $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1,\,1,\,1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt2\,\mathbf{u}_1+\mathbf{u}_2}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(1,\,1,\,-1)^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt2\,\mathbf{u}_1-\mathbf{u}_2}$ lie in the span of $\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2$, and both are perpendicular to $\mathbf{u}_3$.

Exercise 29 ★★★ Properties of singular values

  1. Show: for a square matrix, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\det\mathbf{A}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1d_2\cdots d_n}$.

  2. Show: $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ have the same positive singular values.

  3. Show: for $\mathbf{u}\ne\mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$, the outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top}$ has exactly one positive singular value, namely $\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert$.

  4. Show: if $\mathbf{Q}$ is orthogonal, then $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ have the same singular values.

Solution

(a) By Chapter 1, Section 14.5, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\det\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\det\mathbf{U}\cdot\det\mathbf{D}\cdot\det\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$. The two orthogonal matrices have the determinant $\pm1$, and $\mathbf{D}$ is a diagonal matrix with the determinant $d_1\cdots d_n$. Hence $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{|\det\mathbf{A}|}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1\cdots d_n}$.

(b) From $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$ it follows that $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\top}}$. The diagonal matrices $\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^{\top}$ have the same entries $d_j^2>0$ on the diagonal, only different numbers of zeros. The singular values of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}$, so again the $d_j$, supplemented by zeros.

(c) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top})^{\top}(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}\,\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{v}^{\top}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2\,\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}}$. This matrix maps $\mathbf{v}$ to $\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2\,\mathbf{v}$ and every vector perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$ to zero. Its eigenvalues are therefore $\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2$ and otherwise $0$, and the only positive singular value is $\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert$.

(d) $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A})^{\top}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A})}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}}$. Both matrices have the same Gram matrix and hence the same singular values. Intuitively: a rotation or reflection after $\mathbf{A}$ does not change the shape of the ellipse, only its position.

Singular values
Definition 9.1 (Singular values)

Let $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, and let $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots\ge\lambda_n\ge0$ be the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, sorted in descending order, with multiple ones counted according to their multiplicity. The numbers

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sqrt{\lambda_j},}$$
$$d_1\ge d_2\ge\dots\ge d_n\ge0 ,$$
(9.1)

are called the singular values of $\mathbf{A}$.

Theorem 9.2 (Singular values and rank)

The number of singular values that are not zero is the rank of $\mathbf{A}$.

Singular vectors
Definition 9.3 (Singular vectors)

Orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$ of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ for the eigenvalues $d_1^2,\dots,d_n^2$ are called right singular vectors of $\mathbf{A}$. For $d_j>0$,

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}{d_j},}$$
$$\text{so}$$
$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\mathbf{u}_j ,}$$
(9.2)

is called the corresponding left singular vector.

The decomposition
Theorem 9.4 (Singular value decomposition)

Every matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$ can be written as

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$$
(9.3)

with orthogonal matrices $\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ and a matrix $\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ that carries the singular values $d_1\ge\dots\ge d_r>0$ on its diagonal and only zeros elsewhere. The columns of $\mathbf{V}$ are right singular vectors, and the first $r$ columns of $\mathbf{U}$ are the corresponding left ones. An equivalent form is the compact form

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\sum_{j=1}^rd_j\,\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top} .}$$
(9.4)
Building blocks of rank 1 and the spaces of a matrix
Theorem 9.5 (What the singular vectors span)

Let $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}$ with rank $r$. Then:

  1. $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r$ is an orthonormal basis of the column space of $\mathbf{A}$, and $\mathbf{u}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_m$ is an orthonormal basis of its orthogonal complement;

  2. $\mathbf{v}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$ is an orthonormal basis of the null space of $\mathbf{A}$, and $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_r$ is an orthonormal basis of the column space of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$.

Symmetric matrices
Theorem 9.6 (Singular values of symmetric matrices)

The singular values of a symmetric matrix are the magnitudes $|\lambda_i|$ of its eigenvalues. If $\mathbf{q}_i$ is a normalized eigenvector for $\lambda_i\ne0$, then $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{v}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{q}_i}$ is a right singular vector and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_i)\,\mathbf{q}_i}$ the corresponding left singular vector.

The spectral norm
Definition 9.7 (Spectral norm)

The spectral norm of a matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is

$$\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\max_{\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert} .}$$

Theorem 9.8 (Largest and smallest stretch)

For every $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with the singular values $d_1\ge\dots\ge d_n$ and every $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$,

$$d_n\,\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert\le d_1\,\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert .$$
(9.5)

The bounds are attained at $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}_1}$ and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{x}}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{v}_n}$. In particular, $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_1}$.

Key relationships
  • On “Perpendicular directions that stay perpendicular”: The eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ are perpendicular directions whose images under $\mathbf{A}$ are again perpendicular to each other; the images have the lengths $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.
  • On “Singular values”: The singular values $d_j$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$; they measure how strongly $\mathbf{A}$ stretches in its principal directions, and the number of positive ones is the rank.
  • On “Singular vectors”: The right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_j$ are eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, the left ones are $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{u}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j/d_j}$; both families are orthonormal, and $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{d_j\mathbf{u}_j}$.
  • On “The decomposition”: Every matrix is $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ with two orthogonal matrices and the singular values on the diagonal of $\mathbf{D}$, or, equivalently, a sum $\sum_jd_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}$.
  • On “Rotation, stretch, rotation”: Every matrix rotates, stretches along the axes and rotates again, up to reflections; the unit circle becomes an ellipse with the semi-axes $d_j\mathbf{u}_j$.

  • On “Building blocks of rank 1 and the spaces of a matrix”: A matrix is the sum of $r$ building blocks $d_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^{\top}$ of rank $1$; the $\mathbf{u}_j$ with $d_j>0$ span the column space, and the $\mathbf{v}_j$ with $\textcolor{#1b7f4a}{d_j}\textcolor{#15633a}{=}\textcolor{#c2410c}{0}$ span the null space.
